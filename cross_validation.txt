CROSS-VALIDATION: SCHEMES, PITFALLS, NESTED CV, AND INTERVIEW Q&A
================================================================================

ML_Lifecycle/theory/04_data_splitting_validation.txt covers this as a process
step in the lifecycle. This note is the concept-and-pitfalls version: what the
CV estimate actually estimates, how each scheme differs, and the ways it lies.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT WE ARE TRYING TO ESTIMATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Training error is not an estimate of anything useful: the model chose its
parameters to make it small. We want the expected error on data drawn from the
same distribution but not seen during fitting:

    Generalisation error = E_(x,y)[ loss(y, f_hat_D(x)) ]

Two subtly different targets, and the distinction resolves several arguments:

    Conditional error   the error of THIS fitted model, trained on THIS D.
                        What you care about when you are about to ship it.
    Expected error      the error of the (algorithm, n) pair, averaged over
                        training sets. What k-fold CV actually estimates,
                        because each fold refits on a different subset.

So k-fold CV evaluates a PROCEDURE, not a model. That is why "which of the k
models do I deploy?" is a category error -- you refit on all the data at the end
and rely on CV to have told you that the procedure generalises.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE SPLITS AND WHAT EACH IS FOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Train        fit parameters.
    Validation   choose hyperparameters, features, model family, threshold.
    Test         estimate error ONCE, after all choices are frozen.

Every time you look at the test set and change something, it becomes a
validation set and its estimate becomes optimistic. Treat it as write-once.

Single hold-out, the problems with it:
    1. High variance. With 20% held out of 1000 rows, the estimate rests on 200
       points; a couple of unlucky rows move the number by percent.
    2. Wastes data. The model you evaluate was trained on 80% of what the
       shipped model will see, so the estimate is pessimistic for small n.
    3. One arbitrary partition. Different seeds give different winners in a
       model comparison, which is how people fool themselves.

k-fold CV fixes 1 and 2 by making every point a test point exactly once.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. K-FOLD MECHANICS AND THE CHOICE OF K
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Partition the data into k roughly equal folds. For each fold j: train on the
other k-1 folds, predict fold j. Every point gets exactly one out-of-fold
prediction. Report the mean of the fold scores AND their standard deviation --
the spread is information, not noise to be hidden.

    CV_k = (1/k) * sum_j score(fold j)

Bias-variance of the ESTIMATOR (not of the model -- keep these separate):

    k small (e.g. 2)   each model trains on little data, so it is worse than the
                       final model  ->  the error estimate is PESSIMISTIC
                       (upward bias). Low variance, cheap.
    k large (e.g. n)   each model trains on nearly all the data  ->  low bias.
                       But the k training sets are almost identical, so the k
                       scores are highly correlated and averaging them does not
                       reduce variance much. Expensive.

    k = 5 or 10 is the standard compromise, empirically well supported.

LOOCV specifics:
    - Nearly unbiased for the expected error.
    - High variance despite averaging n numbers, for the correlation reason
      above; also its models differ by one point, so it cannot see instability
      that comes from resampling.
    - For linear models it is nearly free via the hat matrix (the PRESS
      statistic / generalised cross-validation shortcut), which is why it is
      standard for ridge but not for gradient boosting.
    - For classification it is awkward: each test fold has one label, so
      per-fold accuracy is 0 or 1, and stratification is impossible.

Repeated k-fold: run the whole thing with several different random partitions
and average. Reduces the variance due to the arbitrary partition, at linear
cost. Cheap and underused for small datasets.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. SCHEME SELECTION -- MATCH THE SPLIT TO THE DEPENDENCE STRUCTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The default KFold assumes i.i.d. rows. Almost no real dataset satisfies that.
Ask: "what does the model need to generalise ACROSS at deployment time?" and
split along that axis.

4.1 Stratified k-fold
    Preserve the class proportions in every fold. Mandatory for imbalanced
    classification -- with 1% positives and 10 folds on 500 rows, plain KFold
    can produce a fold with zero positives, making the fold score undefined or
    meaningless. Also worth doing for regression by binning the target, when the
    target is skewed. Cost: nothing. Just use it.

4.2 Group / leave-one-group-out
    When rows share a source that will not be shared at prediction time:
    multiple visits per patient, multiple sessions per user, several photos of
    the same object, repeated measurements per device. Random splitting puts the
    same patient in train and test, the model memorises the patient, and the
    score is inflated -- the model has learned identity, not the disease.
    Rule: if the deployment question is "a NEW patient walks in", the split must
    be by patient. See data_leakage.txt section on group leakage.

4.3 Time-series CV
    Random folds train on the future and predict the past, which is both
    impossible in deployment and wildly optimistic when there is any trend or
    autocorrelation. Use forward-chaining:

        Expanding window (grows the train set):
            train [1..100]        test [101..120]
            train [1..120]        test [121..140]
            train [1..140]        test [141..160]

        Rolling window (fixed train size, for non-stationary data):
            train [  1..100]      test [101..120]
            train [ 21..120]      test [121..140]

    Refinements that matter in practice:
      - GAP / embargo. If the label at time t is computed from a window
        [t, t+h] (e.g. 30-day churn), then a training row at t-1 partially
        overlaps a test row at t. Insert a gap of at least h between train and
        test. In finance this is "purging and embargoing".
      - Match the horizon. If you predict 7 days ahead in production, test at a
        7-day-ahead horizon, not one step.
      - Retraining cadence. If prod retrains weekly, the backtest should
        retrain weekly too, otherwise you are measuring a model that will
        never exist.

4.4 Spatial / hierarchical / nested structure
    Same principle: block along the axis of correlation. Spatial data needs
    spatially blocked folds; store sales need store-level or region-level
    grouping if you are forecasting for new stores.

4.5 Choosing, in one table

    Deployment question                       Scheme
    ---------------------------------------   -------------------------------
    New i.i.d. rows, balanced                 KFold
    New i.i.d. rows, imbalanced               StratifiedKFold
    New user / patient / device               GroupKFold (by that entity)
    Future time periods                       TimeSeriesSplit + gap
    New entity AND future time                grouped time-based split
    Very small n                              Repeated stratified k-fold / LOOCV

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE GOLDEN RULE: EVERYTHING FITTED GOES INSIDE THE FOLD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Any step that LEARNS from data is part of the model and must be refit on each
training fold only:

    scaling and normalisation      (mean/std learned from data)
    imputation                     (mean/median/model learned from data)
    target / mean encoding         (per-category target statistics -- the worst
                                    offender, needs inner folds of its own)
    feature selection              (correlation filters, RFE, importance cuts,
                                    univariate tests)
    dimensionality reduction       (PCA components, embeddings)
    resampling for imbalance       (SMOTE must see only the training fold, and
                                    must never touch the validation fold)
    calibration                    (Platt/isotonic fitted on data the model did
                                    not train on -- see probability_calibration)
    outlier removal                (a threshold learned from the full data)

Scaling before splitting is the classic. The leak is small but real: the test
fold's mean and variance bleed into the training transformation. With SMOTE
before splitting, the leak is enormous -- synthetic points interpolated from
training rows land in the validation fold, so the model is scored on near-copies
of what it trained on, and AUC can go from 0.75 to 0.95 for free.

Practical enforcement: put every step in a Pipeline and cross-validate the
pipeline, not the estimator. Then correctness is structural rather than a thing
you have to remember.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. NESTED CV (and the selection bias that makes it necessary)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The problem. You try 200 hyperparameter configurations, take the best CV score,
and report it. That number is biased optimistically, and the mechanism is the
maximum of noisy estimates: each configuration's CV score is the truth plus
noise, and argmax over 200 of them selects partly for a genuinely good
configuration and partly for a lucky one. The winner's score therefore contains
the luck. The bias grows with the number of configurations and shrinks with the
size of the validation data.

The fix. Two loops:

    outer loop (k_out folds)   -> estimates generalisation error
        inner loop (k_in folds on the outer training part) -> selects hyperparams
        refit on the whole outer training part with the selected hyperparams
        score once on the outer test fold

    Nested CV score = mean of the outer fold scores.

The inner loop may choose DIFFERENT hyperparameters in different outer folds.
That is not a bug, and it is the second thing people trip on: nested CV
evaluates the tuning PROCEDURE, it does not produce one model. Getting a final
model is a separate step -- run the inner selection once on all the data and
refit. If the chosen hyperparameters swing wildly across outer folds, that is a
useful warning that your selection is unstable and the hyperparameter barely
matters.

Cost: k_out * k_in * n_configs fits. When you cannot afford it, the honest
cheap alternative is a genuinely untouched hold-out test set used exactly once.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. USING CV FOR SELECTION: THE ONE-STANDARD-ERROR RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fold scores give you a mean and a standard error, se = std / sqrt(k). When two
configurations are within one standard error of each other, the difference is
noise. The one-standard-error rule: among all configurations whose score is
within one se of the best, pick the SIMPLEST (largest lambda, shallowest tree,
fewest features).

Rationale: the CV curve is flat near its optimum, so you give up almost nothing
in estimated error and buy real robustness, plus a model that is less likely to
have been selected by luck. This is the standard practice for choosing lambda in
ridge and lasso.

Also: because the fold scores are computed on overlapping training sets, they
are not independent, so se underestimates the true uncertainty and a paired
t-test across folds is anti-conservative. Use it as a rough guide, not a p-value.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. WHAT CROSS-VALIDATION CANNOT DO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    1. It cannot detect distribution shift. CV resamples the data you have, so
       it estimates error under the TRAINING distribution. If production drifts,
       CV was answering a different question. That is monitoring's job
       (ML_Lifecycle/theory/12_monitoring_and_drift.txt).
    2. It cannot fix leakage, and it will faithfully report the inflated score
       that leakage produces. A suspiciously good CV number is evidence of a
       leak, not of a good model.
    3. It cannot compensate for a wrong split axis. Random folds on grouped or
       temporal data give a confidently wrong number.
    4. It is unstable when n is small -- the very regime where you most want it.
       Use repeated CV and report the spread.
    5. It says nothing about feedback effects: a recommender that changes user
       behaviour cannot be evaluated offline alone, which is why online A/B
       tests exist.
    6. It measures average error, not the tail, the per-segment error, or
       fairness across groups. Slice your out-of-fold predictions
       (ML_Lifecycle/theory/10_error_analysis_and_fairness.txt).

Bonus use: the out-of-fold predictions themselves are valuable. They are the
input to stacking, the right data for choosing a threshold, and the right data
for fitting a calibrator.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. THE THIRTY-SECOND ANSWER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cross-validation estimates how a training procedure generalises by refitting on
several partitions so that every point is used for validation exactly once,
which removes the variance and the wastefulness of a single hold-out. k trades
off the bias and variance of the ESTIMATE -- small k means each model sees less
data and the estimate is pessimistic, k equal to n is nearly unbiased but its
folds are so correlated that averaging barely helps -- so 5 or 10 is standard.
The split must be chosen to match the dependence structure and the deployment
question: stratified for imbalance, grouped when rows share a user or patient,
forward-chaining with a gap for time series. Everything that learns from data --
scaling, imputation, target encoding, feature selection, SMOTE -- must be refit
inside each training fold, which is what pipelines are for. And if you tune
hyperparameters on the CV score, that score is optimistically biased by
selection, so you need nested CV or a genuinely untouched test set to report an
honest number.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. INTERVIEW Q&A
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Why is 5 or 10 folds standard rather than n?
A: k controls the bias-variance trade-off of the error estimate. Small k trains
each model on much less data than the final model will get, so the estimate is
pessimistically biased. LOOCV has almost no such bias, but its n training sets
differ by a single point, so the n scores are highly correlated and averaging
them removes much less variance than the count suggests -- and it costs n fits.
Five or ten sits near the empirical sweet spot. The exception is linear models
with a closed-form LOOCV shortcut, where the cost argument disappears.

Q: You get 0.94 AUC in CV and 0.71 in production. Walk me through it.
A: I would rank the hypotheses by prior probability. First, leakage -- a feature
that is unavailable or computed differently at serving time, or preprocessing
fitted before the split; I would check feature importance for a suspiciously
dominant feature and verify point-in-time availability for the top features.
Second, the wrong split axis -- if rows are grouped by user or ordered in time
and I used random folds, CV was answering an easier question than production
asks. Third, genuine distribution shift between the training window and now.
Fourth, selection bias if I tuned heavily against that CV score. Fifth, a
train/serve skew bug in the feature pipeline. The diagnostic that separates
leakage from shift is a temporal backtest: if training on old data and testing
on the most recent period reproduces the 0.71, it is shift or a bad split; if it
still shows 0.94, it is leakage or serving skew.

Q: Can you use k-fold CV on time-series data?
A: Not the standard random version. Random folds train on future observations to
predict past ones, which is impossible in deployment, and with autocorrelation
the neighbouring points in the training folds are near-duplicates of the test
points, so the estimate is badly optimistic. Use forward chaining with an
expanding or rolling window, insert a gap at least as long as the label horizon
so overlapping label windows do not straddle the boundary, and evaluate at the
same forecast horizon and retraining cadence you will use in production.
Follow-up: when would a rolling window beat an expanding one? When the process
is non-stationary and old data actively misleads -- then a fixed recent window
is a feature, not a limitation.

Q: What is nested cross-validation and when do you actually need it?
A: An inner CV loop selects hyperparameters and an outer loop estimates error,
so the data used to select is never the data used to report. You need it when
the selection is aggressive relative to the data -- many configurations, small
dataset -- because taking the max over many noisy CV scores captures the luck as
well as the quality, biasing the reported number upward. With a large dataset a
single untouched hold-out is simpler and just as honest. And nested CV does not
hand you a model: it evaluates the procedure, so the final model comes from
running the selection once on all the data and refitting.

Q: You SMOTE your minority class and CV AUC jumps from 0.75 to 0.95. Are you
happy?
A: No, that is the signature of resampling before splitting. SMOTE creates
synthetic minority points by interpolating between real ones, so if it runs
before the fold split, the validation fold contains points interpolated from
training rows -- near-duplicates of training data. The model is being scored on
what it memorised. The fix is to resample inside the training fold only, and
never to resample the validation fold, which must keep the true class balance
because that is the balance production has. Done correctly, resampling usually
gives a modest change, and threshold tuning on a calibrated model often beats it
outright.

Q: Your dataset has multiple rows per customer. How do you split?
A: By customer, using grouped folds, provided the deployment question is
"predict for a customer we have not seen". With random row-level splits the same
customer appears in train and test, so the model can memorise customer-specific
quirks and the score reflects recall of identity rather than generalisation.
If instead production always has history for the customer and I am predicting
their next event, then row-level splitting is not automatically wrong -- but the
split must still respect time, so the right scheme is grouped and time-ordered.
The rule is to split along whatever axis the model must generalise across.

Q: Should you report the mean CV score or something else?
A: Mean and standard deviation, always both. The spread tells you whether a
difference between models is real and whether the procedure is stable -- a model
scoring 0.80 plus or minus 0.01 is a different proposition from 0.80 plus or
minus 0.09, and only the first supports a confident claim. I would then apply
the one-standard-error rule for selection, preferring the simplest model within
one standard error of the best, and I would look at the worst fold to understand
what makes it hard. Note the fold scores are not independent because the
training sets overlap, so the standard error understates the real uncertainty
and I would not turn it into a p-value.

Q: Is there any leakage risk in cross-validation itself, even with pipelines?
A: Yes, two kinds. First, repeated use: every hyperparameter decision, feature
choice and threshold tuned against the same CV partition leaks information
gradually, which is the selection bias nested CV addresses. Second, duplicate or
near-duplicate rows -- pipelines cannot help you if the same record appears
twice and lands in different folds, and this is common after joins or in scraped
data. So deduplicate before splitting, and split on the entity, not the row.
