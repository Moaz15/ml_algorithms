1. CORE MECHANICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: minimize J(theta) iteratively when there is no closed form, or when d and
m are too large for one.

  theta := theta - alpha * grad_J(theta)

  theta          weights and biases
  alpha          learning rate (step size)
  grad_J(theta)  vector of partial derivatives of J w.r.t. each parameter

Why the negative gradient works (first-order Taylor):
  J(theta - alpha*g) ~ J(theta) - alpha*||g||^2
The correction term is strictly negative, so the loss decreases for a
sufficiently small alpha. The negative gradient is the direction of steepest
local descent - not the fastest route to the minimum (see section 4).

Points where grad = 0:
  - Minimum: all Hessian eigenvalues positive.
  - Saddle point: mixed-sign eigenvalues; dominant in high dimensions.
  - Plateau: gradient near zero over a wide region.
Plain GD stalls at all three; momentum and adaptive methods do not (sec 5-6).

The gradient itself comes from backpropagation (reverse-mode autodiff) in deep
nets, or analytically for linear and logistic regression.


2. BATCH vs STOCHASTIC vs MINI-BATCH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Property           Batch GD          SGD               Mini-Batch GD
  -----------------  ----------------  ----------------  ------------------
  Samples/update     all m             1                 B (32-512)
  Updates/epoch      1                 m                 m / B
  Gradient           exact             very noisy        low-noise estimate
  Path               smooth            zig-zag           mildly noisy
  Memory             scales with m     minimal           set by B
  Hardware fit       poor              no parallelism    GPU-optimal
  Escapes saddles    no                yes (noise)       yes
  Typical use        small data        online/streaming  the default

- Mini-batch is the default. Choose B for hardware (powers of 2, fill the GPU)
  and for noise level, not by theory.
- Batch GD's better per-iteration rate is misleading: it pays O(m) per step
  while SGD pays O(1), so SGD reaches a useful loss far sooner on large data.
- Shuffle every epoch. On class-sorted data, consecutive batches pull weights in
  contradictory directions and the loss oscillates cyclically. Reshuffling each
  epoch (no replacement) also converges faster than sampling with replacement.


3. LEARNING RATE: STABILITY AND CONVERGENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
alpha is the single most important hyperparameter.

  Too high  overshoots the minimum; loss oscillates or diverges to NaN.
  Too low   crawls, wastes compute, can stall on a plateau.

Formal limit: for an L-smooth loss (L = largest Hessian eigenvalue) GD is stable
only for alpha < 2/L, and alpha = 1/L is the fastest fixed step. So the usable
alpha depends on the curvature of your data - there is no universal value. In
practice, find it with an LR range test (section 7) rather than by estimating L.

What convergence actually guarantees:
  - Convex + smooth: converges to the global minimum, error ~ O(1/t).
  - Strongly convex: linear convergence; iterations scale with the condition
    number kappa (section 4).
  - Non-convex (any deep net): only a stationary point is guaranteed - no global
    optimum. In practice we optimize until validation stops improving.

Learning-rate decay is not optional for exact convergence: SGD converges to the
minimum only if sum(alpha_t) = infinity and sum(alpha_t^2) < infinity (e.g.
alpha_t = alpha_0 / t). With a constant alpha, SGD ends up bouncing inside a
noise ball around the minimum whose radius grows with alpha and with the
gradient variance sigma^2/B. That noise ball is the flat noisy plateau in a loss
curve, and decaying alpha late in training is what produces the characteristic
sudden drop.


4. CONDITIONING AND FEATURE SCALING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Unscaled or correlated features turn the loss contours into long thin ellipses.
One alpha serves every coordinate, so it is capped by the steepest direction
while the flat direction crawls: the zig-zag. Cost scales with the condition
number kappa = L/mu (largest over smallest curvature).

For linear regression the Hessian is X^T X, so
  kappa(X^T X) = (sigma_max(X) / sigma_min(X))^2
The squaring makes a mildly skewed design matrix a badly conditioned
optimization problem; collinear features push sigma_min -> 0 and kappa -> huge.
This is the optimization face of multicollinearity.

Fixes, cheapest first:
  - Standardize or min-max scale. Fit the scaler on train only.
  - Mean-center: removes coupling between the bias term and the weights.
  - PCA / whitening: decorrelates as well as rescales.
  - Normalization layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm): re-condition the problem at
    every layer, mid-training. Much of their benefit is optimization, not
    regularization.
  - L2: adds lambda to every Hessian eigenvalue, so
    kappa becomes (L+lambda)/(mu+lambda) - it speeds up training too.
  - Adaptive optimizers (section 6) approximate per-coordinate rescaling;
    second-order methods (section 10) are scale-invariant by construction.

Interview point: the linear-regression solution is itself scale-invariant (the
normal equation just gets numerically worse conditioned). The sensitivity
belongs to the iterative solver, not the model. Trees split on thresholds, so
they are unaffected by feature scale.


5. MOMENTUM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  v := beta * v + grad_J(theta)          beta = 0.9 typical
  theta := theta - alpha * v

- For a constant gradient the velocity saturates at grad/(1-beta), so momentum
  multiplies the effective step by 1/(1-beta): 10x at beta=0.9, 100x at 0.99.
  alpha and beta must therefore be tuned together.
- Fixes three things at once: cancels the oscillating component of the zig-zag
  while accumulating the consistent one; carries velocity through plateaus and
  saddle points; averages out mini-batch noise.
- Nesterov: evaluate the gradient at the look-ahead point theta - alpha*beta*v,
  so the step brakes before overshooting instead of after. Small but essentially
  free gain; SGD + Nesterov(0.9) is the standard CNN recipe.
- Framework trap: PyTorch uses v := beta*v + grad (inflates the effective LR),
  Keras uses v := beta*v + (1-beta)*grad (does not). The same beta behaves
  differently across libraries.


6. ADAPTIVE OPTIMIZERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One global alpha is wrong when coordinates differ in curvature or in update
frequency (sparse embeddings vs dense layers). Adaptive methods keep
per-parameter statistics and rescale each coordinate.

AdaGrad
  G := G + grad^2                            (running sum)
  theta := theta - alpha * grad / (sqrt(G) + eps)
  Rare features keep large effective steps - strong for sparse linear models
  (CTR, bag-of-words). G only grows, so the effective LR decays monotonically to
  zero and long deep-learning runs stall.

RMSProp
  E := rho * E + (1 - rho) * grad^2          rho = 0.9 (EMA, not a sum)
  Fixes AdaGrad's stalling by forgetting old gradients.

Adam = RMSProp + momentum + bias correction
  m := b1*m + (1-b1)*g                       first moment  (direction)
  v := b2*v + (1-b2)*g^2                     second moment (per-param scale)
  m_hat = m / (1 - b1^t) ;  v_hat = v / (1 - b2^t)
  theta := theta - alpha * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)

  Defaults: b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8, alpha=1e-3 (3e-4 to 1e-4 for
  Transformers). b2=0.999 implies a ~1000-step averaging window.
  Bias correction is needed because m and v start at zero and warm up at very
  different speeds; without it the early updates are badly mis-scaled.
  The update size is roughly capped at alpha, which makes Adam robust to badly
  scaled gradients - the reason it dominates NLP.

AdamW - the current default
  theta := theta - alpha * m_hat/(sqrt(v_hat)+eps)        (data gradient only)
  theta := theta - alpha * wd * theta                     (decoupled decay)
  L2 added inside the Adam loss is divided by sqrt(v_hat), so heavily-updated
  weights receive less decay. Decoupling makes decay uniform; decay values are
  not transferable between Adam and AdamW.

Also worth naming: LARS/LAMB apply a per-layer trust ratio
||theta||/||update|| and are what makes very large batch training work;
Adafactor and 8-bit Adam shrink optimizer state for large models.

Optimizer state and memory:

  Optimizer         Extra state   Handles bad scaling   Note
  ----------------  ------------  --------------------  ---------------------
  SGD               none          no                    needs scaled features
  SGD + momentum    1x params     partly                best CNN accuracy
  AdaGrad           1x params     yes (sparse)          LR decays to zero
  RMSProp           1x params     yes                   no momentum term
  Adam / AdamW      2x params     yes                   robust default
  LARS / LAMB       2x params     yes                   huge-batch training
  L-BFGS            m x params    yes (curvature)       full-batch only

Mixed-precision training costs about 16 bytes per parameter with Adam:
2 (fp16 weight) + 2 (fp16 grad) + 4 (fp32 master weight) + 4 (m) + 4 (v).
A 7B model therefore needs ~112 GB of training state, which is why ZeRO/FSDP
sharding exists (section 12).

Adam vs SGD in one line: Adam trains faster and tolerates bad gradient scaling;
SGD + momentum often generalizes slightly better on CNNs. Transformers
effectively require AdamW.


7. LEARNING RATE SCHEDULES AND TUNING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Principle: large steps early to travel, small steps late to settle inside the
noise ball.

  Step decay            x0.1 at fixed milestones. Simple, still strong.
  Cosine annealing      smooth decay to ~0; default for vision and LLMs.
  Linear + warmup       standard LLM pretrain/finetune recipe.
  One-cycle             ramp up to a large peak then down; the large mid-run
                        LR acts as a regularizer.
  Inverse sqrt          original Transformer schedule; step-budget agnostic.
  ReduceLROnPlateau     reactive; use when the step budget is unknown.

Warmup (ramp alpha from ~0 over the first few hundred to few thousand steps) is
needed because Adam's v_hat is a high-variance estimate early on, and because
the surface at initialization is badly conditioned - one oversized early step is
unrecoverable. Mandatory for Transformers and for large-batch training.

LR range test: run a few hundred steps with alpha rising exponentially, plot
loss vs alpha, and pick roughly one order of magnitude below the blow-up point.
Cheap, and far better than guessing.

Coupling with batch size: alpha ~ B for SGD/momentum (linear scaling rule),
alpha ~ sqrt(B) for Adam-family. Both break down past the critical batch size,
where extra samples no longer improve gradient quality.

Tuning order (what to say when asked how you would tune this):
  1. Overfit a single mini-batch to ~zero loss - validates the whole pipeline.
  2. Find alpha with a range test; search on a log scale, factors of 3.
  3. Pick B for hardware utilization, then rescale alpha.
  4. Add a schedule, plus warmup if using Adam or large batches.
  5. Only then tune weight decay, momentum/b1, b2, eps.
Everything after alpha is second order.


8. GRADIENT PATHOLOGIES AND NUMERICAL STABILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Vanishing gradients: repeated multiplication by small Jacobians (saturated
sigmoid/tanh, depth, long sequences) shrinks the gradient toward zero, so early
layers stop learning. Fixes: ReLU-family activations, residual/skip connections,
normalization layers, He init (ReLU) or Xavier/Glorot init (tanh), LSTM/GRU
gating.

Exploding gradients: the same product blowing up; loss spikes then goes NaN.
Fixes: clipping, lower alpha, normalization, better init.

Gradient clipping: prefer global norm, g := g * min(1, c/||g||) with c ~ 1.0,
because it rescales the whole gradient and preserves the step direction. Value
clipping truncates only the large coordinates and distorts direction. Standard
for RNNs, Transformers and RL. If it fires on most steps, alpha is too high.

NaN / Inf causes, in order of likelihood:
  1. alpha too high -> weights explode -> Inf -> Inf - Inf = NaN.
  2. log(0) in cross-entropy -> compute the loss from logits (log-sum-exp).
  3. exp overflow in softmax/sigmoid -> subtract the max logit, clip inputs.
  4. division by ~0: zero-variance feature, empty cluster, missing eps.
  5. fp16 overflow (max 65504) without loss scaling.
Diagnose by logging the gradient norm each step and finding the last finite one.

Mixed precision: fp16 underflows small gradients, so dynamic loss scaling
multiplies the loss by S before backward and unscales the gradients before the
update; bf16 keeps fp32's exponent range and usually needs none. Keep an fp32
master copy of the weights so tiny updates are not lost to rounding.

Gradient checking (essential for from-scratch implementations):
  numeric_i = (J(theta + eps*e_i) - J(theta - eps*e_i)) / (2*eps)
  rel_err   = ||numeric - analytic|| / (||numeric|| + ||analytic||)
Use the two-sided form, eps ~ 1e-4 to 1e-7 in float64; rel_err < 1e-7 is
correct, > 1e-2 is a bug. Disable dropout first and check a random subset of
coordinates.

Dead ReLU: one large update pins a unit negative and its gradient stays exactly
zero forever. Lower alpha, LeakyReLU/GELU, sane init.


9. NON-DIFFERENTIABLE OBJECTIVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
|theta| has no derivative at 0 - the subgradient there is any value in [-1, 1].
Consequence: plain SGD on a Lasso objective produces tiny weights, not zeros,
because the constant alpha*lambda step makes weights jitter in a band around 0.

Proximal gradient step (ISTA) - the correct fix. Gradient step on the smooth
part, then soft-threshold:
  z := theta - alpha * grad_J_smooth(theta)
  theta := sign(z) * max(|z| - alpha*lambda, 0)
Everything inside the threshold band collapses to exactly zero, which is what
actually produces sparsity.

Coordinate descent: minimize one coordinate at a time; for Lasso each 1-D
subproblem is that same closed-form soft-threshold. This is what scikit-learn's
Lasso and ElasticNet use, and it has no learning rate to tune.

Other non-smooth losses needing the same care: hinge (SVM), MAE, quantile.
ReLU's subgradient at 0 is conventionally taken as 0 by every framework.


10. SECOND-ORDER METHODS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Newton: theta := theta - H^-1 * grad. Effectively a per-direction learning rate
of 1/eigenvalue, so it is scale-invariant and immune to the kappa problem, and
converges quadratically near the optimum. Costs O(d^2) memory and O(d^3) per
step, and the step moves uphill when H has negative eigenvalues.

  IRLS  Newton for logistic regression, H = X^T W X with W = diag(p(1-p)).
        What sklearn's lbfgs / newton-cg do: ~10 iterations, not thousands.
  L-BFGS  approximates H^-1 from the last m (5-20) gradient differences,
        O(m*d) memory. Best for small/medium, full-batch, deterministic convex
        problems; breaks on noisy mini-batch gradients.
  Gauss-Newton / Levenberg-Marquardt  H ~ J^T J for least squares; LM damps
        toward gradient descent when the quadratic model is poor. Curve fitting.

Why deep learning stays first-order: mini-batch curvature estimates are far too
noisy, O(d^2) is impossible at billions of parameters, negative curvature makes
Newton steps unsafe, and Adam plus normalization layers already recover most of
the conditioning benefit for far less compute.


11. BATCH SIZE, NOISE AND GENERALIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Gradient variance scales as sigma^2/B: doubling B cuts noise only by sqrt(2).
  Past the critical batch size, extra samples per step buy almost nothing.
- Large-batch generalization gap: fewer, smoother steps let the model settle in
  sharper minima. Mitigate with the linear scaling rule, warmup, and LARS/LAMB.
- Flat vs sharp minima: train and test surfaces are slightly shifted relative to
  each other, so a wide basin degrades less under that shift. SGD noise is the
  standard explanation for why small batches and large mid-run LRs generalize
  better. (Sharpness is reparameterization-dependent, so this is an empirical
  story, not a theorem.)
- Gradient accumulation: run k micro-batches, average their gradients, take one
  step - emulates batch k*B under a memory limit. Divide the loss by k, and note
  BatchNorm statistics are still per micro-batch (LayerNorm is unaffected).
- Weight averaging, cheap accuracy: EMA of weights
  (theta_ema := d*theta_ema + (1-d)*theta, d ~ 0.999) or SWA (average the last
  epochs of a cyclic schedule). Recompute BatchNorm statistics afterwards.


12. DISTRIBUTED TRAINING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Data parallelism: the model fits on one GPU. Split the mini-batch across
devices, compute local gradients, All-Reduce to average them, then update. The
default; scales until the global batch grows past what is useful (section 11).

Model parallelism, when the model does not fit:
  - Tensor parallel: slice weight matrices within a layer; needs NVLink-class
    interconnect because activations cross devices every layer.
  - Pipeline parallel: slice sequential layers across devices; 1F1B scheduling
    interleaves forward and backward passes to shrink idle "bubbles".

State sharding (ZeRO / FSDP): partition optimizer state, gradients and
parameters across ranks. This is the practical answer to the 16 bytes/param
problem in section 6.

Large-batch recipe: linear LR scaling + warmup + LARS/LAMB.


13. DIAGNOSTICS AND DEBUGGING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Log every step: training loss (per step, not per epoch - epoch averages hide
spikes), global gradient norm, update-to-weight ratio
||alpha*update||/||theta|| (healthy around 1e-3), and the current LR to confirm
the schedule fires. Log the validation metric per epoch.

Stopping criteria:
  - fixed epoch/iteration budget (what most scratch implementations use)
  - |J_t - J_{t-1}| < tol, or ||theta_t - theta_{t-1}|| < tol
  - ||grad|| < tol: theoretically right, useless with mini-batch noise
  - early stopping on validation with patience: the only criterion aimed at
    generalization rather than at optimization

  Symptom                          Likely cause
  -------------------------------  -------------------------------------------
  NaN after a few steps            alpha too high, or log(0)/overflow (sec 8)
  Loss oscillates, no downtrend    alpha too high, or batch too small
  Loss falls very slowly           alpha too low, or features unscaled (sec 4)
  Loss flat from step 0            broken gradient path, or dead units
  Loss drops then plateaus high    LR decayed too early, or underfitting
  Sudden drop at a schedule step   normal: leaving the noise ball (sec 3)
  Train loss down, val loss up     overfitting, not an optimization issue
  Spiky loss on Transformers       needs warmup, clipping, or lower b2

Debug in this order:
  1. Overfit a single mini-batch to ~zero loss. If that fails, the bug is in the
     model, loss, or data plumbing - never tune hyperparameters first.
  2. Check gradients numerically (section 8) on a tiny input.
  3. Verify input scaling and label encoding.
  4. Only then touch alpha, schedule, batch size, regularization.


14. WHEN NOT TO USE GRADIENT DESCENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Closed form exists and d is small: the normal equation
    theta = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y is exact at O(d^3). Prefer GD when d or m is large,
    when X^T X is singular, or when updates must be online.
  - A specialized solver exists: SMO/QP for kernel SVM, coordinate descent for
    Lasso, IRLS or L-BFGS for GLMs. They beat GD on their own problem class.
  - The objective is not differentiable in the parameters: trees choose splits
    greedily by impurity. Gradient boosting uses gradients w.r.t. predictions,
    not w.r.t. tree structure - a favourite interview distinction.
  - Parameters are discrete or combinatorial: feature subsets, k in KMeans,
    architecture choices.
  - Latent variables with closed-form steps: EM (KMeans is hard-assignment EM).
  - Hyperparameters: no usable gradient, so use random search, Bayesian
    optimization, or Hyperband.


15. OPTIMIZER SELECTION CHEAT-SHEET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Problem                         Recipe
  ------------------------------  --------------------------------------
  Linear / logistic regression    closed form or L-BFGS
  Lasso / ElasticNet              coordinate descent or proximal GD
  Kernel SVM                      SMO / QP solver
  Large sparse linear (CTR)       SGD or AdaGrad/FTRL, single pass
  CNN from scratch                SGD + Nesterov 0.9 + cosine + wd 5e-4
  Transformer pretrain/finetune   AdamW + warmup + cosine, clip 1.0
  Very large batch (>8k)          LARS/LAMB + linear LR scaling + warmup
  Embeddings / recommenders       Adam or AdaGrad (sparse-aware)
  Reinforcement learning          Adam, alpha ~ 1e-4, clip, lower b2
  Memory-constrained LLM          Adafactor / 8-bit Adam / ZeRO-FSDP
  Small full-batch convex         L-BFGS with line search

Default when picking blind: AdamW, alpha = 3e-4, warmup 5% of steps, cosine
decay, gradient clipping at 1.0 - then run an LR range test.


16. INTERVIEW Q&A
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Q1: What happens if the learning rate is too high or too low, and is there a
    formal limit?
A1: Too high overshoots the minimum, so the loss oscillates or diverges to NaN;
    too low crawls, wastes compute, and can stall on a plateau. Formally, for an
    L-smooth loss (L = largest Hessian eigenvalue) GD is stable only for
    alpha < 2/L, with alpha = 1/L optimal - so the usable range is a property of
    your data's curvature, not a universal constant. In practice you find it
    with an LR range test rather than by estimating L.

Q2: Why shuffle the dataset before creating mini-batches?
A2: Shuffling breaks any ordering in the data. If the file is sorted by label,
    consecutive batches contain a single class and pull the weights in
    contradictory directions, giving cyclic oscillation and poor convergence.
    Reshuffle every epoch; this also converges faster than sampling with
    replacement.

Q3: Local minimum vs saddle point - which actually blocks training?
A3: At a local minimum the gradient is zero and all Hessian eigenvalues are
    positive; at a saddle point the gradient is zero but the eigenvalues have
    mixed signs, so some directions still descend. In high dimensions saddle
    points and plateaus vastly outnumber bad local minima, and they are the real
    stalling risk because plain GD sees grad ~ 0 and stops moving. Momentum
    carries velocity through the flat region, Adam enlarges steps in
    low-curvature directions, and mini-batch noise kicks the iterate off the
    saddle.

Q4: Batch GD has the better convergence rate. Why is mini-batch the default?
A4: Rates are per iteration, and iterations cost very different amounts: batch
    GD pays O(m) per step and SGD pays O(1), so on large data SGD makes
    thousands of updates in the time batch GD makes one and reaches a useful
    loss much sooner. Mini-batches then add what the rates ignore: B in the
    32-512 range saturates GPU parallelism, keeps gradient variance moderate,
    and retains enough noise to escape saddle points. High-precision
    convergence, where batch methods genuinely win, is rarely what a learning
    problem needs.

Q5: Why is feature scaling critical for GD but irrelevant for decision trees?
A5: GD applies the same alpha to every coordinate, so a feature 1000x larger
    creates ~1000x larger curvature in its direction; the stable alpha is then
    dictated by the steepest direction while the flat one crawls - the zig-zag.
    Standardizing makes the contours near-circular so one alpha suits all
    coordinates. Trees split on thresholds (is x > t), which is invariant to any
    monotone rescaling. Note the linear-regression solution is itself
    scale-invariant: the sensitivity belongs to the solver, not the model.

Q6: How do L1 and L2 change the update step?
A6: L2 adds (lambda/2)*||theta||^2 whose gradient is lambda*theta, giving
    theta := theta*(1 - alpha*lambda) - alpha*grad_J - a multiplicative shrink
    proportional to weight size, which never reaches exactly zero. L1 adds
    lambda*||theta||_1 whose subgradient is lambda*sign(theta), giving
    theta := theta - alpha*grad_J - alpha*lambda*sign(theta) - a constant pull
    regardless of magnitude, which drives unimportant weights to zero and yields
    sparsity. L2 also adds lambda to every Hessian eigenvalue, so it improves
    conditioning and speeds up optimization as well as regularizing.

Q7: Plain SGD on a Lasso objective gives tiny weights, not exact zeros. Why?
A7: |theta| is not differentiable at 0 - its subgradient there is the whole
    interval [-1, 1] - so a fixed alpha*lambda step overshoots past zero and the
    weight jitters in a band of width ~alpha*lambda instead of landing on it.
    The fix is a proximal step: take the smooth gradient step, then
    soft-threshold with sign(z)*max(|z| - alpha*lambda, 0), which collapses
    everything inside the band to exactly zero. Coordinate descent solves that
    same 1-D problem in closed form per feature, which is why sklearn's Lasso
    uses it instead of GD.

Q8: Momentum vs Nesterov momentum?
A8: Momentum keeps a velocity v = beta*v + grad and steps along it, amplifying
    consistent directions and cancelling oscillating ones; because the effective
    step is multiplied by 1/(1-beta), alpha and beta must be tuned together.
    Nesterov takes the gradient at the look-ahead point theta - alpha*beta*v,
    so it brakes before overshooting rather than after. The practical gain is
    small but free; SGD + Nesterov(0.9) is the standard CNN recipe.

Q9: Why does Adam need bias correction?
A9: m and v are EMAs initialized at zero, so early estimates are biased toward
    zero - after one step m = 0.1*g. Dividing by (1 - b1^t) and (1 - b2^t) makes
    them unbiased. It matters because the two moments warm up at very different
    speeds (b1=0.9 is a ~10-step window, b2=0.999 a ~1000-step one), so without
    correction the sqrt(v) denominator is far too small early and the first
    updates are huge and erratic - which is also why Adam benefits from warmup.

Q10: Is L2 regularization the same as weight decay?
A10: In plain SGD yes: adding lambda*theta to the gradient equals multiplying
     theta by (1 - alpha*lambda). In Adam no: the lambda*theta term passes
     through the adaptive denominator, so parameters with large historical
     gradients get divided by a large sqrt(v_hat) and receive less decay, which
     couples regularization strength to gradient magnitude. AdamW decouples them
     by applying -alpha*wd*theta outside the adaptive step, which is why decay
     values are not transferable between Adam and AdamW.

Q11: Adam converges faster. Why is SGD + momentum still used?
A11: Adam is faster in training loss and tolerates bad scaling, but it
     tends to land in sharper minima and generalize slightly worse, while SGD's
     noise biases it toward flat basins. On vision CNNs, SGD + Nesterov + cosine
     still tends to beat AdamW at equal budget. On Transformers the reverse
     holds: gradient scales differ wildly across embeddings, attention and
     LayerNorm parameters, and Adam's per-parameter normalization is what makes
     training stable at all. Rule of thumb: adaptive when scales are
     heterogeneous or tuning budget is low, SGD + momentum when you can tune.

Q12: Why is AdaGrad still preferred for very sparse features?
A12: It divides by the square root of the accumulated squared gradient per
     parameter, so a feature that fires in one example out of a million keeps a
     large effective step while frequent features are damped. That is exactly
     right for high-dimensional sparse linear models such as CTR prediction or
     bag-of-words, where the alternative is learning nothing about rare features
     or diverging on frequent ones. Its monotonically decaying LR - fatal for
     long deep runs - is harmless in a single-pass convex setting.

Q13: Why does learning-rate warmup help, especially for Transformers?
A13: Statistically, Adam's second moment is estimated from very few samples in
     the first steps, so early adaptive steps are erratic. Geometrically, the
     surface at initialization is badly conditioned and one oversized step is
     unrecoverable. Warmup keeps early steps small until curvature and the
     moment estimates settle, and it becomes mandatory with large batches, where
     the linear scaling rule asks for an alpha that would diverge at step 0.

Q14: You quadruple the batch size. What do you change?
A14: Scale alpha up - linearly (4x) for SGD/momentum, roughly sqrt(4) = 2x for
     Adam-family - and add or lengthen warmup so the larger step does not
     diverge at initialization. Recompute any step-based schedule, since an
     epoch now contains 4x fewer updates. Expect a generalization gap once you
     go well past the critical batch size, where extra samples stop improving
     gradient quality; LARS/LAMB or a batch-size ramp mitigate it.

Q15: With a constant learning rate, does SGD reach the exact minimum?
A15: No. It reaches a noise ball around the minimum whose radius grows with
     alpha and with gradient variance sigma^2/B, then bounces there forever -
     the flat noisy plateau in loss curves. Exact convergence needs decaying
     steps satisfying sum(alpha_t) = infinity and sum(alpha_t^2) < infinity, for
     example alpha_0/t. This is why decaying the LR late in training produces a
     sudden visible drop in loss.

Q16: Your loss goes NaN at step 300. How do you debug it?
A16: Log the loss and global gradient norm every step and find the last finite
     step. A norm already growing means alpha is too high or clipping is
     missing. An instant jump from normal values points to a domain error:
     log(0) in cross-entropy, exp overflow in softmax/sigmoid, division by a
     zero-variance feature, or fp16 overflow without loss scaling. Fix by
     computing the loss from logits, adding eps inside logs, clipping sigmoid
     inputs, and checking the inputs for NaN/Inf - then reproduce with
     a fixed seed on one batch before changing anything else.

Q17: When do you clip gradients, and by norm or by value?
A17: Clip when the architecture can emit occasional huge gradients - RNNs over
     long sequences, Transformers, RL with high-variance returns - or when you
     see rare loss spikes. Prefer global-norm clipping, g := g*min(1, c/||g||)
     with c ~ 1.0, because it rescales the whole gradient and preserves the step
     direction, whereas value clipping truncates only the large coordinates and
     distorts direction. It is a safety net: if it fires on most steps, alpha is
     simply too high.

Q18: Is gradient accumulation over k micro-batches identical to one batch of
     k*B?
A18: For the gradient, yes - provided you average, or divide the loss by k,
     otherwise the effective LR is k times too large. Not identical in
     practice: BatchNorm computes statistics per micro-batch, and any loss that
     is non-linear across the batch (contrastive losses, batch-level metrics)
     does not decompose. LayerNorm models are unaffected, which is why this
     is the standard trick for training large models on small GPUs.

Q19: Newton's method converges quadratically. Why is it not used in deep
     learning?
A19: Cost - the Hessian is O(d^2) to store and O(d^3) to invert, hopeless at
     billions of parameters. Noise - mini-batch curvature estimates are far
     noisier than gradient estimates. Non-convexity - with negative eigenvalues,
     and saddle points are everywhere in high dimensions, the Newton step can
     move uphill. Cheaper surrogates get most of the benefit: Adam is a crude
     diagonal preconditioner and normalization layers re-condition the surface
     directly. L-BFGS remains the right tool for small, deterministic,
     full-batch problems.

Q20: Training loss drops fast then plateaus well above zero. Optimization
     problem or modeling problem?
A20: Separate them by experiment. Try to overfit a single mini-batch: if it will
     not reach ~zero loss, the problem is optimization or plumbing - dead units,
     vanishing gradients, an LR decayed to nothing, a detached gradient path. If
     it can, but the full training set plateaus, you are underfitting and need
     more capacity, better features, or less regularization. Also check whether
     the schedule decayed alpha too early, and whether the plateau is just the
     SGD noise floor - drop the LR once and see if the loss steps down.
