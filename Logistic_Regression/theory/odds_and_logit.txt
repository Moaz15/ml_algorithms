ODDS, LOG-ODDS AND READING COEFFICIENTS
=======================================

THE PROBLEM THIS FILE SOLVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We want to keep the thing that makes linear models good — a weighted sum of
features — and use it to predict whether a subscriber churns.

    score = -2.0 + 0.8·weeks_inactive - 0.03·tenure_months + 1.2·is_trial

A weighted sum can come out as -2, as 0.4, as 17. It can be any real number, and
there is no way to stop it. But a probability has to sit between 0 and 1.

So the two sides do not fit. Either we cripple the right-hand side, or we find
something on the left that CAN take any real number and still carries the
information we want. The second option is what logistic regression does, and it
takes two steps.


STEP 1 — ODDS REMOVE THE CEILING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Instead of "12% of users like this churn", say it as a ratio:

    12 churn for every 88 who stay

That is odds: how many times more likely the event is than its absence.

    odds = p / (1 - p)

    p = 0.12  ->  0.12 / 0.88  =  0.136     "about 1 to 7"
    p = 0.50  ->  0.50 / 0.50  =  1.0       "even money, 1 to 1"
    p = 0.75  ->  0.75 / 0.25  =  3.0       "3 to 1"
    p = 0.90  ->  0.90 / 0.10  =  9.0       "9 to 1"

Watch what the denominator does. As p climbs toward 1, (1-p) shrinks toward zero
and the odds shoot off to infinity. The ceiling at 1 is gone — odds live in
(0, infinity).

Note before going further: odds are NOT probability. p = 0.75 is "3 to 1", not
3. Everyday speech blurs these ("a 3 to 1 chance") and interviewers check
whether you do.

Halfway there. But odds still cannot go negative, and our weighted sum certainly
can.


STEP 2 — THE LOG REMOVES THE FLOOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Take the natural log of the odds:

    log-odds = ln( p / (1-p) )

Logs turn a floor at zero into no floor at all: ln of a tiny positive number is
a large negative one. Now the left-hand side spans (-infinity, +infinity),
exactly like our weighted sum.

All three scales side by side:

        p       odds     log-odds
      0.10      0.111      -2.197
      0.25      0.333      -1.099
      0.50      1.000       0.000
      0.75      3.000       1.099
      0.90      9.000       2.197

Same information three ways — each converts to the others exactly. But look at
the log-odds column: it is SYMMETRIC around zero. p = 0.9 and p = 0.1 sit at
+2.197 and -2.197, mirror images. Probability has no such symmetry, and odds
certainly do not (9 versus 0.111).

That symmetry is why the scale is pleasant to model on. Zero means "even odds,
no evidence either way", positive means the event is favoured, negative means it
is not, and the distances are comparable in both directions.


STEP 3 — THAT IS THE WHOLE MODEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Put the weighted sum on the right and the log-odds on the left, and nothing is
left to fix:

    ln( p / (1-p) ) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + βₙxₙ

This is why the assumption is stated as "linear in the LOGIT" rather than
"linear in p" (assumptions.txt section 2). The logit is just the name for
ln(p/(1-p)).

To get a probability back out, undo the two steps. Exponentiate to undo the log,
then rearrange to undo the ratio:

    from  ln(p/(1-p)) = z
          p/(1-p) = e^z
          p = e^z(1-p)  ->  p(1 + e^z) = e^z  ->  p = e^z / (1 + e^z)
          p = 1 / (1 + e^(-z))

That last line is the sigmoid. So the logit and the sigmoid are inverses: the
logit takes probability to score, the sigmoid takes score back to probability.
Logistic regression is a linear model on the logit, read back through the
sigmoid.


READING A COEFFICIENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Back to the churn model. What does 0.8 on weeks_inactive actually mean?

Strictly: each extra inactive week adds 0.8 to the LOG-ODDS of churn, holding
the other features fixed. True, complete, and useless in a meeting — nobody
thinks in log-odds.

So undo the log, the same way we did above. The model is additive in log-odds,
which makes it MULTIPLICATIVE in odds:

    odds ratio = e^β

    weeks_inactive   β = +0.80   e^0.80 = 2.23
                     each inactive week MULTIPLIES the odds of churn by 2.2

    is_trial         β = +1.20   e^1.20 = 3.32
                     trial users have 3.3x the odds of churn of paid users,
                     all else equal

    tenure_months    β = -0.03   e^-0.03 = 0.97
                     each extra month multiplies the odds by 0.97, about 3%
                     lower

Reading the number:

    OR > 1    raises the odds     (positive coefficient)
    OR = 1    no effect            (coefficient of zero)
    OR < 1    lowers the odds     (negative coefficient)

An OR below 1 often reads better inverted: 0.25 is "a quarter the odds", the
same claim as "4x the odds the other way round".

Two cautions:
  - An odds ratio is not a risk ratio. "3.3x the odds" is not "3.3x as likely".
    They are close only when the probabilities involved are small.
  - It applies per ONE UNIT of the feature. Measure tenure in years instead of
    months and the same relationship reports OR = 0.70, not 0.97. Always say
    what the unit is.


WHY THE SAME COEFFICIENT DOES DIFFERENT THINGS TO PROBABILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here is the consequence people miss. The coefficient is constant on the log-odds
scale. It is NOT constant on the probability scale.

Take that same +0.8 from one extra inactive week, applied to three users who
start at different scores:

    starting z    p before    p after    change in p
       -2.0        0.119       0.232       +0.113
        0.0        0.500       0.690       +0.190
       +2.0        0.881       0.943       +0.062

Same coefficient, same one-week change, three different effects. The impact is
biggest near p = 0.5, where the sigmoid is steepest, and shrinks at both ends
where it flattens out.

    In plain terms: one inactive week matters enormously for a user on the fence
    and barely at all for one already certain to leave.

Which is exactly the behaviour we wanted when we rejected the straight line in
concept.txt — probability has to flatten near 0 and 1.

So never say "this feature raises churn probability by 8%". Say it in odds, or
quote the probability change for a stated starting point.


THE INTERCEPT, AND CATEGORICAL FEATURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTERCEPT. The -2.0 is the log-odds when every feature is zero: a paid user with
zero weeks inactive and zero tenure. Convert it the usual way — e^-2.0 = 0.135,
so odds of about 1 in 7.4, or p = 0.119. As in linear regression, it only means
something if "all features zero" is a real state; centre the continuous features
if it is not.

CATEGORICAL FEATURES are read against their reference level. One-hot encode plan
type and drop "monthly" as the reference. A coefficient of -0.5 on is_annual
then means:

    annual subscribers have e^-0.5 = 0.61 times the odds of churn
    OF A MONTHLY SUBSCRIBER

— not in absolute terms. Change which level you dropped and every one of those
numbers changes, though the predictions do not.


COMPARING FEATURES AGAINST EACH OTHER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
It is tempting to rank features by coefficient size. In our model:

    weeks_inactive   β = +0.80    ranges over roughly 0 to 8 in the data
    tenure_months    β = -0.03    ranges over roughly 0 to 60

0.80 looks 27 times larger than 0.03. But multiply each coefficient by the range
it actually moves over: inactivity shifts the log-odds by up to +6.4, tenure by
up to -1.8. Still bigger, but nothing like 27x.

And the coefficient is not even a fixed property of the feature. Switch tenure
from months to years and it changes from -0.03 to -0.36, with identical data and
identical predictions. A number that moves when you change units cannot measure
importance.

To rank features honestly:
  1. STANDARDIZE the features, then refit. Each coefficient is then the log-odds
     change per one standard deviation — a common footing.
  2. Quote the standard error alongside. 0.6 +/- 0.5 is a shrug; 0.2 +/- 0.02 is
     a finding.
  3. Remember correlated features split the credit arbitrarily and can swap
     ranks between refits. Neither ranking is real (assumptions.txt section 4).

With L1 regularization, a coefficient of zero means "not needed given the
others", not "irrelevant".


QUICK REFERENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    probability -> odds          odds = p / (1 - p)
    odds -> probability          p = odds / (1 + odds)
    probability -> log-odds      z = ln( p / (1-p) )
    log-odds -> probability      p = 1 / (1 + e^(-z))
    coefficient -> odds ratio    OR = e^β
    odds ratio -> coefficient    β = ln(OR)

    p = 0.5  <->  odds 1  <->  z = 0
    p = 0.75 <->  odds 3  <->  z = +1.10
    p = 0.9  <->  odds 9  <->  z = +2.20

Traps worth rehearsing:
  - Odds are not probability, and an odds ratio is not a risk ratio.
  - Coefficients are additive in log-odds, multiplicative in odds, and neither
    in probability.
  - A one-unit change means nothing until you say what the unit is.
  - Raw coefficient sizes cannot be compared across differently-scaled features.
