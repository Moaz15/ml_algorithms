ASSUMPTIONS OF LOGISTIC REGRESSION
==================================
WHAT AN "ASSUMPTION" MEANS HERE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
You have fitted a churn model. It returns coefficients, probabilities and
p-values, and it will return them whether or not it had any business being used
on your data — nothing throws an error.

An assumption is a condition that has to hold for those outputs to mean what you
think they mean. Break one and the model still runs; it just quietly lies about
something. The six below are ordered the way you meet them: what you are
predicting, what the model claims, what your rows are, how your features relate,
how much data you have, and which points dominate the fit.


1. THE OUTCOME MUST BE DISCRETE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The first question is whether this is even a classification problem.

    "Will this user play any episode next week?"    -> yes/no, a class
    "How many episodes will they play?"             -> a count, not a class

The assumption: y is binary (0/1), or categorical for the multinomial and
ordinal extensions.

Why: the model maximizes the Bernoulli likelihood, P(y|x) = ŷ^y (1-ŷ)^(1-y),
which is only defined for y in {0,1}. Hand it y = 7.3 and the log-likelihood
stops being a meaningful quantity. Hand it y = 0.6 (a soft label) and it still
computes, but you are fitting a probability to a probability, not classifying.

Counts belong to Poisson regression, continuous targets to linear regression.

Once you know it is classification, pick the right variant:

    2 classes             binary logistic
    3+ unordered          multinomial / softmax
                          (genre: drama, crime, comedy)
    3+ ordered            ordinal logistic, which preserves the ranking
                          (rating: low, medium, high)

2. THE LOGIT MUST BE LINEAR IN THE PREDICTORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Now the model's actual claim. Suppose churn rises with user age among
teenagers, falls through middle age, then rises again among retirees — a U
shape.

The model fits ONE coefficient for age. It averages the rising and falling
halves to roughly zero and reports that age does not matter, while
misclassifying both ends. No amount of data fixes it.

The assumption:

    ln( p / (1-p) ) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + βₙxₙ

Note carefully what it does NOT say. It does not say p is linear in x — that
relationship is always an S-curve. It says the LOG-ODDS move at a constant rate
per unit of x. This is a much weaker claim, and stating it as "linear
relationship with the target" is the most common way to get this wrong.

How to spot it:
  - Binned logit plot: split a continuous feature into deciles, compute the
    empirical log-odds in each bin, plot against the bin midpoints. Straight is
    fine; curved is not. Easiest and most direct.
  - Box-Tidwell test: add an x·ln(x) term. A significant coefficient means the
    logit is not linear in that feature.
  - Residuals plotted against each continuous feature.

How to fix it: add a quadratic term or an interaction; log or square-root
transform the feature; bin it into buckets (handles any shape, loses
information); or use splines / a GAM.

Worth knowing why you would fix this rather than switch to XGBoost: logistic
regression serves in microseconds (one dot product), scales to billions of
sparse features with online learning, and stays interpretable enough for
regulatory sign-off. In ad ranking and credit scoring those often outrank a
point of AUC.


3. THE OBSERVATIONS MUST BE INDEPENDENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Next, what a row actually is. Compare two datasets of 100 rows:

    100 events from 100 different users   -> 100 independent pieces of evidence
    100 events from 5 power users         -> 5 people's habits, counted 20x each

The second looks like 100 rows to the model. It is not.

The assumption: each row is a separate draw, and one row's outcome tells you
nothing about another's beyond what the features already say.

What breaks: the COEFFICIENTS come out roughly right, but the standard errors
are far too small. That inflates z-scores, shrinks p-values, and makes you
declare features significant when they are not — Type I errors. Your model is
fine; your confidence in it is not.

Common violations: repeated measures on the same user; users clustered by
region, device or cohort; anything time-ordered, where today's state depends on
yesterday's.

How to spot it: ask what one row represents, which catches most cases. Then
intraclass correlation for grouped data, or Durbin-Watson for time-ordered data.

How to fix it: aggregate to one row per user; use cluster-robust standard errors
(corrects the p-values, leaves coefficients alone); or fit a mixed-effects model
(GLMM) with a random intercept per group.


4. NO PERFECT MULTICOLLINEARITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Now how the features relate to EACH OTHER. Put both "total listening minutes"
and "average minutes per session" into the churn model. They move together, so
when churn drops, the fit cannot tell which one deserves the credit. It splits
it arbitrarily — and splits it differently every time you refit.

The assumption: no feature is an exact linear combination of the others. Strong
correlation short of exact is a quality problem rather than a violation, but it
causes most of the practical damage.

Why it hurts, in one formula:

    Cov(β̂) = (XᵀWX)⁻¹ ,     W = diag( pᵢ(1 - pᵢ) )

Note the W, which linear regression does not have. Each row is weighted by how
uncertain the model is about it: points near p = 0.5 carry almost all the
information, points it is already sure about contribute almost nothing. When
features are collinear, XᵀWX approaches singular and these variances explode.

What breaks: coefficients swing wildly between refits, sometimes with signs that
make no sense; standard errors balloon; features that obviously matter come out
insignificant. Predictions are largely unaffected — this damages interpretation,
not accuracy.

How to spot it: correlation matrix for a quick look, then VIF (above 5, strictly
above 10, is a flag).

How to fix it: drop or merge the redundant feature; L2 regularization, which
adds λI before inversion and forces invertibility; or PCA on the correlated
block.


5. ENOUGH DATA, AND ENOUGH OF THE RARE CLASS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Now sample size — and the count that matters is not rows.

    50,000 rows, 20 features, fraud is 0.2% of them
    -> 100 fraud cases for 20 features
    -> 5 events per variable

Fifty thousand rows sounds ample. Five examples per coefficient is not.

The assumption: enough observations of the RARE class relative to the number of
parameters. Rule of thumb: at least 10 events per variable (EPV), ideally 15.

The specific failure mode is PERFECT SEPARATION, and it is worth understanding
because it looks like a bug. Suppose one sparse feature happens to be 1 for
every fraud case and 0 for everything else. The likelihood is then maximized by
driving that coefficient toward infinity, because a perfect step beats every
finite S-curve. The optimizer never converges.

What breaks: coefficients in the hundreds, enormous standard errors, convergence
warnings, and a training loss that keeps falling toward zero. It is most likely
with small samples, rare classes, and high-cardinality categorical features.

How to fix it: L1 or L2 regularization, which makes infinite weights infinitely
expensive; Firth's penalized likelihood; or merging the offending category. See
regularization.txt.


6. NO SINGLE POINT SHOULD DOMINATE THE FIT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Finally, individual rows. A data-entry error records a user's tenure as 9999
months. Its log-odds are extreme, and if its label disagrees with the model, the
loss from that one row dominates the gradient and drags the boundary toward it.

The reason is that cross-entropy is unbounded on the wrong side: a confidently
wrong prediction costs -log(tiny), which is enormous.

The nuance that matters: an extreme feature value with a CONSISTENT label is
mostly harmless. The model is already confident there, and the gradient p(1-p)
is tiny. The damage comes from an extreme value with a SURPRISING label — that
is influence, not leverage alone.

How to spot it: Cook's distance, hat values (leverage), standardized Pearson or
deviance residuals.

How to fix it: investigate first, since many such points are data errors. Then
winsorize at the 99th percentile, log-scale heavy-tailed features, or
regularize.


NOW THE USEFUL PART: WHAT IS NOT ON THE LIST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
With all six in hand, the comparison is worth making. Logistic regression
assumes far less than people expect, and candidates routinely recite linear
regression's list by mistake.

NOT assumed:
  - Normally distributed features. There is no distributional assumption on X.
  - Normally distributed errors. The errors are Bernoulli by construction.
  - Homoscedasticity. The variance IS p(1-p), so it changes with the prediction
    by design — largest at p = 0.5, smallest near 0 and 1.
  - A linear relationship between the features and y. The linearity is with the
    log-odds (assumption 2), which is a far weaker claim.

The reason: those extra conditions exist in linear regression to justify least
squares and its standard errors. Logistic regression is fitted by maximum
likelihood on a Bernoulli model, which brings its own variance structure.


HOW BADLY DOES EACH ONE HURT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Not equally, and knowing the ranking is worth more than knowing the list.

    1 and 2 broken  ->  the model is MIS-SPECIFIED. Coefficients are biased,
                        predictions systematically wrong. Worst case.
    3 broken        ->  coefficients are roughly fine; STANDARD ERRORS are too
                        small, so p-values lie. Predictions are usable.
    4, 5, 6 broken  ->  coefficients are unstable or diverge; predictions are
                        often still acceptable.

The practical read: if you only need to rank users, violations of 3 to 6 may not
matter much. If you need to report what a feature does, they matter enormously.


SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Assumption              Breaks             Detection           Fix
----------------------  -----------------  ------------------  ----------------
1 Discrete outcome      Wrong model        Check the target    Right variant
2 Linear logit          BIAS               Binned logit plot   Transform, spline
3 Independence          Standard errors    ICC; what is a row  Cluster SEs, GLMM
4 No multicollinearity  Unstable coefs     VIF > 5             Drop, L2, PCA
5 Enough events (EPV)   Divergence         EPV < 10            L1/L2, Firth
6 No dominant points    Boundary shifts    Cook's distance     Winsorize, L2
