Logistic Regression: Core Assumptions 
----------------------------------------

1. Binary or Categorical Outcome

The Assumption: The dependent variable (Y) must be discrete—either binary (0, 1) for standard logistic regression, or categorical for multinomial/ordinal extensions.
The Math: Logistic regression models the probability of class membership (p = P(Y=1|X)). Continuous outcomes violate this as they cannot be mapped to Bernoulli distributions.


"What happens if you run standard logistic regression on a continuous target?"
Answer: The optimization will fail or produce meaningless probabilities. Logistic regression relies on Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) based on the Bernoulli log-likelihood. Continuous targets require a different loss function (like Mean Squared Error for Gaussian distributions).


* **"How do you handle a target with 3+ unordered classes vs 3+ ordered classes?"**
* *Answer:* For unordered classes (e.g., predicting Ad clicks: *No Click, Click Text, Click Image*), use **Multinomial Logistic Regression** (Softmax regression) which uses a cross-entropy loss. For ordered classes (e.g., User Satisfaction: *Low, Medium, High*), use **Ordinal Logistic Regression** (Proportional Odds Model) to preserve the ranking.

## 2. Linearity of Logit with Predictors

* **The Assumption:** The natural log of the odds (the logit) must have a linear relationship with the continuous independent variables.
* **The Math:**

$$\ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \dots + \beta_nx_n$$


* **Detection:**
* **Box-Tidwell Test:** Formally adds an interaction term $x \cdot \ln(x)$ to the model. If its coefficient is statistically significant ($p < 0.05$), the linearity assumption is violated.
* **Quantile Binning Logit Plots:** Divide a continuous feature into equal-sized bins, calculate empirical log-odds for each bin, and plot against the bin midpoints to check for a straight line.


* **Production Fixes:**
* **Feature Engineering:** Apply polynomial terms ($x^2$), log/square-root transformations, or interaction terms.
* **Discretization:** Bin continuous variables into categorical buckets to handle non-linear effects natively (at the cost of information loss).
* **Splines:** Use Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) for piecewise polynomials.



### 💡 FAANG Interview Questions

* **"Derive the probability formula $p$ from the logit equation."**
* *Answer:* Show that if $\ln(p / (1-p)) = z$, then $p / (1-p) = e^z$. Solving for $p$ yields the sigmoid function:

$$p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$




* **"If a feature has a U-shaped relationship with the target, how does standard logistic regression behave, and how do you fix it?"**
* *Answer:* The model will struggle to draw a linear decision boundary, resulting in low AUC-ROC/high LogLoss because the linear coefficient will average out to near zero. Fix it by adding a quadratic term ($x^2$) or using B-splines.


* **"Why would we fix this assumption in a Logistic Regression model instead of just switching to XGBoost?"**
* *Answer:* In high-throughput production pipelines (e.g., ad ranking, fraud detection), logistic regression offers **ultra-low serving latency** (a simple dot product), scales seamlessly to **billions of sparse features** via online learning (e.g., FTRL-Proximal), and ensures strict **model interpretability** required for regulatory compliance.

## 3. Independence of Observations

* **The Assumption:** Erroneous components (residuals) must be uncorrelated. The outcome of one data point cannot influence another.
* **The Danger:** Violations deflate standard errors, inflating $Z$-scores and causing artificially low $p$-values. This leads to severe **Type I errors** (false positives).
* **Common Violations (Dependencies):**
* *Clustered/Nested:* Users grouped by geographic region or device types.
* *Temporal/Autocorrelation:* Multi-day actions by the same user where current state depends on prior state.


* **Detection:**
* **Intraclass Correlation (ICC):** Measures variance explained by clusters.
* **Durbin-Watson:** Detects autocorrelation in time-series sequences.


* **Production Fixes:**
* **Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE):** Modifies the optimization to inject a working correlation matrix.
* **Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM):** Introduces fixed and random effects to account for group-specific baselines.
* **Clustered Robust Standard Errors:** Adjusts the variance-covariance matrix post-estimation to provide "honest" $p$-values without altering the coefficients.



### 💡 FAANG Interview Questions

* **"If you have 100 event logs from 5 power-users, and 100 event logs from 100 unique users, can you feed them into the same logistic regression model?"**
* *Answer:* No. The 100 logs from 5 users violate the independence assumption due to intra-user correlation. The model will treat them as 100 independent votes, overindexing on those 5 users. You must either downsample, aggregate at the user level, or use a Mixed Effects model with a random intercept for `user_id`.

## 4. No Perfect Multicollinearity

* **The Assumption:** Predictors must not be perfectly or highly linearly correlated ($X_i \approx f(X_j)$).
* **The Math:** High multicollinearity makes the design matrix $X^TX$ near-singular (non-invertible). This causes the variance of the coefficients to explode:

$$\text{Var}(\beta) \propto (X^TX)^{-1}$$



Coefficients become highly unstable, swinging wildly with tiny data changes.
* **Detection:** **Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)**. A $\text{VIF} > 5 \text{ or } 10$ indicates severe multicollinearity.
* **Production Fixes:** Drop redundant features, apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to decouple features, or apply **L2 Regularization (Ridge)** to condition the matrix.

### 💡 FAANG Interview Questions

* **"Why does L2 regularization fix multicollinearity mathematically?"**
* *Answer:* L2 regularization adds a diagonal penalty term ($\lambda I$) to the matrix before inversion: $(X^TX + \lambda I)^{-1}$. This forces the matrix to be strictly positive-definite and invertible, stabilizing the variance of the weights ($\beta$).



---

## 5. Adequate Sample Size & The Rare Class Problem

* **The Assumption:** Requires sufficient observations relative to the number of parameters to avoid overfitting and ensure MLE convergence.
* **The Rule of Thumb:** At least 10–15 observations per predictor. For imbalanced data, the minority class must have at least 10 **Events Per Variable (EPV)**.
* **The Risk:** Small sample sizes or severe class imbalances lead to **Complete Separation** (where a predictor perfectly splits 0s and 1s). This causes MLE weights to diverge to infinity ($\pm\infty$) and optimization loops to fail.
* **Production Fixes:** Firth’s Penalized Likelihood (adds a Jeffreys prior penalty), L1/L2 regularization, or downsampling/upsampling paradigms.

### 💡 FAANG Interview Questions

* **"You are training a model on a dataset with a 99.9% negative class imbalance (e.g., fraud). Your logistic regression coefficients explode to infinity. What happened and how do you fix it?"**
* *Answer:* This is **complete or quasi-complete separation**. A sparse feature perfectly segregates the fraud cases, pushing the MLE loss gradient to seek infinite weights to achieve a perfect 0/1 step probability. Fix this by applying **L1 or L2 regularization** to penalize large weights, or use **Firth's Correction**.



---

## 6. No Extreme Outliers

* **The Assumption:** The model is highly sensitive to leverage and high-influence anomalies because it optimizes log-likelihood using continuous values.
* **The Impact:** An outlier with an extreme $X$ value yields massive log-odds ($\beta^T X$). Because the sigmoid asymptotes at 0 and 1, a misclassified extreme outlier generates an enormous penalty in the cross-entropy loss function:

$$\text{Loss} = -[y \log(p) + (1-y)\log(1-p)]$$



This forces the decision boundary to shift drastically to accommodate a single anomalous point, degrading overall generalization.
* **Detection:** Cook's Distance, Hat values (Leverage), and Standardized Residuals.
* **Production Fixes:** Winsorization (capping at the 99th percentile), log-scaling heavy-tailed distributions, or shifting to a robust loss function (Huberized loss).

### 💡 FAANG Interview Questions

* **"How do L1 vs L2 regularization differ in handling noisy or outlier features?"**
* *Answer:* **L2 (Ridge)** keeps all features but shrinks the weights of noisy/outlier features uniformly toward zero, mitigating their dominance. **L1 (Lasso)** drives the coefficients of completely noisy or redundant features exactly to zero, performing built-in feature selection.
