2. FOUNDATIONS

2.1 What regularization is, and why it works
--------------------------------------------

Training a model means minimizing error on data I already have. The problem is that data is signal
plus noise, and nothing in that objective tells the model which is which. Regularization adds a
second term to the loss that charges the model for complexity - usually the size of its weights. Now
every large weight has to pay for itself: a feature only gets a big coefficient if it cuts the error
enough to justify the cost. Patterns that exist only in this particular sample can't cover that
cost, so they get pushed toward zero. And small weights matter because a large weight means the
prediction swings hard on a tiny change in input; small weights give a smooth prediction surface, so
slightly unfamiliar data still gets a sensible answer.

> Real-world: predicting house prices, an unregularized model might learn "blue curtains add 50k"
  because that happened twice in training. Regularization asks that feature to justify a large
  weight, it can't, and the weight collapses toward zero.

> Likely follow-up: "So you're deliberately making the model worse?"
  On training data, yes. I'm trading a little training accuracy for a lot of stability on unseen
  data, and the test score is the only one that matters.

2.2 Overfitting, underfitting, and model capacity
-------------------------------------------------

Capacity is just how many different patterns a model is able to represent. Too little capacity and
it can't even fit the training data - error is high everywhere, that's underfitting. Too much and it
can fit anything, including the noise - training error goes to nearly zero while test error gets
worse. I diagnose it by the gap, not by any single number: both errors high means underfit, low
training error with high validation error means overfit. Regularization is how I dial effective
capacity down without changing the model class.

> Real-world: a student who memorizes ten years of past exam papers. Perfect on the papers he has
  seen, lost the moment the question is phrased differently. The student who learned the concepts
  scores slightly lower on the old papers and much higher on the real exam.

> Likely follow-up: "How do you actually see this in practice?"
  Learning curves - train and validation error plotted against epochs or training-set size.
  Overfitting is the point where the two lines start diverging.

2.3 Bias-variance tradeoff
--------------------------

Bias is the error from the model being too simple to represent the real pattern - it's wrong in the
same direction every time. Variance is the error from being too sensitive to the particular sample -
retrain on a different slice of data and you get a noticeably different model. Total error is bias
plus variance plus irreducible noise, and lambda is the dial between the first two: raise it and you
add bias but kill variance, drop it to zero and you get a low-bias, high-variance model. There is no
setting that minimizes both, so I cross-validate to find the bottom of that U-curve.

> Real-world: archery. Bias is a misaligned scope - every arrow tightly grouped, all of them off-
  center. Variance is shaky hands - arrows scattered all around the bull's eye. Regularization is
  steadying the hands at the cost of nudging the scope slightly off.

> Likely follow-up: "Does collecting more data fix bias or variance?"
  Variance. More data will never rescue a model that is structurally too simple for the problem.

2.4 Penalty form vs constraint form, and the L1 / L2 geometry
-------------------------------------------------------------

Same idea written two ways. The penalty form says: minimize the error, and I will charge you a fine
that grows with the size of your weights. The constraint form says: minimize the error, but your
weights have to fit inside a fixed budget. They are equivalent - a bigger fine is just a tighter
budget. The constraint form is the one worth knowing, because it is the one you can draw, and the
drawing is what explains why L1 deletes features while L2 only shrinks them.

Take a model with just two weights, so every possible model is a single dot on a flat map - w1 along
one axis, w2 along the other. Two things live on that map. First, the plain unregularized best fit,
call it the bullseye, with rings of equal error spreading outward from it like contour lines on a
hiking map: the further out, the worse the error. Second, the region the budget allows, always
centred on the origin. For L2 the budget is w1 squared plus w2 squared, which draws a circle. For L1
the budget is |w1| + |w2|, which draws a diamond - spend everything on one weight and you sit on a
corner, split it evenly and you sit in the middle of an edge.

The bullseye is outside the allowed region, because the whole point is to force the weights smaller
than they want to be. So the answer is the lowest-error dot still inside the region, which is
wherever the growing rings first touch it. A circle is smooth the whole way round, so the ring
touches it at some ordinary spot where both weights are non-zero - L2 shrinks everything and deletes
nothing. A diamond's corners stick out further than the flat edges between them, so a ring arriving
from most directions hits a corner first, and a corner sits exactly on an axis, which means one
weight is exactly zero. That is L1 doing feature selection.

        L2 budget: a circle                     L1 budget: a diamond

                w2                                          w2
                |                                           o
              .-+-.                                        / \
            .'  |  '.                                     /   \
      ------+---+---+------ w1                    -------o--+--o-------- w1
            '.  |  .'                                     \   /
              '-+-'                                        \ /
                |                                           o

      no corners anywhere, so a ring        four corners, each sitting exactly on an
      touches at an ordinary point          axis, and they stick out further than the
      and both weights survive              edges - touching one sets that weight to 0

> Real-world: think of a fixed budget you spend across features. Under L2 the price per unit climbs
  the more you load onto a single feature, so concentrating is wasteful and you end up spreading the
  money thinly - every feature gets a little. Under L1 the price stays flat no matter how much you
  have already bought, so the smart move is to put everything into the few features with the best
  return and buy nothing at all from the rest. Those get exactly zero.

> Likely follow-up: "Why does it hit a corner rather than a flat edge?"
  Because the corners stick out furthest, so they get touched first from most directions. Hitting
  the middle of an edge needs the rings to arrive at one exact angle, which is the special case
  rather than the rule - and it gets rarer as you add features, which is why sparsity gets stronger
  in high dimensions, not weaker.

2.5 Multicollinearity and an ill-conditioned X'X
------------------------------------------------

When two features carry nearly the same information, the model genuinely cannot tell which one
deserves the credit. It can put +500 on one and -500 on the other and produce almost identical
predictions, so the fit looks fine while the weights are enormous, unstable, and liable to flip sign
if you resample the data. In matrix terms X'X is close to singular, and inverting it amplifies tiny
bits of noise into huge coefficients. Ridge adds lambda down the diagonal, which makes that matrix
well-conditioned again and forces the correlated features to share the weight instead of fighting
over it.

> Real-world: a churn model containing both "total spend in last 30 days" and "average spend per day
  in last 30 days" - effectively the same column. Unregularized, you get one huge positive and one
  huge negative coefficient and the model is uninterpretable. Ridge splits the credit between them
  and the weights become readable again.

> Likely follow-up: "Wouldn't Lasso solve it too?"
  It would zero one of them, which stabilizes predictions, but which one survives is close to
  arbitrary and can change between runs. If I want both selection and stability I use Elastic Net.

2.6 Explicit vs implicit regularization
---------------------------------------

Explicit regularization is anything I deliberately wrote into the loss - L1, L2, dropout, weight
decay. Implicit regularization is everything else in the training setup that quietly limits
complexity without ever appearing in the objective: SGD with small batches, the learning rate
schedule, stopping before full convergence, and the architecture itself. This matters because in
deep learning most of the generalization you get is implicit - teams routinely gain more from batch
size and learning rate than from tuning the weight decay coefficient.

> Real-world: nobody writes a penalty term saying "a cat shifted ten pixels right is still a cat",
  but a CNN's weight sharing enforces exactly that constraint structurally. That single
  architectural choice is why CNNs beat fully-connected networks on images given the same amount of
  data.

> Likely follow-up: "Name an implicit regularizer nobody calls a regularizer."
  Batch size. Small batches mean noisy gradients, and that noise regularizes. Scale the batch up
  without adjusting anything else and generalization usually degrades.

2.7 Regularization vs feature selection vs dimensionality reduction vs more data
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All four reduce overfitting, but they are not interchangeable. More data is the best fix whenever
it's available and affordable, because it cuts variance without adding bias. Regularization is what
I reach for when the data is fixed - it keeps every feature but caps how much influence any one of
them can have. Feature selection removes columns outright, which is what I want when serving latency
or interpretability is the real constraint. Dimensionality reduction keeps the information but
rewrites it into fewer directions, which helps with collinearity and speed but costs
interpretability, since a principal component is not a real feature anyone can reason about. In
practice I start with regularization because it's the cheapest thing to try, then move to selection
or reduction only for a specific reason.

> Real-world: a churn model with 3,000 features. First pass is L2 across all of them. If the model
  then has to serve in 10ms, I switch to L1, drop to the 50 features carrying real signal, and ship
  something small.

> Likely follow-up: "Which of these would you not use to fix overfitting?"
  PCA on its own. It's unsupervised - it keeps the directions with the most variance, not the ones
  that predict your target, so you can easily discard the signal and keep the noise.

2.8 Double descent in over-parameterized models
-----------------------------------------------

The textbook curve says test error falls, bottoms out, then rises as you add capacity. That holds
right up to the point where the model has roughly as many parameters as training examples - it can
just barely memorize the data, and it does so in the ugliest way possible, so test error spikes.
Push well past that point, far more parameters than data, and test error comes down again, often
below the earlier minimum. The reason is that once there are many ways to fit the data perfectly,
SGD tends to pick one of the smoothest ones - implicit regularization doing the work. The practical
takeaway is that "this model is huge relative to my data, so it must be overfitting" is no longer a
safe inference, and the fix for a bad model is sometimes a bigger one rather than a smaller one.

    test error
    ^
    |                                        .
    |   \                                   / \
    |    \                                 /   \
    |     \                               /     \
    |      \                             /       \
    |       \                           /         \
    |        \                         /           \
    |         \                       /             \
    |          \__                   /               \
    |             \_________________/                 \
    |                                                  \________________
    +----------------------------------------------------------------------> model capacity
                           ^                 ^                  ^
                       classical       interpolation   over-parameterized
                      sweet spot         threshold           regime
                               (parameters = training rows)

> Real-world: this is why a large language model with far more parameters than it has training
  examples per task doesn't simply memorize and collapse - it's operating well past the
  interpolation spike.

> Likely follow-up: "Then does regularization still matter for large models?"
  Yes. Weight decay, augmentation and early stopping still buy measurable gains. Double descent says
  raw capacity isn't the enemy, not that regularization stopped working.

3. THE PENALTIES

3.1 L2 / Ridge
--------------

The penalty is the sum of squared weights. Because the cost grows with the square, one big weight is
far more expensive than several small ones - a weight of 10 costs 100, while two weights of 5 cost
50 total - so Ridge always prefers to spread influence around rather than concentrate it. It shrinks
every weight toward zero but never reaches zero, and the reason is in the gradient: the pull is
proportional to the weight itself, so as a weight gets small the pull gets weak and the shrinking
stalls before it arrives. It's smooth everywhere, which means it has a closed-form solution and is
cheap and stable to fit. This is my default when I believe most features carry at least a little
signal, and especially when features are correlated.

> Real-world: medical diagnosis with 100 blood markers. Most carry a small amount of useful
  information. I don't want to delete any of them, but I also don't want one marker dominating the
  prediction. Ridge keeps all 100 and turns down the volume on each.

> Likely follow-up: "What exactly does Ridge do with two correlated features?"
  Splits the weight between them, roughly evenly. That's the same squared-cost argument - spreading
  a fixed total across two weights is cheaper than loading it all onto one.


3.2 L1 / Lasso
--------------

The penalty is the sum of absolute values, and the effect is sparsity - it drives weights to exactly
zero, so feature selection happens as part of fitting rather than as a separate step. The reason is
again in the gradient: the pull toward zero is a constant lambda no matter how small the weight
already is, so it never weakens. Any feature whose contribution to reducing error is worth less than
that constant pull gets driven to zero and pinned there. The price is that the absolute value has a
sharp corner at zero so there's no closed form, and it's unstable with correlated features - it
picks one of them essentially at random.

> Real-world: genetics, screening 20,000 genes when only 10 or 20 actually drive the disease. Lasso
  zeroes out the other 19,980 and hands back a short list you can take to a lab.

> Likely follow-up: "Are the coefficients that survived Lasso trustworthy?"
  No - they're biased toward zero, because the same constant pull that killed the others is still
  shrinking the survivors. If I need honest magnitudes I refit a plain unpenalized model on just the
  selected features.

3.3 Elastic Net
---------------

It's both penalties at once, with a knob controlling the mix. It exists because Lasso has two
specific failures: with correlated features it arbitrarily keeps one and kills the rest, and when
you have more features than rows it can't select more than n of them no matter what. The L2 part
restores the grouping behaviour so correlated features tend to enter or leave together, while the L1
part still zeroes out the genuine junk. So I get Lasso's feature selection with Ridge's stability.

> Real-world: text classification. "Awesome" and "excellent" are highly correlated. Lasso might keep
  one and delete the other, which makes the model look arbitrary to anyone reading it. Elastic Net
  keeps both with smaller, similar weights, while still zeroing out "the" and "and".

> Likely follow-up: "How do you tune two penalties at once?"
  I don't tune them as two separate lambdas. Scikit-learn reparameterizes it as alpha for total
  strength and l1_ratio for the mix, so I grid a few l1_ratio values and let alpha be cross-
  validated along the path - that's what ElasticNetCV does.

3.4 L0 and best-subset selection
--------------------------------

L0 penalizes the count of non-zero weights, which is literally what we mean when we say "use fewer
features" - nothing is shrunk, a feature is either in at full strength or out. The problem is that a
count has no useful gradient; it's flat everywhere and jumps at the boundaries, so there's nothing
for an optimizer to follow. Solving it honestly means trying every combination, which is 2^p subsets
- at just 30 features that's over a billion models. So L1 is the practical stand-in: it's the
closest convex relaxation of L0, meaning it's actually solvable, and under reasonable conditions it
recovers the same subset.

> Real-world: "pick the best 10 features out of 100" sounds like a small ask, but it's about 17
  trillion combinations. This is the whole reason Lasso earned its place - it turns an impossible
  search into one convex optimization.

> Likely follow-up: "Isn't stepwise selection doing the same job?"
  Stepwise is a greedy approximation of L0 and it's fragile - one early inclusion changes every
  decision after it, and the p-values it reports are invalid because the data was used to choose the
  model in the first place.

3.5 Group Lasso
---------------

Plain L1 treats every column as an independent thing to keep or drop, which is wrong whenever
columns come in natural groups. A one-hot encoded category is one concept spread across many
columns, and an embedding is one entity spread across many dimensions. Lasso will happily zero out
three of the five levels of a category, which doesn't mean anything - you've half-deleted a feature.
Group Lasso penalizes the norm of each group as a single unit, so a group either survives intact or
goes to zero entirely.

> Real-world: a model where "city" is one-hot encoded into 200 columns, and the real question is
  whether the pipeline should collect the city field at all. Lasso can't answer that - it keeps 40
  of the 200 columns, so you still need the field. Group Lasso drops all 200 or keeps all 200, which
  is the decision you actually have to make.

> Likely follow-up: "Where does this idea show up outside linear models?"
  Structured pruning in neural networks. You want to remove whole channels or attention heads, not
  scattered individual weights, because only whole-unit removal actually makes the model faster on
  real hardware.
