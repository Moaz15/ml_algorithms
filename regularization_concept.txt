2. FOUNDATIONS

2.1 What regularization is, and why it works
--------------------------------------------

WHAT IT IS
Any deliberate change to a learning algorithm that makes it fit the training data LESS well in
order to predict new data better. That is the whole definition, and it covers two delivery routes:

    charge for complexity     minimise  Loss(w) + lambda * penalty(w)    Ridge, Lasso, ccp_alpha
    cap capacity outright     max_depth, early stopping, bagging, fewer features

The first is what people usually mean by the word. The second adds nothing to the loss and is
regularization all the same - section 7 walks through both, algorithm by algorithm.

WHY IT WORKS
Your rows are signal plus noise, and "minimise training error" cannot tell the two apart - it
reproduces both. So the last stretch of training error is bought by memorising noise, and noise by
definition does not repeat on new data. Surrendering that stretch costs you nothing real.

--> under a charge, a weight survives only if the error it removes is worth more than it costs
--> a genuine pattern repays the charge; one that exists only in this sample cannot, so it collapses
--> bonus with weight penalties: small weights mean the prediction cannot swing on a tiny nudge

> Real-world: an unregularized house-price model learns "blue curtains add 50k" because it happened
  twice. That feature cannot justify the charge, so the weight dies.

> Likely follow-up: "So you are deliberately making the model worse?"
  On training data, yes - a little training accuracy for a lot of stability on unseen data. The
  test score is the only one that counts.

2.2 Overfitting, underfitting, and model capacity
-------------------------------------------------

CAPACITY is how many different patterns a model is able to represent. Both failures are capacity
set wrong, and you tell them apart by the GAP, never by either number alone.

UNDERFIT - too little capacity to represent the real pattern
--> train error high and validation error high, sitting close together
--> regularization is the wrong lever here; you need more capacity or better features
OVERFIT - enough capacity to fit the noise as well as the signal
--> train error near zero, validation error far worse - the gap itself is the symptom
--> this is what regularization is for: dial effective capacity down, same model class

> Real-world: a student who memorizes ten years of past exam papers. Perfect on the papers he has
  seen, lost the moment the question is phrased differently. The student who learned the concepts
  scores slightly lower on the old papers and much higher on the real exam.

> Likely follow-up: "How do you actually see this in practice?"
  Learning curves - train and validation error plotted against epochs or training-set size.
  Overfitting is the point where the two lines start diverging.

2.3 Bias-variance tradeoff
--------------------------

Total error = bias + variance + irreducible noise. Lambda is the dial between the first two: raise
it and you buy bias to kill variance. No setting minimises both, so cross-validate for the floor.

BIAS - the model is too simple to represent the real pattern
--> wrong in the same direction every time, whatever sample you train on
--> a straight line through a curve: every resample gives nearly the same wrong line
VARIANCE - the model is too sensitive to the particular sample you drew
--> retrain on a different slice of data and you get a noticeably different model
--> a depth-20 tree: two resamples give two trees that disagree all over the place

> Real-world: archery. Bias is a misaligned scope - every arrow tightly grouped, all of them off-
  center. Variance is shaky hands - arrows scattered all around the bull's eye. Regularization is
  steadying the hands at the cost of nudging the scope slightly off.

> Likely follow-up: "Does collecting more data fix bias or variance?"
  Variance. More data will never rescue a model that is structurally too simple for the problem.

2.4 Penalty form vs constraint form, and the L1 / L2 geometry
-------------------------------------------------------------

Same idea written two ways. The penalty form says: minimize the error, and I will charge you a fine
that grows with the size of your weights. The constraint form says: minimize the error, but your
weights have to fit inside a fixed budget. They are equivalent - a bigger fine is just a tighter
budget. The constraint form is the one worth knowing, because it is the one you can draw, and the
drawing is what explains why L1 deletes features while L2 only shrinks them.

Take a model with two weights, so every possible model is one spot on a map - w1 running east, w2
running north.

Now throw a stone onto that map, right where the unregularized best fit sits, and watch the ripples
spread out from it. Each ripple is a ring of models that are all equally wrong, and the wider the
ripple, the worse they are. Around the origin stands a fence: your budget. Inside it you can afford
the model, outside you cannot. The ripple spreads until it just grazes the fence, and THAT spot is
your answer - the best model the budget lets you have.

So the whole thing comes down to one question: where does a spreading ripple graze a fence first?

A ROUND fence (L2, the budget w1^2 + w2^2 <= c)
--> nothing on it sticks out, so the ripple brushes it at an unremarkable spot - say (0.7, 0.3)
--> a bit of each weight: both survive, both shrunk, and nothing is ever deleted
A DIAMOND fence (L1, |w1| + |w2| <= c) - this is where L1's feature selection comes from
--> four spikes, each pointing straight down an axis
--> a spike reaches furthest toward the oncoming ripple, so it is hit first from almost any angle
--> standing on a spike means (1.0, 0.0): the whole budget in one feature, the other exactly zero

        L2: a round fence                L1: a diamond fence

              w2                                 w2
              |                                  o
            .-+-@                               / \
     -------+-+-+------ w1              -------o-+-@------- w1
            '-+-'                               \ /
              |                                  o

      @ = first touch: an ordinary       @ = first touch: a spike, and a spike
      spot, (0.7, 0.3) - both live       IS an axis, (1.0, 0.0) - w2 is gone

> Real-world: a fixed budget spent across features. Under L2 the price per unit climbs the more you
  load onto one feature, so concentrating is wasteful - you spread the money thin and everyone gets
  a little. Under L1 the price stays flat however much you have already bought, so the smart move is
  to pour it all into the best few and buy nothing from the rest. Those are the zeros.

> Likely follow-up: "Does this still hold with 1000 features?"
  It gets stronger. To land on a flat edge the ripple has to arrive almost head-on to that edge - a
  narrow band of directions. The spikes catch everything else, and their share grows with every
  feature you add, so sparsity is more reliable in high dimensions, not less.

2.5 Multicollinearity and an ill-conditioned X'X
------------------------------------------------

Two features carry nearly the same information, and the model cannot tell which deserves credit.
--> it can put +500 on one and -500 on the other and still predict almost the same thing
--> the fit looks fine while the weights are enormous, unstable, and flip sign on a resample

WHY THEY EXPLODE
Easiest to see with a single feature, where the fitted weight is just a fraction:

    plain:   w = sum(x*y) / sum(x*x)          ridge:   w = sum(x*y) / ( sum(x*x) + lambda )

--> the denominator is how much independent variation that feature has to offer
--> two near-duplicates leave each other almost none, so you divide by near-nothing and fly off
--> adding lambda means the denominator can never get near zero

THE SAME THING IN MATRIX FORM
With many features the denominator becomes X'X: a square table, one row and column per feature,
holding how much each feature overlaps with the others.
--> "ill-conditioned" means two rows are near-copies, so dividing by it magnifies noise
--> ridge adds lambda the whole way down the diagonal - that bump is the ridge in the name
--> correlated features are then made to share the weight instead of fighting over it

> Real-world: a churn model containing both "total spend in last 30 days" and "average spend per day
  in last 30 days" - effectively the same column. Unregularized, you get one huge positive and one
  huge negative coefficient and the model is uninterpretable. Ridge splits the credit between them
  and the weights become readable again.

> Likely follow-up: "Wouldn't Lasso solve it too?"
  It would zero one of them, which stabilizes predictions, but which one survives is close to
  arbitrary and can change between runs. If I want both selection and stability I use Elastic Net.

2.6 Explicit vs implicit regularization
---------------------------------------

EXPLICIT is the part you wrote into the objective and can point at in the formula: the
lambda*penalty term in Ridge and Lasso, or alpha * (number of leaves) in cost-complexity pruning.

IMPLICIT is everything else in the setup that quietly limits what the model can express, while
never appearing in the objective at all:

  max_depth = 4          cannot express the 5-condition rule needed to isolate one odd row
  bagging                trees overfit their own resample differently; averaging cancels the noise
  max_features='sqrt'    hides the strongest feature from most splits, so trees stop being copies
  learning_rate = 0.05   each tree adds a twentieth of its advice, so a noisy one barely counts
  early stopping         stop at round 300 of 2000 - the rounds you skip would memorise noise
  k = 25 in KNN          averaging 25 neighbours means one mislabelled point cannot own its corner

> Real-world: a Random Forest has no penalty term anywhere in it, yet it is one of the hardest
  models to overfit. All of that comes from bagging and feature subsampling - regularization by
  construction rather than by formula.

> Likely follow-up: "Is max_depth really regularization, or just a smaller model?"
  Regularization. You are deliberately giving up capacity the model would otherwise use to fit the
  training data better, and whether that happens in the loss or in the hypothesis space does not
  change what it is doing.

2.7 Regularization vs feature selection vs dimensionality reduction vs more data
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All four cut overfitting, and they are not interchangeable. The order I actually use:
regularization first because it is cheapest, then selection or reduction only for a named reason.

MORE DATA - the only one that cuts variance without adding any bias
--> use it whenever it is available and affordable; nothing else dominates it
--> it cannot rescue a model that is structurally too simple - that is bias, not variance
REGULARIZATION - caps how much influence any column can have, without deleting any (L1 aside)
--> the move when the data is fixed, and the cheapest to try, so it goes first
--> cost: every surviving coefficient is shrunk, so the magnitudes stop being honest
FEATURE SELECTION - deletes columns outright
--> the move when serving latency or interpretability is the real constraint, not accuracy
--> cost: a dropped column takes whatever small signal it carried with it
DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION - keeps the information but rewrites it into fewer directions
--> the move for collinearity and speed when you cannot afford to throw information away
--> cost: a principal component is not a real feature anyone can reason about

> Real-world: a churn model with 3,000 features. First pass is L2 across all of them; when it has
  to serve in 10ms, switch to L1, keep the 50 columns carrying real signal, ship something small.

> Likely follow-up: "Which of these would you not use to fix overfitting?"
  PCA on its own. It is unsupervised - it keeps the directions with the most variance, not the ones
  that predict your target, so you can easily discard the signal and keep the noise.

3. THE PENALTIES

3.1 L2 / Ridge
--------------

    penalty = lambda * sum( w^2 )        squared, so one big weight costs more than several small

WHAT IT DOES - shrinks every weight toward zero, and never reaches it

--> a weight of 10 costs 100, two weights of 5 cost 50, so spreading influence is always cheaper
--> with orthonormal features the whole effect is one factor:  w_ridge = w_ols / (1 + lambda)
--> the pull toward zero is proportional to the weight, so it weakens as the weight shrinks and
    stalls before arriving - which is why nothing is ever deleted.
--> smooth everywhere, so there is a closed form and the fit is cheap and stable.

WHEN TO REACH FOR IT
--> most features carry at least a little signal and you do not want to lose any of them
--> features are correlated - Ridge splits the credit instead of picking a winner
--> cost: every column stays in the model, so serving cost and interpretability do not improve

> Real-world: medical diagnosis with 100 blood markers. Most carry a small amount of useful
  information. I don't want to delete any of them, but I also don't want one marker dominating the
  prediction. Ridge keeps all 100 and turns down the volume on each.

> Likely follow-up: "What exactly does Ridge do with two correlated features?"
  Splits the weight between them, roughly evenly. That's the same squared-cost argument - spreading
  a fixed total across two weights is cheaper than loading it all onto one.


3.2 L1 / Lasso
--------------

    penalty = lambda * sum( |w| )        absolute, so the price per unit never changes

WHAT IT DOES - drives weights to exactly zero, selecting features during the fit, not before it
--> the pull toward zero is a constant lambda however small the weight already is; it never weakens
--> so any feature worth less than that constant pull is driven to zero and pinned there
--> the exact rule is soft-thresholding (section 4): move every weight lambda closer to zero, and
    anything that would cross over is set to zero
THE PRICE
--> no closed form - the absolute value has a corner at zero, so it is solved iteratively
--> the survivors are shrunk too, so their magnitudes are biased; refit unpenalised on the selected
    columns when you need honest numbers
--> with correlated features it keeps one essentially at random, and the winner can change per run
--> when p > n it can never select more than n features, whatever lambda you choose

> Real-world: genetics, screening 20,000 genes when only 10 or 20 actually drive the disease. Lasso
  zeroes out the other 19,980 and hands back a short list you can take to a lab.

> Likely follow-up: "Are the coefficients that survived Lasso trustworthy?"
  No - they're biased toward zero, because the same constant pull that killed the others is still
  shrinking the survivors. If I need honest magnitudes I refit a plain unpenalized model on just the
  selected features.

3.3 Elastic Net
---------------

    penalty = alpha * ( l1_ratio * sum|w|  +  (1 - l1_ratio)/2 * sum w^2 )     sklearn's form

WHY IT EXISTS - the two places Lasso fails
--> correlated features: it keeps one at random and kills the rest
--> p > n: it cannot select more than n features no matter what
WHAT THE MIX BUYS
--> the L2 part restores the grouping effect, so correlated features enter and leave together
--> the L1 part still zeroes the genuine junk, so selection survives
--> net result: Lasso's feature selection with Ridge's stability
HOW TO TUNE IT
--> not as two lambdas - sklearn gives alpha for total strength and l1_ratio for the mix
--> grid a few l1_ratio values and let alpha be cross-validated along the path (ElasticNetCV)

> Real-world: text classification. "Awesome" and "excellent" are highly correlated. Lasso might keep
  one and delete the other, which makes the model look arbitrary to anyone reading it. Elastic Net
  keeps both with smaller, similar weights, while still zeroing out "the" and "and".

> Likely follow-up: "How do you tune two penalties at once?"
  I don't tune them as two separate lambdas. Scikit-learn reparameterizes it as alpha for total
  strength and l1_ratio for the mix, so I grid a few l1_ratio values and let alpha be cross-
  validated along the path - that's what ElasticNetCV does.

3.4 L0 and best-subset selection
--------------------------------

    penalty = lambda * (number of non-zero weights)     counts features, ignores how big they are

WHY THIS SECTION EXISTS
L0 is the penalty you actually want. It selects without shrinking - a feature is in at full strength
or out - so the survivors keep honest coefficients, which is precisely the bias Lasso leaves behind
(3.2). You settle for L1 anyway, because L0 cannot be computed.

WHY IT CANNOT BE OPTIMISED
--> the penalty is identical for w = 0.001 and w = 1000; it only asks whether w is zero
--> so its gradient is zero wherever it exists and jumps at zero - nothing for a solver to follow
--> solving it honestly means trying every subset: 2^p of them, already over a billion at p = 30
WHAT YOU USE INSTEAD
--> L1, the closest convex relaxation of L0 - actually solvable, and it recovers the same subset
    when the features are not too correlated
--> AIC and BIC are L0 penalties you have already used: -2logL + 2k and -2logL + k*ln(n), where the
    parameter count k IS the L0 term and the only difference is the price per feature
--> mixed-integer solvers now crack exact best-subset up to a few thousand features, so the honest
    verdict is "expensive and rarely worth it" rather than "impossible"

> Real-world: "pick the best 10 features out of 100" sounds like a small ask, but it's about 17
  trillion combinations. This is the whole reason Lasso earned its place - it turns an impossible
  search into one convex optimization.

> Likely follow-up: "Isn't stepwise selection doing the same job?"
  Stepwise is a greedy approximation of L0 and it's fragile - one early inclusion changes every
  decision after it, and the p-values it reports are invalid because the data was used to choose the
  model in the first place.

3.5 Group Lasso
---------------

    penalty = lambda * sum over groups of  sqrt(group size) * ||w_group||_2      one norm per GROUP

THE PROBLEM IT FIXES
Plain L1 judges every column on its own, which is wrong whenever columns arrive in natural blocks -
a one-hot category is one concept spread over many columns, an embedding is one entity spread over
many dimensions.
--> Lasso will cheerfully zero three of a category's five levels, which means nothing: you have
    half-deleted a feature and still have to collect the field
--> Group Lasso scores each group as a single unit, so a group survives intact or leaves entirely
--> the sqrt(group size) factor stops large groups being penalised merely for being large

> Real-world: a model where "city" is one-hot encoded into 200 columns, and the real question is
  whether the pipeline should collect the city field at all. Lasso can't answer that - it keeps 40
  of the 200 columns, so you still need the field. Group Lasso drops all 200 or keeps all 200, which
  is the decision you actually have to make.

> Likely follow-up: "What if a feature belongs to two groups at once?"
  That is overlapping group lasso, and the plain version misbehaves - zeroing a group drags its
  shared members down with it, so a feature survives only if every group containing it survives.
  The usual fix is the latent / union-of-groups formulation, which lets a feature live as long as
  one of its groups does.
