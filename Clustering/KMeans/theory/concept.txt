What is K-Means? (Partition / Centroid-based clustering)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Unsupervised clustering algorithm that partitions data into K clusters.
Goal/Objective: minimize within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS / inertia)

Produces K non-overlapping clusters + a centroid (mean) for each cluster.
Similarity measure: typically Euclidean distance (so scaling matters).
Output: cluster assignment for each point + centroid locations.
Best for: roughly spherical/convex, similar-sized clusters; works well in low–moderate dimensions.


K-Means Working Steps
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.Choose K
Methods: Elbow (WCSS/inertia), Silhouette score, domain knowledge.

2.Initialize centroids
Random K points or K-Means++ (preferred) for better stability.

3.Assignment step (E-step)
Assign each point to the nearest centroid (commonly Euclidean distance).

4.Update step (M-step)
Recompute each centroid as the mean of points in that cluster.

5.Repeat
Alternate assignment ↔ update until convergence.

6.Convergence criteria
Centroids change < tolerance, or
Assignments stop changing, or
Max iterations reached.

Key notes: each iteration non-increasing WCSS; can converge to a local optimum (use multiple restarts).


Assumptions of K-Means
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Cluster Shape: Spherical/Convex
   - Clusters are expected to be roughly ball-shaped and well-separated.
   - Reason: K-means uses Euclidean distance, which creates Voronoi regions (best for spherical shapes). Non-convex shapes (like Moons or Circles) often get misclustered.

2. Cluster Size & Density: Uniform
   - Clusters should be of similar size and similar density.
   - Reason: The algorithm minimizes the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE), which biases it toward equal-radius partitions. Dense or small clusters may be absorbed by larger, more spread-out ones.

3. Feature Type: Numeric & Continuous
   - Relies strictly on means and Euclidean distance.
   - Reason: Centroids are computed as the average (mean) of points. This isn't meaningful for categorical data (use K-Modes or K-Prototypes for those).

4. Feature Scaling: Normalized/Standardized
   - All features should contribute equally to the distance calculation.
   - Reason: Euclidean distance is highly sensitive to the scale of features. Larger-range features will dominate the clustering process unless standardized.

5. Cluster Membership: Hard Assignment
   - Each data point belongs to exactly one cluster with no overlap.
   - Reason: Points are assigned to the single nearest centroid. It cannot model probabilistic or overlapping memberships (Gaussian Mixture Models are better for this).

6. Number of Clusters: Predefined
   - The value of 'K' must be chosen upfront.
   - Reason: Optimization is conditioned on a fixed K; the algorithm does not infer the optimal number of clusters automatically.

7. Noise & Outliers: Low Sensitivity
   - K-means is very sensitive to outliers.
   - Reason: Since squared distances are used in the objective function, outliers pull centroids significantly away from the true cluster center, distorting the final assignments.


Distance Metrics in K-Means
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Default: Euclidean (L2)
   - Why Default: Standard K-Means objective is to minimize SSE (Inertia).
     Formula: Σ ||x - μ||² — this directly corresponds to L2 distance to the mean.
   - Geometric Intuition: Partitions space into Voronoi cells around centroids; favors spherical/ball-shaped clusters.
   - Key Property: For L2, the centroid update is simply the arithmetic mean (closed-form optimal solution).

2. Alternative: Manhattan (L1)
   - Note: Minimizing L1 distances requires the "centroid" to be the median. This is known as K-Medians or K-Medoids, not vanilla K-Means.
   - Benefit: More robust to outliers than L2 (less performance penalty on distant points).

3. Alternative: Cosine Distance
   - Application: Common for text and high-dimensional embeddings.
   - Use Case: When the direction of the vector matters more than its magnitude.
   - Practice: Typically requires L2-normalizing vectors (unit length) before clustering.

4. Can K-Means use Cosine Similarity?
   - Answer: Yes, but it requires a specific framing.
   - Mechanism: With L2-normalized vectors, Euclidean distance Squared and Cosine distance are monotonic:
     ||x - y||² = 2(1 - cos(x, y)) [where ||x|| = ||y|| = 1].
   - Result: This leads to Spherical K-Means, where centroids are re-normalized to unit length after each update to optimize cosine similarity.

5. Additional Considerations
   - Minkowski Distance: A general family (L1 and L2 are special cases). Changing the p-value typically changes the required centroid update rule.
   - Feature Scaling: Crucial! Both Euclidean and Manhattan are highly sensitive to scales; always standardize/normalize first.
   - Categorical Data: Hamming distance or similar doesn't fit mean-centroid updates; prefer K-Modes or K-Prototypes.


Objective Function: WCSS / Inertia
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Core Definition
   - WCSS (Within-Cluster Sum of Squares): Also known as inertia, it measures the compactness of clusters.
   - Meaning: The total squared distance of all points from their assigned cluster centroid. 
   - Goal: Lower WCSS indicates tighter, more cohesive clusters (for a fixed k).

2. Mathematical Objective
   Minimize: Σ (i=1 to k) Σ (x ∈ Ci) ||x - μi||²

   Where:
   - k: Total number of clusters.
   - Ci: The i-th cluster (the set of points assigned to it).
   - μi: The centroid (mean) of cluster Ci.
   - ||x - μi||²: The squared Euclidean distance between point x and centroid μi.

3. Key Interview Points
   - Centroid Optimality: Using the Squared L2 loss (Euclidean distance squared) is why the optimal centroid update is mathematically the arithmetic mean: μi = mean(Ci).
   - Convergence: Each iteration of the algorithm (Assignment ↔ Update) is guaranteed to never increase the WCSS. However, it may converge to a local minimum rather than the global optimum (which is why initialization matters).

Choosing the Right Number of Clusters (K)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. The Elbow Method
   - Core Idea: As K increases, clusters become smaller and more compact, causing the WCSS (Inertia) to decrease monotonically.
   - The Elbow Point: The value of K where adding another cluster provides diminishing returns (only a small additional drop in WCSS).
   - Implementation Steps:
     1. Run K-Means for a range of K values (e.g., 1 to 10 or 20).
     2. Compute the WCSS (Inertia) for each K.
     3. Plot WCSS vs. K.
     4. Select the K at the "elbow" or "bend" of the curve where the rate of improvement sharply slows down.
   - Best Use Case: When clusters are well-separated and roughly spherical/convex.
   - Limitations: The elbow can be ambiguous (subjective) if the curve is smooth. It is also sensitive to feature scaling, outliers, and initialization.

2. Silhouette Score
   - Core Idea: Provides a quantitative measure (-1 to +1) of overall cluster quality by answering two key questions for each data point i:
     - Cohesion: How close am I to my own cluster members? (Lower is better)
     - Separation: How far am I from the nearest neighboring cluster? (Higher is better)

   - Mathematical Formula:
     - a(i): Intra-cluster distance. Average distance of point i to all other points in the SAME cluster (meaures Cohesion).
     - b(i): Nearest-cluster distance. Average distance of point i to all points in the NEAREST neighboring cluster (measures Separation).
     - s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i))

   - Why Divide by max(a,b)?
     - Optimization: It normalizes the score to a fixed scale of -1 to +1. Without this normalization, the score would range from -∞ to +∞, making comparison between datasets impossible.

   - Interpretation of Cases:
     - s(i) ≈ +1 (a << b): Excellent clustering. Point i is very close to its own cluster and far from others.
     - s(i) ≈ 0  (a ≈ b): Overlapping clusters. Point i is on the decision boundary between two clusters.
     - s(i) ≈ -1 (a >> b): Poor clustering. Point i is likely assigned to the wrong cluster.

   - Aggregate Goal:
     - Avg Silhouette Score = Σ s(i) / n
     - The closer the average score is to +1, the higher the overall clustering quality.

   - Why Use Silhouette Score?
     1. Robust metric combining both Cohesion and Separation.
     2. Provides a tangible, quantitative measure of cluster quality.
     3. Helps determine the optimal K automatically by maximizing the average score.
     4. Agnostic to the specific clustering algorithm used.

   - Limitations to Consider:
     1. Computationally Expensive: Requires calculating all-to-all distances [O(N²)].
     2. Shape Bias: Assumes well-separated, convex clusters; fails on complex, non-convex shapes.
     3. Density Sensitivity: Less reliable for clusters with significant variations in size or density.
     4. Outlier Sensitivity: Individual scores can be heavily distorted by extreme values.
     5. Hard Clustering Only: Not suitable for modeling probabilistic or "soft" memberships.



3. Gap Statistic
   - Core Idea: Compares the WCSS of the observed data against the WCSS of a reference dataset with a uniform random distribution (no clusters).
   - Logic: A significant gap indicates that the data has a strong underlying cluster structure compared to random noise.
   - Selection: Choose the K that maximizes Gap(k) = E[log(WCSS_random)] - log(WCSS_data).

4. Domain Knowledge
   - Core Idea: Using prior business or scientific information to pre-determine the number of clusters.
   - Examples:
     - Market Segmentation: Creating specific "Low", "Medium", and "High" value customer tiers (K=3).
     - Image Compression: Reducing a complex color space to a specific palette of 8 or 16 primary colors.
