MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD, MAP, AND BAYESIAN ESTIMATION: THE UNIFYING VIEW
================================================================================

This is the note that ties the rest of the repo together. Squared error, log
loss, L2 and L1 penalties, and most of the "why this formula" questions in
Linear_Regression and Logistic_Regression all fall out of one principle.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE SETUP
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Assume the data came from a parametric family p(y | x, theta). We have i.i.d.
observations D = {(x_i, y_i)} and want theta.

Likelihood (a function of theta, NOT a probability distribution over theta):

    L(theta) = p(D | theta) = prod_i p(y_i | x_i, theta)

Probability answers "given theta, how likely is this data". Likelihood answers
"given this data, how well does each theta explain it". Same expression, read in
opposite directions. L(theta) does not integrate to 1 over theta, which is
exactly why you cannot treat it as a distribution without a prior.

Log-likelihood, because products of many small numbers underflow and sums
differentiate cleanly:

    l(theta) = sum_i log p(y_i | x_i, theta)

    theta_MLE = argmax_theta l(theta) = argmin_theta [ -l(theta) ]

That last equality is the bridge: NEGATIVE LOG-LIKELIHOOD IS A LOSS FUNCTION.
Every loss in loss_functions.txt that is a proper scoring rule is a negative
log-likelihood for some noise model.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE DERIVATIONS WORTH KNOWING COLD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 Gaussian noise -> squared error (linear regression)

    Assume  y_i = theta^T x_i + e_i ,  e_i ~ N(0, sigma^2)  i.i.d.
    So      p(y_i | x_i, theta) = (1/sqrt(2*pi*sigma^2)) * exp(-(y_i - theta^T x_i)^2 / (2*sigma^2))

    l(theta) = sum_i [ -0.5*log(2*pi*sigma^2) - (y_i - theta^T x_i)^2 / (2*sigma^2) ]
             = const - (1/(2*sigma^2)) * sum_i (y_i - theta^T x_i)^2

    Maximising l over theta = minimising sum_i (y_i - theta^T x_i)^2.

    OLS IS MLE UNDER GAUSSIAN NOISE. Nothing else. This is why the Gaussian-
    errors item sits in Linear_Regression/theory/assumptions.txt: it is not
    needed for OLS to be the best linear unbiased estimator (Gauss-Markov needs
    only zero-mean, homoscedastic, uncorrelated errors), but it IS what makes
    OLS the maximum likelihood estimator and what makes the usual t-tests,
    F-tests and confidence intervals exact rather than asymptotic.

    Also note sigma^2 dropped out of the argmin. The point estimate does not
    depend on the noise level; only the uncertainty around it does.

    MLE for the variance itself:
        d/d(sigma^2) l = 0  =>  sigma^2_MLE = (1/n) * sum_i r_i^2
    which divides by n, not by (n - p). So the MLE of the variance is BIASED
    downward; the unbiased estimator is the one with (n - p) in the denominator.
    A clean example of MLE being consistent but not unbiased.

2.2 Bernoulli -> log loss (logistic regression)

    Assume  y_i | x_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i) ,  p_i = sigmoid(theta^T x_i)
    Single-observation likelihood, the standard trick for writing a two-case
    expression as one product:

        p(y_i | x_i) = p_i^{y_i} * (1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}

    l(theta) = sum_i [ y_i*log(p_i) + (1-y_i)*log(1-p_i) ]

    Negating gives exactly binary cross-entropy. LOG LOSS IS THE BERNOULLI
    NEGATIVE LOG-LIKELIHOOD. Full gradient and Hessian in
    Logistic_Regression/theory/mathematical_derivation_binary.txt; multiclass
    (categorical / softmax) in the multiclass derivation file.

2.3 Laplace noise -> absolute error

    p(y|x) proportional to exp(-|y - theta^T x| / b)  =>  minimise sum |r_i|.
    So MAE is the MLE under Laplace (double-exponential) noise, whose heavier
    tails is precisely why it is robust: large residuals are less surprising
    under a Laplace model, so they exert less pull.

2.4 Poisson -> Poisson deviance (count targets)

    y | x ~ Poisson(lambda), lambda = exp(theta^T x)
    -l = sum_i [ exp(theta^T x_i) - y_i * theta^T x_i ] + const

    The log link is not arbitrary; it keeps lambda positive and makes the
    log-likelihood concave. This is the GLM pattern: pick a distribution from
    the exponential family, take its canonical link, and the loss writes itself.

2.5 The pattern

    Noise / target model     Negative log-likelihood        Common name
    ---------------------    ----------------------------   ----------------
    Gaussian                 squared error                  MSE / OLS
    Laplace                  absolute error                 MAE
    Bernoulli                -[y log p + (1-y) log(1-p)]    log loss
    Categorical              -log p_correct                 cross-entropy
    Poisson                  exp(z) - y*z                   Poisson deviance
    Gamma / Tweedie          deviance forms                 insurance losses

    Asked "why squared error?", the senior answer is: because I am assuming
    additive Gaussian noise, and if that assumption is wrong -- heavy tails,
    counts, skew -- the loss should change with it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. PROPERTIES OF THE MLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Under regularity conditions (correct model, identifiable, interior optimum):

    Consistency          theta_MLE -> theta_true in probability as n -> infinity.
    Asymptotic normality sqrt(n)(theta_MLE - theta_true) -> N(0, I(theta)^-1)
                         where I is the Fisher information (per observation).
    Efficiency           asymptotically attains the Cramer-Rao lower bound: no
                         consistent estimator has smaller asymptotic variance.
    Invariance           if theta_MLE maximises the likelihood, then g(theta_MLE)
                         is the MLE of g(theta), for any function g.

What it does NOT give you:

    Unbiasedness         MLEs are frequently biased in finite samples (see the
                         sigma^2 example above). Efficiency is asymptotic.
    Robustness           it is only optimal if the model is right. Under
                         misspecification it converges to the parameter that
                         minimises KL divergence to the truth (the
                         quasi-MLE) -- often still useful, but not "the truth".
    Existence            the maximum can fail to exist or sit at infinity. See
                         section 6.

Fisher information, the object behind all of it:

    I(theta) = -E[ d^2 l / d theta^2 ]      (expected negative Hessian)

    High curvature at the optimum = the likelihood falls away sharply = the data
    pin theta down tightly = low variance. The observed information (the actual
    Hessian at theta_MLE) is what implementations use, and its inverse diagonal
    gives the standard errors printed by statsmodels. It is also the matrix in
    Newton's method, which is why Newton / IRLS is the natural optimiser for
    GLMs -- the Hessian is a byproduct of the statistics, not an extra cost.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. MAP: WHERE REGULARISATION COMES FROM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Put a prior p(theta) on the parameters. Bayes:

    p(theta | D) = p(D | theta) * p(theta) / p(D)

    theta_MAP = argmax_theta [ log p(D|theta) + log p(theta) ]
              = argmin_theta [ -l(theta) - log p(theta) ]
                                            \-------------/
                                             the penalty term

4.1 Gaussian prior -> L2 / Ridge

    theta_j ~ N(0, tau^2)  =>  log p(theta) = -(1/(2*tau^2)) * ||theta||^2 + c

    Combined with Gaussian noise:
        minimise  sum_i (y_i - theta^T x_i)^2 + (sigma^2/tau^2) * ||theta||^2

    So lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2. Read that off carefully, it is a genuinely
    illuminating identity:
      - lambda large  <=>  tau small  <=>  a tight prior around zero, i.e. a
        strong prior belief that coefficients are small.
      - lambda -> 0   <=>  tau -> infinity <=> a flat prior <=> plain MLE/OLS.
      - lambda also grows with the noise level: noisier data justifies more
        shrinkage, which matches intuition and matches bias_variance.txt.

4.2 Laplace prior -> L1 / Lasso

    theta_j ~ Laplace(0, b)  =>  log p(theta) = -(1/b) * ||theta||_1 + c

    The Laplace density has a spike at zero and heavier tails than a Gaussian:
    it says "most coefficients are exactly irrelevant, but a few may be large".
    That spike is the probabilistic reason L1 produces exact zeros where L2 only
    shrinks. The geometric argument (corners of the L1 ball) and the soft-
    thresholding argument are in regularization_concept.txt; this is the third
    view of the same fact.

    Caveat worth stating: the LASSO estimate is the MAP under a Laplace prior,
    but the Laplace POSTERIOR MEAN is not sparse. Sparsity is an artefact of
    taking the mode, not a property of the Bayesian model.

4.3 What MAP loses

    MAP is a point estimate, so it throws away the posterior's shape and gives
    no uncertainty. It is also NOT invariant to reparameterisation -- the mode
    of a density moves when you change variables, because the Jacobian
    reweights the density, while the MLE is invariant. That is a real
    philosophical wart on MAP and a good interview curveball.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE FULL BAYESIAN TREATMENT (and why we usually skip it)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Instead of a point estimate, integrate:

    p(y* | x*, D) = integral p(y* | x*, theta) * p(theta | D) d theta

The posterior predictive averages over every theta weighted by its plausibility,
which propagates parameter uncertainty into predictions. Predictive intervals
are then honest rather than conditional-on-a-guess.

    MLE      : argmax p(D | theta)                     no prior
    MAP      : argmax p(D | theta) * p(theta)          prior, point estimate
    Bayesian : the whole p(theta | D), then integrate  prior, full uncertainty

As n grows the likelihood dominates any fixed prior, so all three converge --
which is the formal version of "regularisation matters most when data is
scarce". Conjugate cases (Gaussian-Gaussian, Beta-Bernoulli) are closed form;
otherwise you need MCMC or variational inference, and the cost is rarely worth
it for a large-n prediction problem. It pays off with small n, expensive
experiments, or when a decision needs a calibrated interval rather than a number.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHEN MLE BREAKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 Perfectly separable logistic regression
    If a hyperplane separates the classes, scaling theta by any c > 1 strictly
    increases the likelihood -- p_i -> 1 for every point and l -> 0 from below.
    The supremum is approached only as ||theta|| -> infinity, so no finite MLE
    exists. Symptoms: coefficients blowing up over iterations, giant standard
    errors, non-convergence warnings. Fix: any amount of L2, which supplies a
    finite MAP where no MLE exists. This is the sharpest practical example of
    regularisation rescuing estimation rather than merely improving it.
    Also happens with a rare category that perfectly predicts the outcome.

6.2 Unbounded likelihood in mixtures
    For a Gaussian mixture, letting one component collapse onto a single point
    sends its variance to zero and the likelihood to infinity. So the GMM
    likelihood has no global maximum. Fixes: variance floors, covariance
    regularisation, or a prior (i.e. MAP-EM). Relevant to Clustering/GMM.

6.3 Latent variables -> EM
    When some variables are unobserved (cluster assignments, missing labels),
    the log-likelihood contains a log of a sum and does not separate.
    Expectation-Maximisation alternates:
        E step: compute responsibilities, the posterior over the latent
                variables given the current parameters.
        M step: maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood.
    Each iteration cannot decrease the likelihood, so it converges -- to a local
    optimum, hence the sensitivity to initialisation shared with k-means. In
    fact k-means is the limiting case of EM for a GMM with spherical, equal,
    vanishing covariances and hard assignments. See Clustering/KMeans and
    Clustering/GMM.

6.4 Misspecification
    If the true noise is heavy-tailed and you assume Gaussian, MLE happily
    returns the KL-closest Gaussian model, which can be dragged badly by
    outliers. The failure is silent. Diagnose with residual plots and QQ plots
    (Linear_Regression/theory/assumptions.txt), fix by changing the noise model
    (Huber/Laplace/t) rather than deleting inconvenient rows.

6.5 Small n, many parameters
    Asymptotic guarantees are asymptotic. With p comparable to n the MLE
    overfits, standard errors from the Hessian are unreliable, and you should
    be regularising and cross-validating rather than trusting the theory.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. GENERATIVE VS DISCRIMINATIVE, IN LIKELIHOOD TERMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Discriminative  models p(y | x) only. Logistic regression, SVM, trees, nets.
                    Fewer assumptions, usually lower asymptotic error.
    Generative      models the joint p(x, y) = p(x | y) * p(y), then applies
                    Bayes to classify. Naive Bayes, GDA, GMM.
                    Stronger assumptions, so more bias, but converges faster in
                    n and can handle missing features and generate samples.

Naive Bayes is MLE on the joint under a conditional-independence assumption;
logistic regression is MLE on the conditional with no assumption about p(x).
For the Gaussian case, GDA and logistic regression assume the same functional
form for p(y|x), so GDA is asymptotically worse if its Gaussian assumption is
wrong and better if it is right. See Naive_Bayes/concept.txt.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. THE THIRTY-SECOND ANSWER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the observed data most
probable under an assumed noise model, and because we minimise the negative log
of it, every standard loss function is a likelihood in disguise: Gaussian noise
gives squared error, Bernoulli gives log loss, Laplace gives absolute error,
Poisson gives the Poisson deviance. Adding a prior turns MLE into MAP, and the
prior is the regulariser -- a Gaussian prior is L2 with lambda = sigma^2/tau^2,
a Laplace prior is L1 and its spike at zero is why L1 gives exact zeros. MLE is
consistent, asymptotically normal and efficient, but not unbiased in finite
samples, and it can fail outright -- there is no finite MLE for separable
logistic regression, which is the cleanest argument for always carrying a little
L2. Full Bayes goes one step further and integrates over the posterior instead
of picking a point, which buys honest uncertainty at a computational cost that
usually only pays off when data is scarce.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. INTERVIEW Q&A
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Derive the squared-error loss from first principles.
A: Assume y = theta^T x + e with e ~ N(0, sigma^2) i.i.d. The log-likelihood of
the data is a constant minus (1/(2 sigma^2)) times the sum of squared residuals,
so maximising the likelihood over theta is identical to minimising the sum of
squared residuals, and sigma^2 cancels out of the argmin entirely. So least
squares is not a convention, it is the maximum likelihood estimator under
additive Gaussian noise -- and if the noise is not Gaussian, that is the signal
to change the loss.

Q: Likelihood versus probability?
A: Same function p(D | theta), read along different axes. Fix theta and vary the
data and it is a probability distribution that integrates to one. Fix the data
and vary theta and it is a likelihood, which is not a distribution over theta
and does not integrate to one -- so "the probability that theta equals 0.3" is
meaningless in a purely likelihood framework. Getting a distribution over theta
requires a prior, and that is the step from MLE to Bayes.

Q: Is the MLE unbiased?
A: Not in general. It is consistent and asymptotically unbiased, but the finite-
sample bias is often non-zero. The standard example is the Gaussian variance:
the MLE divides the sum of squared residuals by n, whereas the unbiased
estimator divides by n minus the number of estimated parameters. MLE optimises
likelihood, not bias, and as bias_variance.txt argues, unbiasedness was never
the goal anyway -- low expected error is.

Q: Show that L2 regularisation is a Gaussian prior.
A: Take a zero-mean Gaussian prior on each coefficient with variance tau^2. Its
log density is minus the squared L2 norm over 2 tau^2, plus a constant. Adding
that to the Gaussian log-likelihood and negating gives the residual sum of
squares plus (sigma^2 / tau^2) times the squared L2 norm, so ridge is exactly
MAP estimation and lambda equals sigma^2 over tau^2. The identity is
interpretable: a tighter prior or noisier data both mean more shrinkage, and a
flat prior recovers OLS.
Follow-up: so what prior gives L1? A Laplace prior -- its density has a spike at
the origin, which is the probabilistic reason the mode lands exactly on zero for
weak features.

Q: Why do you not regularise the intercept?
A: Because the prior story only makes sense for the slopes. Shrinking the
intercept toward zero encodes a belief that the target's mean is near zero,
which is an arbitrary statement about the units you happened to measure y in --
shift y by a constant and the penalty changes the fit. Penalising only the
slopes keeps the estimator equivariant to shifts in y and to centring of x.

Q: Logistic regression fails to converge and the coefficients keep growing.
Why?
A: The classes are almost certainly linearly separable, possibly because of one
feature or a rare category that perfectly predicts the label -- or because of
leakage. Under separation the likelihood increases monotonically as you scale
the weight vector up, so the supremum sits at infinity and no finite MLE exists.
Add L2, and a finite MAP appears immediately. Before doing that, though, check
for a leaked feature, because perfect separation on real data is more often a
pipeline bug than a lucky dataset (data_leakage.txt).

Q: When is a full Bayesian treatment actually worth it over MAP?
A: When the posterior is wide relative to the effect you care about, so a point
estimate hides the thing that matters: small n, expensive or few experiments,
hierarchical structure with little data per group, sequential decisions under
uncertainty like bandits and Bayesian optimisation, or any setting where a
calibrated interval is the deliverable. With large n the likelihood swamps the
prior and the posterior concentrates, so MAP and MLE and the posterior mean all
agree, and paying for MCMC buys you nothing.

Q: What breaks if your assumed noise distribution is wrong?
A: MLE does not error out -- it converges to the parameter value minimising KL
divergence from the truth to your assumed family, the quasi-MLE. Point
predictions may still be reasonable, but everything downstream that relies on
the distributional assumption becomes wrong: standard errors, p-values,
prediction intervals, and any calibration claim. Practically, assuming Gaussian
noise on heavy-tailed data gives a fit dominated by a handful of extreme points.
Diagnose with residual and QQ plots; fix by choosing a noise model that matches
the tails.

Q: How does EM relate to maximum likelihood?
A: EM is a way to do maximum likelihood when some variables are latent, so the
log-likelihood contains a log of a sum and cannot be optimised directly. It
builds a lower bound on the log-likelihood using the posterior over the latent
variables (E step) and maximises that bound (M step); since the bound touches
the true objective at the current parameters, each iteration cannot decrease the
likelihood. It converges to a local optimum only, which is why GMM and k-means
both need careful initialisation and multiple restarts.
