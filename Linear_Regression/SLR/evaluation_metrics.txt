REGRESSION EVALUATION METRICS
==============================

1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MAE = (1/n) * Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|

Meaning:
    - Measures the average absolute difference between actual and predicted values.
    - Tells you “on average, how far off” the predictions are.
    - Easy to interpret — same units as the target variable.
    - More robust to outliers compared to MSE (no squaring of errors).

When to use:
    - When all errors should be treated equally.
    - When you want a simple, intuitive average error.
    - When your data may contain outliers.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Delivery time prediction (Zomato, Swiggy)
      → “On average, we are off by 6 minutes.”

    - Taxi fare prediction (Uber, Ola)
      → Users care about absolute difference, not squared difference.

    - House price estimation for customers
      → A ₹50,000 error is meaningful; squaring exaggerates errors.

    - Forecasting demand in retail (Amazon, Flipkart)
      → Outliers (holiday spikes) shouldn’t dominate.

2. Mean Squared Error (MSE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MSE = (1/n) * Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²

Meaning:
    - Measures the average of squared differences between actual and predicted values.
    - Penalizes large errors heavily (because errors are squared).
    - Useful when large deviations are especially harmful.

Characteristics:
    - Units are squared → not directly interpretable.
    - More sensitive to outliers than MAE.

When to use:
    - When large mistakes must be penalized strongly.
    - Financial forecasting, risk estimation, anomaly detection.


Practical Scenarios:
    - Loan default prediction (banks)
      → Large prediction errors are extremely costly.

    - Insurance claim cost prediction
      → Underestimating big losses is dangerous.

    - Sensor calibration in manufacturing
      → Large deviations can break machines.

    - Algorithmic trading / risk modeling
      → Large forecasting errors can cause major financial loss.

3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    RMSE = √MSE

Meaning:
    - Square root of MSE → brings metric back to the original units.
    - Represents average magnitude of prediction error.
    - Sensitive to large errors, but easier to interpret than MSE.

When to use:
    - When you want interpretability like MAE, but still want to penalize large errors more.
    - Balanced choice for most regression tasks.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Energy consumption forecasting (electricity usage)
      → RMSE tells “typical error” in actual units (kWh).

    - Weather forecasting (temperature, rainfall)
      → Large errors matter more; but units must remain interpretable.

    - Traffic speed prediction / congestion forecasting
      → RMSE in km/h helps understand typical deviation.

    - Ad-click or impression forecasting (Google Ads, Meta Ads)
      → Large deviations break budgeting.


4. R² Score (Coefficient of Determination)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    R² = 1 − (RSS / TSS)

Meaning:
    - Measures how much of the variance in y is explained by the model.
    - R² = 1 → perfect prediction
    - R² = 0 → model does no better than predicting the mean
    - R² < 0 → model is worse than mean prediction

When to use:
    - To understand how much variation in y is captured by the model.
    - For comparing models with the same dependent variable.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Academic/statistical modeling
      → Measure explanatory power of a regression (econometrics).

    - Marketing ROI models (regression analysis)
      → Understand how much variation in sales is explained by ad spend.

    - Medical studies predicting blood pressure, BMI, etc.
      → How well do features explain the target?

    - Business dashboards where interpretability matters
      → R² shows "model quality" at a glance.


5. Adjusted R²
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    Adjusted R² = 1 − [(1 − R²) * (n − 1) / (n − p − 1)]

Where:
    n = number of data points
    p = number of predictors (features)

Meaning:
    - Adjusts R² for number of predictors.
    - Penalizes adding irrelevant features.
    - Only increases if the new feature genuinely improves model performance.

When to use:
    - Comparing models with different numbers of features.
    - Avoiding overfitting when adding more predictors.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Feature selection in ML pipelines
      → Helps detect if adding new features actually improves the model.

    - Economics models with many correlated variables
      → Avoids falsely high R² from adding useless predictors.

    - Marketing mix modeling (MMM)
      → Many candidate features (spend, impressions, seasonality).

    - Real estate pricing model (location, amenities, size)
      → Prefer simpler models unless new predictors add real value.
