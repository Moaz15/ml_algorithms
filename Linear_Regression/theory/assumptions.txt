ASSUMPTIONS OF LINEAR REGRESSION
================================

WHY THESE ASSUMPTIONS EXIST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Gauss-Markov: if assumptions 1-5 hold, OLS is BLUE — the minimum-variance
estimator among all linear unbiased ones. Assumption 6 (normality) is NOT part of
that; it only buys exact small-sample inference.

The distinction that matters more than the list itself:

    Break exogeneity (2) or linearity (1)  ->  the COEFFICIENTS are biased.
                                               The model is confidently wrong.
                                               More data does not help.

    Break independence (3) or             ->  the coefficients stay UNBIASED;
    homoscedasticity (4)                      only the STANDARD ERRORS break.
                                               Predictions are fine, p-values
                                               and confidence intervals are not.

Treating all six as equally important is the most common mistake. They are not.


1. LINEARITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The relationship is linear IN THE PARAMETERS. Curved relationships are fine as
long as you supply the right columns (x², log x); a straight line through curved
data is not.

    Crop yield vs rainfall. More rain means more crops — up to a point. Past
    that the field floods and yield collapses. Force a straight line through it
    and the model predicts that endless rain gives infinite crops.

Breaks: bias. The model is systematically wrong, worst at the extremes.
Test: residuals vs fitted — a curve or U-shape means non-linearity. Ramsey RESET.
Fix: add polynomial terms (PLR/concept.txt), transform the feature, or use a
model that does not assume a functional form.


2. ZERO CONDITIONAL MEAN / EXOGENEITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
E[ε | X] = 0 — errors average to zero at every level of the predictors. In plain
terms: no hidden factor drives both a predictor and the target.

    Income predicted from years of education. Natural ability and family
    connections push people toward BOTH more education AND higher pay. Ability
    is not in the model, so it sits in the error term correlated with education,
    and the education coefficient absorbs its effect.

Breaks: bias — the worst violation there is. No amount of extra data fixes it;
more data just makes you more confident in a wrong number.
Test: not directly testable. It is a domain-reasoning question about what you
left out.
Fix: include the confounder, use instrumental variables, or state explicitly that
the model is for prediction only and its coefficients are not causal.

This is the assumption separating "my model predicts well" from "this coefficient
is the effect of X on y". A model can have excellent R² and badly biased
coefficients at the same time. See MLR/concept.txt section 3.


3. INDEPENDENCE OF ERRORS (NO AUTOCORRELATION)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One error should tell you nothing about the next. Usually an issue in time-series
and spatial data.

    Daily ice cream sales. A week-long heatwave hits: Monday's prediction is off
    by +200, and because weather persists, Tuesday's is too. The errors are
    chained together by something the model cannot see.

Breaks: coefficients stay unbiased, but standard errors are wrong — usually too
small, so you end up overconfident.
Test: Durbin-Watson (≈2 means no autocorrelation), ACF/PACF plots, or simply
residuals plotted against time or row order.
Fix: add lag features (yesterday's sales), use time-series models, or use
autocorrelation-robust (Newey-West) standard errors.


4. HOMOSCEDASTICITY (CONSTANT ERROR VARIANCE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The spread of errors should be roughly the same across all predictions, not
fanning out as predictions grow.

    Weekly food budget from income. For students the model predicts $50 and the
    actual lands between $45 and $55. For millionaires it predicts $500, and the
    actual is anywhere from $50 (fast food) to $5,000 (caviar). The error spread
    explodes with income.

Breaks: coefficients stay unbiased, standard errors are wrong, so tests and
confidence intervals are invalid. The model is also far less reliable at the high
end than its overall metrics suggest.
Test: residuals vs fitted — a funnel shape. Scale-location plot. Breusch-Pagan or
White test.
Fix: model log(y) instead of y, use Weighted Least Squares, or keep OLS and
report robust (Huber-White) standard errors.


5. NO PERFECT MULTICOLLINEARITY (MLR AND PLR ONLY)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Meaningless in SLR — it is a relationship BETWEEN predictors, and SLR has one.

Two severities, and the difference gets tested:

  PERFECT — one predictor is an exact linear combination of others (temperature
  in both Celsius and Fahrenheit; all k one-hot levels plus an intercept). XᵀX is
  singular and OLS has no unique solution. The model does not fit at all. Fix:
  drop the redundant column.

  HIGH but imperfect — predictors strongly correlated (house area and bedroom
  count). OLS runs and stays unbiased, but coefficients become unstable and hard
  to interpret individually. Predictions are essentially unaffected.

Test: correlation matrix, VIF (above 5, strictly above 10, is a flag).
Fix: drop or merge features, ridge, or PCA.
Symptoms and worked examples: MLR/concept.txt section 6. Math: derivation Part 5.


6. NORMALITY OF RESIDUALS (INFERENCE ONLY)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The residuals should be roughly bell-shaped, with large errors rare and balanced
in both directions. Note this is about the RESIDUALS — never about the
distribution of X or y.

    Pizza delivery time. Usually off by a minute or two. Drivers never arrive 30
    minutes early — cooking takes time — but they do arrive 45 minutes late with
    a flat tyre. The errors are strongly right-skewed.

Breaks: nothing about the coefficients — they stay unbiased and BLUE. Only
small-sample confidence intervals and p-values become unreliable. At large n the
CLT makes them approximately valid anyway.
Test: Q-Q plot of residuals, Shapiro-Wilk.
Fix: transform the target, or investigate the outliers producing the skew.


7. THE DIAGNOSTIC PLOTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Four plots cover almost everything above. Read them in this order:

  Residuals vs fitted — the workhorse. Should be a shapeless band around zero.
      A curve       -> linearity broken (1)
      A funnel      -> heteroscedasticity (4)
      Isolated dots -> outliers (section 8)

  Scale-location (√|standardized residual| vs fitted) — the same funnel check
  with the sign removed, so a flat line means constant variance. Easier to read
  than the funnel when the data is dense.

  Q-Q plot — residual quantiles vs normal quantiles. On the line means normal;
  the ends curling away means fat tails or skew. Only matters for inference (6).

  Residuals vs leverage — surfaces influential points (section 8).

For MLR, add residuals vs EACH feature: the fitted-value plot says something is
wrong, and this says which feature is wrong.


8. OUTLIERS, LEVERAGE AND INFLUENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Three different things that get used interchangeably and should not be:

  OUTLIER    — unusual in y. Large residual; the model predicts it badly.
  LEVERAGE   — unusual in x. Far from the centre of the feature cloud. Measured
               by hᵢᵢ from the hat matrix (derivation Part 4). High leverage
               means the point has the POTENTIAL to move the fit.
  INFLUENCE  — actually moves the fit. Roughly outlier × leverage.

    In a house price dataset: a normal-sized house that sold for triple its
    market value is an OUTLIER. A 20,000 sqft mansion priced exactly as the trend
    predicts is high LEVERAGE but harmless. A 20,000 sqft mansion sold at a
    family discount is INFLUENTIAL — it is far out on x and off the line, so it
    single-handedly tilts the whole fit.

High leverage alone is not a problem. High leverage plus a large residual is.

Measure: Cook's distance combines both. A common rule of thumb flags D > 4/n.
Drop a flagged point, refit, and see how much the coefficients move — if the
answer changes materially, one row is driving your conclusion.

In MLR a point can be unremarkable on every individual feature and still be
extreme in combination (5 bedrooms in 600 sqft), which no single-feature check
catches. This is why leverage exists as a separate measure.

Do not delete points automatically. Investigate first: a data entry error should
be fixed or dropped; a genuine rare case may be the most informative row you
have, and dropping it is quietly reshaping your conclusion.


SUMMARY TABLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Assumption            BLUE?  If violated              How to test
--------------------  -----  -----------------------  ---------------------------
1 Linearity           Yes    BIAS                     Residuals vs fitted; RESET
2 Exogeneity          Yes    BIAS (worst)             Not testable; domain sense
3 Independence        Yes    Wrong standard errors    Durbin-Watson; ACF
4 Homoscedasticity    Yes    Wrong standard errors    Funnel; Breusch-Pagan
5 No perfect multicol Yes    Model cannot fit at all  Correlation matrix; VIF
6 Normality           No     Bad small-sample tests   Q-Q plot; Shapiro-Wilk

Not assumptions, though often stated as such:
  - X or y must be normally distributed. False — only residuals.
  - Residuals must have mean zero. True by construction with an intercept, not a
    testable assumption (derivation Part 2).
  - Features must be uncorrelated with each other. Only PERFECT correlation is
    disallowed; high correlation is a quality problem, not a violation.
