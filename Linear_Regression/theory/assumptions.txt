Assumptions of Linear Regression 
======================================================================

0. THE GAUSS-MARKOV THEOREM & "BLUE" — why these assumptions exist
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Before listing the assumptions individually, it helps to know *why* they matter.
The Gauss-Markov theorem says: if a specific subset of assumptions holds, the
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimator is BLUE — the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator.

Breaking down BLUE:
* Linear   — the estimator is a linear function of the target values.
* Unbiased — on average, across repeated samples, the estimated coefficients equal
  the true population coefficients (no systematic over/under-estimation).
* Best     — among all linear, unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest possible
  variance (it is the most efficient / most precise one).

The key interview insight: BLUE is a statement about the *coefficients*, not about
predictions, and not about statistical inference (p-values, confidence intervals).
- Assumptions 1-5 below (Linearity, Zero Conditional Mean, Independence,
  No Perfect Multicollinearity, Homoscedasticity) are the actual Gauss-Markov
  conditions — violating them threatens whether OLS is unbiased and/or efficient.
- Assumption 6 (Normality of Residuals) is NOT required for BLUE. It is only
  needed to make exact hypothesis tests and confidence intervals valid in small
  samples. In large samples, the Central Limit Theorem makes OLS coefficients
  approximately normal even if the residuals aren't, so this assumption matters
  less as data grows.

This distinction is the single most common thing candidates get wrong: they treat
all assumptions as equally important, when in fact violating exogeneity (bias) is
categorically worse than violating normality (just unreliable inference).


1. LINEARITY (Correct Model Specification)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
What it means:
The relationship between the predictors and the target outcome follows a
straight-line pattern (it doesn't curve, dip, or wave).

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "Crop Yield" based on "Rainfall".
* Up to a certain point, more rain equals more crops (a positive linear relationship).
* But if it rains too much, the field floods and the crop yield drops to zero.
* If you force a straight line through this data, your model will wrongly predict
  that endless rain equals infinite crops.

Impact & Fix:
Your predictions will be systematically wrong in the extremes.
Fix it by transforming the variable (e.g., squaring the rainfall variable to allow
the model to learn a curve).


2. ZERO CONDITIONAL MEAN / EXOGENEITY (No Omitted-Variable Bias)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
What it means:
Formally, E[ε | X] = 0 — the error term should average out to zero at every level
of the predictors, and should be uncorrelated with them. In plain terms: there is
no hidden factor that affects both a predictor and the target, which the model
doesn't know about.

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "Income" using "Years of Education".
* People with more "natural ability" or family connections tend to both pursue
  more education AND earn more, independent of what the education itself teaches.
* "Ability" is not in the model, but it's baked into the error term, and it's
  correlated with "Years of Education".
* The model now over-attributes ability's effect to education, inflating the
  education coefficient.

Impact & Fix:
This is more serious than the other assumptions: violating it makes the
coefficient estimates BIASED, not just imprecise or hard to test. No amount of
extra data fixes this — more data just gives you a more confident wrong answer.
Fix it by identifying and including the confounding variable, using techniques
like instrumental variables, or being explicit that the model is for prediction
only (not causal interpretation) when the omitted variable can't be measured.

Why this matters for interviews:
This is the assumption that separates "my model predicts well" from "this
coefficient tells you the causal effect of X on y." A model can have a great R^2
while still having badly biased, non-causal coefficients.


3. INDEPENDENCE OF ERRORS (No Autocorrelation)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
What it means:
The model's mistakes (errors) must not follow a predictable sequence. One error
should not give you a clue about the next error. This usually applies to
time-based data.

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "Daily Ice Cream Sales" based on the day of the week.
* Suppose there is an unexpected week-long heatwave.
* On Monday, your model predicts 100 sales, but you get 300 (Error: +200).
* Because weather persists, Tuesday will likely also have a huge positive error.
* Your model's mistakes are linked together by an unseen variable (the heatwave).

Impact & Fix:
The model misses obvious trends and patterns over time, and standard errors
become unreliable (usually understated), making you overconfident in your
coefficients.
Fix it by adding a time-delay feature (like "Yesterday's Sales") or using
time-series specific forecasting models.


4. HOMOSCEDASTICITY (Constant Variance of Errors)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
What it means:
The size of your model's mistakes should be roughly the same across all
predictions. The errors shouldn't fan out or shrink as the predicted numbers get
larger.

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "Weekly Food Budget" based on "Income".
* For low-income college students, the budget is tight. The model predicts $50,
  and the actual is usually $45 to $55. The errors are very small.
* For multi-millionaires, the model predicts $500. But one millionaire might eat
  cheap fast food ($50), while another eats truffles and caviar ($5,000).
* The variance (spread) of the errors gets massively wider as income increases.
  This is heteroscedasticity.

Impact & Fix:
The model is very confident and accurate for low values, but highly unreliable
for high values. Coefficients remain unbiased, but their standard errors are
wrong, so hypothesis tests and confidence intervals become invalid.
Fix it by predicting the logarithm of the food budget instead of the raw dollar
amount, which compresses those massive variations, or use Weighted Least Squares
/ robust (Huber-White) standard errors.


5. NO PERFECT MULTICOLLINEARITY (Predictors Must Be Independent) — MLR only
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Note: this assumption is meaningless for SLR, since there is only one predictor.
It only applies once you have 2+ predictors, i.e. in MLR.

There are two distinct severities, and interviewers often test whether you know
the difference:

(a) PERFECT multicollinearity — a hard requirement, not just "nice to have"
What it means:
One predictor is an exact linear combination of others (e.g., you include both
"temperature in Celsius" and "temperature in Fahrenheit"). The design matrix X is
no longer full rank.
Impact & Fix:
OLS literally cannot compute a unique solution — (X^T X) is not invertible. This
isn't a matter of degree; the model fails to fit at all. Fix by dropping the
redundant column.

(b) HIGH (imperfect) multicollinearity — a lesser, matter-of-degree issue
What it means:
Your input variables (features) are strongly, but not perfectly, correlated with
each other.

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "House Price" using two variables: "Square Footage" and
"Number of Bedrooms".
* These two are heavily linked. A 4,000 sq ft house almost certainly has more
  bedrooms than a 900 sq ft apartment.
* If the model sees the price jump by $100,000, it gets confused. Was it because
  the house is bigger? Or because it has an extra bedroom?
* The model can't assign credit properly.

Impact & Fix:
OLS still runs and remains unbiased, but the coefficients become highly unstable
(large variance) and hard to interpret individually. Adding or removing just one
house from your dataset might wildly flip the importance of "Bedrooms" vs
"Square Footage". Note: this does NOT hurt pure predictive accuracy, only
inference (see Q2 below).
Fix it by dropping one of the overlapping variables, or mathematically combining
them into a single feature (like "House Size Index").


6. NORMALITY OF RESIDUALS (needed for inference, not for BLUE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
What it means:
If you take all the mistakes your model made and put them in a chart, they
should form a normal bell curve. Most mistakes should be tiny, and huge mistakes
should be very rare and equally balanced between over-predicting and
under-predicting.

Real-Life Example:
Imagine predicting "Pizza Delivery Time".
* Most of the time, the model is off by just 1 or 2 minutes (peak of the bell curve).
* However, drivers rarely arrive 30 minutes early (impossible due to cooking time).
* But drivers DO sometimes arrive 45 minutes late (due to flat tires or traffic).
* The errors are completely lopsided (skewed). You have massive positive errors
  but no massive negative errors.

Impact & Fix:
The baseline predictions and coefficients are still unbiased (this is NOT a
Gauss-Markov condition), but any confidence intervals you generate ("We are 95%
sure your pizza will arrive in 20-30 mins") will be unreliable in small samples.
In large samples, the Central Limit Theorem bails you out.
Fix it by removing extreme outliers or applying a mathematical transformation to
the target variable.


SUMMARY TABLE
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Assumption                          | Required for BLUE?    | If violated, what breaks       | How to test it
-------------------------------------|------------------------|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------
1. Linearity                         | Yes                    | Bias (wrong functional form)   | Residuals vs Fitted plot (look for curve); Ramsey RESET test
2. Zero Conditional Mean/Exogeneity  | Yes                    | Bias (worst violation)         | Not directly testable; domain reasoning about omitted confounders
3. Independence of Errors            | Yes                    | Inefficiency, wrong std errors | Durbin-Watson test (~2 = no autocorrelation); ACF/PACF plots
4. Homoscedasticity                  | Yes                    | Inefficiency, wrong std errors | Residuals vs Fitted plot (funnel shape); Breusch-Pagan / White test
5. No Perfect Multicollinearity      | Yes (hard requirement) | Model can't even fit           | Correlation matrix; Variance Inflation Factor (VIF > 5-10 = flag)
6. Normality of Residuals            | No                     | Unreliable inference (small n only) | Q-Q plot of residuals; Shapiro-Wilk test

Top Interview Questions on Linear Regression Assumptions

Q1: Do the independent variables (features) need to be normally distributed in
linear regression?
Answer:
No, this is a very common trap in interviews. Linear regression makes no
assumptions about the distribution of the independent variables ($X$) or the
dependent variable ($y$) itself. The normality assumption applies strictly to the
*residuals* (the errors or the differences between the actual and predicted values).

Q2: Does multicollinearity reduce the predictive accuracy of your model?
Answer:
No. If your primary goal is purely prediction (forecasting a value on unseen
data), multicollinearity does not hurt the model's predictive power—as long as
the correlation between the variables remains the same in the new data.
Multicollinearity ruins *inference*. It makes the coefficients unstable and the
p-values unreliable, meaning you cannot accurately determine *how much* a
specific feature impacts the target.

Q3: How do you detect and fix multicollinearity?
Answer:
Detection: I would use a correlation matrix for a quick check (looking for
absolute values $> 0.8$) and calculate the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for
each feature. A VIF above 5 (or strictly above 10) indicates problematic
collinearity.
Fix: I can drop one of the correlated variables, combine them mathematically
(e.g., taking an average), apply dimensionality reduction like PCA, or use
regularization techniques like Ridge or Lasso regression which handle
collinearity natively.

Q4: What is Heteroscedasticity, and why is it a problem?
Answer:
Heteroscedasticity occurs when the variance of the residuals changes across
different levels of the predicted values (e.g., errors get larger as the
predicted value gets larger). It is a problem because it violates the assumption
of constant variance (homoscedasticity). As a result, the standard errors of the
coefficients become biased. This makes confidence intervals and hypothesis tests
(p-values) completely unreliable, leading you to potentially declare a variable
statistically significant when it isn't.

Q5: How can you correct for Heteroscedasticity?
Answer:
The most common first step is to apply a non-linear transformation to the target
variable, such as taking the log or square root. This often compresses the
spread of the data and stabilizes the variance. If that doesn't work or isn't
appropriate, I would use Weighted Least Squares (WLS) or calculate Robust
Standard Errors (like Huber-White standard errors) to adjust the inference
metrics.

Q6: What is the "Dummy Variable Trap" and how does it relate to these
assumptions?
Answer:
The dummy variable trap is a scenario that causes *perfect multicollinearity*.
It happens when you convert a categorical variable into one-hot encoded binary
columns and include all of them in the model. For example, if you have a
"Gender" feature with "Male" and "Female", keeping both columns means that if
Male is 0, Female is perfectly predictable as 1. To avoid this, you must always
drop one category to serve as the baseline reference.

Q7: If you plot your residuals against your predicted values and see a distinct
"U-shape" or curve, what does this mean?
Answer:
This indicates a violation of the **Linearity** assumption. A systematic curve
in the residuals means the model is failing to capture a non-linear relationship
in the data. To fix it, I would need to add polynomial terms (like $X^2$) to the
model, or apply transformations to the input features.

Q8: Your residuals are not normally distributed. Is your model entirely useless?
Answer:
Not entirely. By the Gauss-Markov theorem, linear regression estimates remain
unbiased and optimal (BLUE - Best Linear Unbiased Estimators) even if errors are
not normal, provided the other assumptions hold. However, you can no longer
trust the statistical tests. Your t-tests, F-tests, confidence intervals, and
p-values will be invalid, meaning you cannot confidently state whether your
coefficients are statistically significant.

Q9: When is the Independence of Errors assumption most likely to be violated?
Answer:
It is almost always violated in time-series data or spatial data. If you are
predicting daily stock prices, an external shock on Monday will affect the
errors on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday. This autocorrelation means the model
is ignoring a time-based pattern. We detect it using the Durbin-Watson test.

Q10: What does it mean for OLS to be "BLUE", and which assumptions are actually
required for it?
Answer:
BLUE stands for Best Linear Unbiased Estimator: among all estimators that are
linear functions of y and unbiased, OLS has the minimum variance. It requires
Linearity, Zero Conditional Mean (exogeneity), Independence of errors, No
Perfect Multicollinearity, and Homoscedasticity — the five Gauss-Markov
assumptions. Normality is notably NOT required for BLUE; it's only needed for
exact small-sample inference (t-tests, F-tests, confidence intervals).

Q11: Between heteroscedasticity and endogeneity (violating zero conditional
mean), which is the bigger problem, and why?
Answer:
Endogeneity is strictly worse. Heteroscedasticity keeps your coefficient
estimates unbiased — it only corrupts the standard errors, so your point
estimates are still centered on the truth, and there are known fixes (WLS,
robust standard errors). Endogeneity means E[ε|X] != 0, which directly biases
the coefficients themselves — the model is confidently wrong, not just
imprecisely right, and no amount of additional data fixes it without addressing
the omitted variable or confounding directly.

Q12: What's the difference between "perfect" and "high" multicollinearity, and
does either one apply to Simple Linear Regression (SLR)?
Answer:
Perfect multicollinearity means one predictor is an exact linear combination of
another (e.g., duplicate/redundant columns) — X^T X becomes singular and OLS
can't be solved at all. High (imperfect) multicollinearity means predictors are
strongly but not perfectly correlated — OLS still runs, but coefficients become
unstable and hard to interpret, though prediction accuracy is largely unaffected.
Neither applies to SLR, since multicollinearity is a relationship *between*
predictors, and SLR has only one.

Q13: Do these assumptions still matter if you're using Ridge/Lasso, or a
tree-based/non-parametric model instead of plain OLS?
Answer:
For Ridge and Lasso, linearity and the general model form are still assumed
(they're still linear-in-parameters models), but the estimator is no longer
BLUE by design: regularization deliberately introduces bias to reduce
variance, trading Gauss-Markov's "unbiased" property for lower test error.
Homoscedasticity and normality matter less because you typically care about
predictive performance, not p-values or confidence intervals on coefficients.
Multicollinearity is actually handled natively — Ridge shrinks correlated
coefficients together instead of letting them blow up.
For tree-based/non-parametric models (Random Forest, GBMs), none of these
assumptions apply: no linearity requirement, no homoscedasticity/normality
requirement on residuals, and they're naturally robust to multicollinearity
for prediction (though correlated features still distort feature-importance
interpretation, similar to unstable coefficients in OLS).
