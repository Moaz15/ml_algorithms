POLYNOMIAL LINEAR REGRESSION — CONCEPT & INTUITION
==================================================
Not a new algorithm: it is MLR run on columns manufactured from one feature.
Everything in ../MLR/concept.txt applies, usually in a more extreme form.
Math: ../mathematical_derivation.txt (X just has more columns).


1. THE PROBLEM IT SOLVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Fuel efficiency against driving speed: crawling at 20 kmph is inefficient, so is
flooring it at 140, and mileage peaks around 60.

Fit a straight line and you get a slope near zero and a terrible fit — the model
concludes speed does not affect mileage, the opposite of the truth. The
relationship is strong; it just is not a line, and more data will not help,
because the model cannot express a hump.

Other genuinely curved relationships: crop yield vs fertilizer (rises, plateaus,
then falls), house price vs area (the 4,000th sqft adds less than the 800th), car
resale value vs age (steep drop, then slow decline).


2. THE TRICK: MANUFACTURE COLUMNS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Build new features from the one you have — speed, speed², speed³ — and hand them
to ordinary MLR:

    mileage = β₀ + β₁·speed + β₂·speed²

With β₁ positive and β₂ negative, that is a downward parabola: the hump we
needed. Same normal equations, same metrics, same assumptions. Only the design
matrix got wider.

This is why it is still LINEAR regression: "linear" refers to the PARAMETERS, not
the shape of the curve. β₁ and β₂ enter as a plain weighted sum, so the problem
stays convex with a closed form. Something like mileage = β₀·speed^β₁ is
genuinely non-linear and has no closed form.

Degree p is the only real hyperparameter, and it is the whole game.


3. DEGREE IS THE BIAS-VARIANCE DIAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The cleanest illustration of bias-variance anywhere in ML. Thirty noisy readings
of mileage against speed:

    Degree 1  — a straight line. Misses the hump, wrong everywhere in the same
                systematic way. Refit on fresh data and you get the same bad
                line. High bias, low variance: UNDERFITTING.
    Degree 2  — the parabola. Captures the shape, ignores the noise. Correct.
    Degree 15 — passes through nearly every point. Training R² near 1 and
                useless: it has modelled the measurement noise. Refit on fresh
                data and the curve is wildly different. OVERFITTING.

The tell is the gap between training and test error. Plot both against degree:
training error falls forever, test error falls, bottoms out, then climbs. That
minimum is your degree.

This is the concrete version of the MLR point that adding columns can only reduce
training RSS — so picking a degree by training R² always picks the highest one
offered.


4. IT GOES NUMERICALLY WRONG FAST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    speed    speed²     speed³
     20        400       8,000
    140     19,600   2,744,000

Two problems at once. The scales differ by orders of magnitude, which wrecks
conditioning — gradient descent crawls or diverges, and even the closed form
loses precision. And over a positive range, speed, speed² and speed³ all rise
together, so their correlation is near 1: you have hand-built the worst possible
multicollinearity, with all the symptoms from MLR section 6 (huge coefficients of
opposite sign that cancel out, a fit that swings on one new data point).

Two cheap fixes:
  - Centre and scale x before expanding. Using (speed - 60) makes the powers of
    small numbers in both directions, which largely decorrelates them.
  - Use an orthogonal basis (Legendre, Chebyshev) instead of raw powers. Same
    fitted curve, uncorrelated columns, stable coefficients. This is what
    numpy.polynomial and R's poly() do by default.


5. RUNGE'S PHENOMENON — THE EDGES OSCILLATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Raise the degree and the curve does not wiggle uniformly. It stays tame in the
middle and goes berserk at the two ends.

    Fit 24 months of sales with a degree-12 polynomial. Months 6 to 18 look
    plausible. At the edges it invents a near-zero dip in February and a spike in
    November that nothing in the business explains.

Interior points are pinned down by data on both sides; edge points are not, so
the curve is free to swing. It is why "just raise the degree" fails, and why
splines exist — low-degree pieces joined together, so flexibility in one region
cannot cause thrashing in another.

Rule: past degree 3 or 4, ask whether you want a different model family instead.


6. EXTRAPOLATION IS CATASTROPHIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Fit quarterly revenue over 2020-2026 with a degree-4 polynomial: R² of 0.98
    inside the window. Extend it to 2028 and it predicts negative ₹400 crore, or
    four times the size of your market.

Outside the data range the highest-power term dominates and x⁴ grows without
limit. The curve has no notion of "revenue cannot be negative" — it only
continues the shape that fit the window.

A linear model extrapolates badly but sanely, at a constant slope. A polynomial
extrapolates to nonsense fast. Treat any prediction outside the observed range of
x as invalid, and say so when handing the model over.


7. FEATURE EXPLOSION WITH MULTIPLE INPUTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One feature at degree p gives p columns. Several features give C(d+p, p), because
a full expansion includes every cross term:

    2 features,  degree 2  ->     5 columns (x₁, x₂, x₁², x₂², x₁x₂)
    10 features, degree 3  ->   285 columns
    20 features, degree 3  -> 1,770 columns

With 500 rows and 285 columns you are near the d > n regime. The cross terms are
also the interactions from MLR section 8, so you get every pairwise and three-way
interaction whether you wanted them or not.

Expand only the one or two features you have reason to think are curved, rather
than calling PolynomialFeatures on the whole matrix.


8. CHOOSING THE DEGREE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Never by training error — it always favours the highest degree.

Use cross-validation: sweep degree 1 to ~10, record mean validation error for
each, plot, take the minimum. The curve is usually flat across two or three
degrees near the bottom; when it is, take the SIMPLEST degree in that flat
region. The difference is noise, and the lower degree extrapolates less
dangerously.

Supporting checks: adjusted R² (it penalizes added columns), the residual plot at
your chosen degree (leftover curvature means the degree is too low), and domain
sense — a quadratic for mileage-vs-speed has a story, an efficient cruising
speed. A degree-9 fit has no story. If you cannot say what the shape MEANS, be
suspicious of it.


9. THE STANDARD PAIRING: POLYNOMIAL + RIDGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The production recipe is not "find the perfect degree". Expand to a generous
degree, then regularize: fit degree 8 with ridge and the penalty shrinks the wild
high-order coefficients toward zero, keeping the curve smooth. You have swapped a
hard discrete choice for a continuous dial (λ) that CV can tune, and ridge is
exactly the fix for the ill-conditioning in section 4.

Standardize the expanded columns before penalizing, or speed³ is punished far
harder than speed just for being larger. Do not penalize the intercept.

When to leave polynomials behind:
  - Splines / GAMs — low-degree pieces joined at knots. Local flexibility, no
    Runge oscillation, saner edges. Usually the better answer for a genuinely
    wiggly relationship.
  - Trees — no curve fitting at all; they handle curvature and interactions
    automatically when you do not need an interpretable functional form.
  - A domain transform. log(x) for saturating returns, 1/x for rates, sqrt(x) for
    diminishing effects. One well-chosen transform beats a degree-6 fit and comes
    with a story.


10. WHEN TO USE IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Use it when the plot shows clear simple curvature (one bend, maybe two), there is
a reason to expect that shape (a physical optimum, saturation, diminishing
returns), and a low degree of 2 or 3 is enough.

Do not use it when the curve is wiggly (splines), when you must predict outside
the training range, when you have many features and no idea which are curved, or
when CV picks a high degree (6+) — that usually means the polynomial family is
wrong, not that you need more terms.

Summary: polynomial regression buys curvature for the price of manufactured
columns, and pays in collinearity, edge oscillation and catastrophic
extrapolation. Keep the degree low, centre and scale, regularize, choose by CV,
and never trust it off the edge of the data.
