SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION — CONCEPT & INTUITION
==============================================

1. THE PICTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One feature x, one continuous target y, n points on a scatter plot. Draw the line
that sits best among them:

    ŷ = m·x + c

"Best" means smallest total squared VERTICAL gap to the points. Everything else
is bookkeeping on that one sentence.

The line estimates the conditional mean: for a given x it predicts the AVERAGE y
at that x, not any individual point. That is why a low R² can still mean a
correct model applied to a noisy process.


2. WHY VERTICAL DISTANCES, NOT PERPENDICULAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The instinct is to minimize the perpendicular distance to the line. OLS does not.
The model is asymmetric: x is treated as known and fixed, and all the error is
assumed to sit in y. We predict y from x, so only the error in y counts.

Two consequences:
  - Measurement error in x is not handled, and biases the slope toward zero
    (attenuation bias).
  - Minimizing perpendicular distance is a different method — Total Least Squares
    — which is what you want when BOTH variables are noisy measurements.


3. READING THE COEFFICIENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Slope m: expected change in y per one-unit increase in x, carrying units. "The
slope is 0.8" is meaningless; "0.8 kg per extra cm of height" is a finding.

Intercept c: predicted y at x = 0, and often not interpretable — saying so is a
mark of maturity. x = 0 may be impossible (zero height) or far outside the data,
making the intercept an extrapolation. Centring x makes the intercept the
predicted y at the AVERAGE x, which usually is interpretable.

The line always passes through the centroid (x̄, ȳ). Useful anchor: the fit pivots
around the middle of the data, so points far out on the x-axis have the most
power to tilt it.


4. SLOPE VS CORRELATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    slope = correlation × (spread of y / spread of x)

  - Correlation is unitless and bounded in [-1, 1]. The slope is neither.
  - They share a sign but say different things. A tiny slope can sit with a
    correlation of 0.99 (tight but shallow); a steep slope with 0.2 (steep but
    scattered).
  - Correlation is symmetric; the slope is not. Regressing y on x and x on y give
    two different lines, coinciding only when correlation is exactly ±1.

Regression toward the mean falls out of this. Standardize both variables and the
slope IS the correlation, always ≤ 1 in magnitude — so a point one SD above
average in x is predicted less than one SD above average in y. Tall parents have
tall children, but on average less extreme than themselves. No biological force
needed, just imperfect correlation.

    This is the source of endless bad analysis: "we coached the worst-performing
    stores and they all improved" — the worst performers would have drifted back
    toward average anyway.

At d = 1 only, R² is literally the square of the correlation between x and y.


5. THE SCATTER PLOT IS THE DIAGNOSTIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLR is the one case where you can LOOK at the data and see everything: curvature,
outliers, changing spread, clusters, and whether a line was ever sensible. Use it
while you can — from MLR onward you are reduced to residual plots.

Two plots answer most questions:
  - y vs x with the fitted line — is a line the right shape at all?
  - residuals vs fitted — any pattern means something is missing. A curve means
    non-linearity; a funnel means non-constant variance.

images/ in this folder holds worked examples, e.g. linearity_test.png.


6. ANSCOMBE'S QUARTET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Four datasets sharing nearly every summary statistic — same means, variances,
correlation, fitted line and R² — that look completely different when plotted:

    1. A genuine linear relationship.
    2. A clean curve; a line is the wrong shape.
    3. A perfect line with one outlier dragging the fit off it.
    4. All points at one x value except one, which alone sets the slope.

Identical metrics do not mean identical models. Never accept an R² without
looking at the plot.


7. CORRELATION IS NOT CAUSATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A slope says "these move together", never "x causes y". Three alternatives:
reverse causation (y drives x), confounding (a third variable drives both — ice
cream sales and drownings, both driven by temperature), and coincidence.

With one predictor you cannot rule any of these out, because you cannot control
for what is not in the model. That limitation is exactly what motivates MLR.


8. WHEN SLR IS THE RIGHT TOOL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Use it when one dominant driver explains most of the variation, when you need an
explainable relationship more than the last percent of accuracy, or as a baseline
and exploration tool.

Do not use it when the plot is visibly curved (transform x, or go polynomial),
when the spread of y grows with x (transform y), when a few extreme points steer
the line, when you must predict outside the observed range of x, or when the
outcome is a category (that is logistic regression).


9. THE BRIDGE TO MLR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything above survives; only the geometry changes. Two things change in kind:
coefficients become PARTIAL effects ("the effect of x₁ holding x₂ fixed"), and
features can now be correlated with each other, which is the whole topic of
multicollinearity and has no SLR analogue.
