SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION — CONCEPT & INTUITION
==============================================
Derivation: ../mathematical_derivation.txt (PART 2).
Running example: predicting delivery TIME (minutes) from DISTANCE (km).


1. WHAT WE ARE ACTUALLY ASKING FOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two orders 5 km away take 26 and 34 minutes. Neither is wrong — traffic, rider
and kitchen queue differ. So "the time for a 5 km order" does not exist as one
number. What does exist is the AVERAGE over all 5 km orders:

    ŷ = E[y | x],  the conditional mean

Two consequences that prevent most misreadings:
  - The line is not trying to hit points. It sits at the centre of the cloud at
    every x.
  - A correct model can still miss individual orders badly, because the spread
    was never its to explain. Hence a low R² is not automatically a bad model.


2. WHY A STRAIGHT LINE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We need a rule turning any distance into a time. The simplest one that still
responds to x is a constant rate:

    ŷ = m·x + c        c = cost before you move at all (pickup, packing)
                       m = what each extra km adds (minutes per km)

Two knobs, both physically meaningful. It is the simplest thing that is not a
flat line: ŷ = c ignores distance, while a curve needs more knobs and more data
to pin them down.

This is a CHOICE, not a discovery. If the truth bends, a line cannot express it
no matter how much data you collect (section 6).


3. WHY VERTICAL DISTANCE, NOT PERPENDICULAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"Closest to the points" is ambiguous until you ask: closest in which direction?
The natural guess is perpendicular. OLS uses vertical instead.

The model is asymmetric — distance is given and treated as fixed, all the
uncertainty is in time. So the mistake that matters is "how far off was the
predicted TIME", a vertical gap. A perpendicular gap would mix in an error in
distance we already assumed away.

  - If x really IS noisy (a GPS distance that is itself an estimate), OLS does
    not account for it and the slope is biased toward zero — attenuation bias.
  - Minimizing perpendicular distance is a different, valid method (Total Least
    Squares), correct when both variables are noisy measurements.


4. READING THE TWO NUMBERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    predicted_time = 12 + 3.5 × distance_km

SLOPE — quote it with units. "3.5" means nothing; "3.5 minutes per km" is a
finding. It is a rate comparing orders, not a promise about one: 6 km orders
average 3.5 minutes longer than 5 km orders.

INTERCEPT — the prediction at x = 0. Here it is real: a 0 km order still needs
12 minutes of cooking and pickup. That is luck. Predict weight from height and
the intercept is the weight of a 0 cm person — x = 0 is outside anything the
data saw, so it is an extrapolation with no evidence behind it. Centre x
(subtract the mean) and the intercept becomes the predicted time at the AVERAGE
distance, which is interpretable again.

From the derivation: the line always passes through the centroid (x̄, ȳ). The fit
PIVOTS there, so a point far out on the x-axis has a long lever arm and far more
power to tilt it. That is leverage (../assumptions.txt section 8).


5. SLOPE VS CORRELATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    slope = correlation × (spread of y / spread of x)

    slope (m)        "1 km more, how many minutes?" Has units, unbounded.
    correlation (r)  "how tightly do points hug the line?" Unitless, in [-1, 1].

Different questions, so they disagree freely:
  - Tight but shallow — time rises 0.5 min/km, every point almost on the line.
    Small slope, r ≈ 0.98.
  - Steep but scattered — time rises 8 min/km with huge spread. Large slope,
    r ≈ 0.2.

A steeper line does not mean a stronger relationship. Correlation is also
symmetric (corr(x,y) = corr(y,x)) while regression is not: time-from-distance
and distance-from-time are two different lines.

REGRESSION TOWARD THE MEAN drops out of the same formula. Standardize both and
the slope IS r, at most 1 — so a point one SD above average in x is predicted
LESS than one SD above average in y.

    A chain coaches its 20 worst-performing stores; next quarter they improve.
    The coaching may have done nothing. Extreme results are part skill, part
    luck, and luck does not repeat — any extreme group drifts back toward the
    average on its own.

That blind spot is how training programmes and "hot streaks" get credited with
effects they never had. At d = 1 only, R² is literally r².


6. THE SCATTER PLOT IS THE DIAGNOSTIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLR is the one case where all the data fits in one picture, so curvature,
outliers, changing spread and clusters are directly visible. Use it while it
lasts — from MLR on you are down to residual plots.

    y vs x with the fitted line   was a line ever the right shape?
    residuals vs fitted           any pattern means something is missing.
                                  A curve  -> the relationship bends.
                                  A funnel -> spread grows with x.

    Our example bends: the first km through city traffic costs more minutes than
    the tenth on a highway. A line splits the difference, over-predicting short
    orders and under-predicting long ones — a U-shape in the residuals, and
    invisible in R² alone.

WHY YOU CANNOT SKIP THE PLOT — Anscombe's quartet: four datasets with nearly
identical means, variances, correlation, fitted line and R², that look nothing
alike plotted.

    1. A genuine linear relationship.
    2. A clean curve — a line is the wrong model.
    3. A perfect line with one outlier dragging the fit off it.
    4. All points at one x except a single far-out point, which alone sets the
       slope.

Same numbers, four different stories. Never accept an R² without the picture.
(images/ here holds worked examples, e.g. linearity_test.png.)


7. CORRELATION IS NOT CAUSATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A slope says only "these move together". Three rivals survive:

    Reverse causation  y drives x.
    Confounding        a third factor drives both — ice cream sales predict
                       drownings; temperature causes both.
    Coincidence        chance, especially after testing many variables.

Ruling them out means HOLDING THEM CONSTANT, and you cannot hold constant what
is not in the model. With one predictor you never can — which is exactly why MLR
exists (../MLR/concept.txt section 3). Even MLR only controls for what you
thought to include.


8. WHEN TO USE IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Use SLR when one dominant driver explains most of the variation, when
explainability beats the last percent of accuracy, or as a baseline.

Do not use it when:
    the plot is visibly curved         -> transform x, or go polynomial
    spread of y grows with x           -> transform y, or weighted least squares
    a few extreme points steer the fit -> investigate them first
    you must predict outside the observed x range
    the outcome is a category          -> logistic regression


9. THE BRIDGE TO MLR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything survives; the line becomes a hyperplane. Two things change in kind:

  - Coefficients become PARTIAL effects — "distance holding traffic fixed" — a
    different quantity from the SLR slope, not a bigger version of it.
  - Features can be correlated with EACH OTHER, which has no SLR analogue and is
    the whole topic of multicollinearity.
