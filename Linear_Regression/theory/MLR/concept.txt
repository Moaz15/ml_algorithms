MULTIPLE LINEAR REGRESSION — CONCEPT & INTUITION
================================================

1. WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Mechanically, nothing. The line becomes a hyperplane and the same normal
equations solve it. SLR is the d = 1 case.

Three things change in kind, and they are this whole file:

  a) Coefficients become PARTIAL effects — a different quantity from an SLR
     slope, not a bigger version of one.
  b) Features can be correlated with EACH OTHER. No SLR analogue, and the source
     of most practical trouble.
  c) You lose the scatter plot, so every diagnostic is now indirect.


2. THE PARTIAL EFFECT — "HOLDING EVERYTHING ELSE CONSTANT"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
βⱼ is the change in y for a one-unit increase in xⱼ WITH THE OTHER FEATURES HELD
FIXED. Dropping that qualifier is the most common interpretation error.

The consequence people miss: this is NOT the slope you would get regressing y on
xⱼ alone.

    SLR slope        -> the TOTAL association, including what xⱼ carries with it
                        through other variables
    MLR coefficient  -> what REMAINS after the other features take their share

So a coefficient can be large and positive alone, then small or negative once
other features join. Nothing broke — the question changed.

Mental model: the coefficient is fitted on the part of xⱼ the other features
cannot explain. If little is left over, it is estimated from almost no
information — which is section 6.

Say it as: "comparing houses identical in every other feature, one more sqft is
associated with ₹4,500 more." Associated with, not causes.


3. WHY YOU ADD FEATURES: CONFOUNDING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The reason to move past SLR is not accuracy. A one-feature model cannot separate
a real effect from a shared cause.

Leave out a variable that drives both x and y, and its influence is absorbed into
the coefficient on x — inflating it, shrinking it, or flipping its sign. That is
omitted-variable bias.

    Ice cream sales predict drownings. Add temperature and the ice cream
    coefficient collapses: temperature was doing the work all along.

Adding the confounder is what earns you the phrase "holding temperature fixed".

Simpson's paradox is the extreme version: a trend present in every subgroup
reverses when the groups are pooled.

    Treatment A beats Treatment B for small stones AND for large stones. But A is
    given mostly to severe cases, so pooled, B looks better — until stone size
    enters the model.


4. ADDING FEATURES IS NOT FREE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Training fit can only improve. Any column, even pure noise, reduces training
    RSS, so training R² never falls. Useless for deciding if a feature helped —
    use adjusted R² or a validation set.
  - Variance rises. More parameters from the same n rows means less precise
    coefficients and easier overfitting.
  - Correlated features destabilize each other (section 6).
  - Interpretation gets harder past a handful of features.

You can also add the WRONG feature. Controlling for a mediator — something on the
causal path from x to y — erases the effect you are measuring.

    Training raises skill, skill raises salary. Control for skill and the
    training coefficient vanishes. Concluding "training does not pay" is wrong.


5. CATEGORICAL FEATURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Categories must be encoded, and the encoding changes what coefficients mean.

One-hot: one binary column per level. The dummy trap is keeping all k levels
alongside an intercept — the columns then sum to the intercept column, XᵀX is
singular, the fit is not unique. Fix: drop one level, or drop the intercept.

With a level dropped, each coefficient is a difference RELATIVE TO that reference
level, not an absolute effect. With "Mumbai" as reference, a Pune coefficient of
-800,000 means Pune homes are ₹8 lakh cheaper than comparable Mumbai homes.
Change the reference and every coefficient changes; predictions do not.

Do not label-encode nominal categories as 1, 2, 3 — it asserts an ordering and
equal spacing that is not there ("Chennai is three times Delhi"). Fine only for
genuinely ordinal features.

High cardinality (say 5,000 pincodes) means many coefficients fitted on a few
rows each. Group rare levels, use target encoding, or use a model that handles
categories natively.


6. MULTICOLLINEARITY — THE MLR-SPECIFIC FAILURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Features correlated with each other. Formulas and fixes: derivation Part 5.

If two features move together, the data cannot tell their effects apart. An
increase in one coefficient offset by a decrease in the other gives nearly the
same predictions, so the fit has no strong preference between them. Running
example: area_sqft and number_of_rooms in a house model.

Four symptoms:

  1. Nonsense signs. Area gets a NEGATIVE coefficient — "bigger houses cost
     less" — purely to offset an inflated positive coefficient on rooms.
  2. Bouncing estimates. Drop 5 houses from 500 and area goes from dominant to
     irrelevant. The fit is on a tightrope.
  3. Good model, no significant features. High R², yet both area and rooms come
     out insignificant — because "area holding rooms fixed" has almost no data
     behind it (3,000 sqft houses with one room barely exist).
  4. One feature takes the credit. Rooms gets a large coefficient, area gets
     nearly zero, though both obviously matter.

The distinction worth remembering: multicollinearity damages the INDIVIDUAL
COEFFICIENTS, not the PREDICTIONS. Harmless if you only need forecasts, fatal if
you need to report per-feature effects. Diagnose with VIF, then decide based on
which of the two you actually need.


7. COEFFICIENT SIZE IS NOT IMPORTANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Coefficients carry units, so magnitudes are not comparable.

    price = 3,200,000 + 4,500×area_sqft - 250,000×distance_to_metro_km

Distance looks 55 times more important. But area varies by ~1,500 sqft across the
data, worth ₹67 lakh of swing; distance varies by ~8 km, worth ₹20 lakh. Area is
the bigger driver — the opposite of what the raw numbers suggested.

Worse, switch area to square metres and its coefficient jumps from 4,500 to
~48,000 on identical data with identical predictions. A number that moves when
you change units cannot measure importance.

Fix: standardize before fitting, so every coefficient answers "how much does
price move per one-standard-deviation increase?" Even then:

  - Precision differs. 0.6 ± 0.5 is a shrug; 0.2 ± 0.02 is a finding.
  - Correlated features split credit arbitrarily and can swap ranks on a refit.
  - A big per-unit effect on a rare feature explains little. "Has helipad" may
    add ₹50 lakh, but if 3 houses in 10,000 have one, it explains nothing.


8. INTERACTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain MLR assumes each feature's effect is constant regardless of the others.
Often false.

    A 10% discount lifts sales far more during the festival season than in a dead
    week. The effect of discount DEPENDS ON season.

An additive model must pick one average discount effect for December and March
alike, overstating one and understating the other, and no amount of data fixes
it. The fix is a product column:

    sales = β₀ + β₁·discount + β₂·festival + β₃·(discount × festival)

With β₁ = 400 and β₃ = 900, a point of discount buys 400 units normally and 1,300
during a festival. Still linear regression — the new column is just a column.
(Same shape: drug dose × body weight, ad spend × channel.)

Two catches. Main effects become conditional: β₁ now means "the effect of
discount when festival = 0", which is fine here but nonsense for something like
area × distance_to_metro, where it becomes "the value of a sqft at zero distance
from the metro". Centring the features first fixes that. And interactions
multiply — 10 features have 45 pairs — so add the ones you have a reason to
expect, not every combination.

The same trick handles curvature (add area²), which is ../PLR/concept.txt.


9. DIAGNOSTICS WITHOUT THE SCATTER PLOT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
You cannot plot y against twelve features, so you read residuals instead.

  - Residuals vs fitted — the general-purpose plot. Cheap houses predicted within
    ₹2 lakh while ₹2-crore houses are off by ₹40 lakh is a funnel: errors grow
    with the prediction. Predictions stay unbiased, but the standard errors are
    wrong. Common fix: model log(price), turning "off by ₹40 lakh" into "off by
    15%".
  - Residuals vs each feature — localizes it. A U-shape against age_of_property
    (under-pricing new AND heritage homes, over-pricing 20-year-olds) says the
    age effect is curved; add age².
  - Q-Q plot — only matters when quoting p-values. A long right tail from a few
    luxury sales makes inference unreliable while predictions stay fine.
  - Correlation matrix and VIF, BEFORE fitting. Seeing VIF 12 across area, rooms
    and bathrooms warns you not to trust those coefficients before you write a
    conclusion you must retract.
  - Leverage and Cook's distance. A listing with 5 bedrooms and 600 sqft is
    unremarkable on each feature alone but impossible in combination — no
    single-feature check catches it, and it can tilt the whole hyperplane.


10. WHEN MLR IS THE RIGHT TOOL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Use it when:
  - Drivers contribute roughly additively — electricity demand from temperature,
    day of week and holiday flags.
  - You must defend the model. A bank rejecting a loan has to say why; a boosted
    forest with the same accuracy may be unusable in that setting.
  - You need the effect, not the prediction — "what does a day of delivery delay
    cost in repeat purchases, holding order value fixed?" is a question about a
    coefficient.
  - You want a baseline. If the deep model beats it by 2%, ship the simple one.

Use something else when:
  - Effects are curved or heavily interacting — taxi fare from pickup and dropoff
    coordinates has no sensible additive effect of "latitude". Use trees.
  - Features are collinear and you need interpretation — marketing mix where TV,
    radio and digital all rise together. Ridge, drop channels, or experiment.
  - d approaches n — 20,000 genes, 200 patients. Regularization is mandatory.
  - The target is not continuous — "will this customer churn?" Linear regression
    will happily predict a 130% probability. Use logistic regression.
