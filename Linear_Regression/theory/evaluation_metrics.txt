REGRESSION EVALUATION METRICS
==============================

Quick Reference Table
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Metric              | Scale-Dependent | Outlier Sensitivity | Interpretability
---------------------|------------------|----------------------|-------------------------------
MAE                  | Yes              | Low                  | High (same units as target)
MSE                  | Yes (squared)    | High                 | Low (squared units)
RMSE                 | Yes              | High                 | High (same units as target)
MAPE                 | No (percentage)  | High (near y=0)      | High (intuitive %)
RMSLE                | Reduced          | Low (log-damped)     | Medium
Median Absolute Error| Yes              | Very Low (ignores)   | High
Huber Loss           | Yes              | Medium (tunable)     | Medium
R²                   | No               | High                 | High (0 to 1 scale)
Adjusted R²          | No               | High                 | High (penalizes extra features)


1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MAE = (1/n) * Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|

Meaning:
    - Measures the average absolute difference between actual and predicted values.
    - Tells you "on average, how far off" the predictions are.
    - Easy to interpret — same units as the target variable.
    - More robust to outliers compared to MSE (no squaring of errors).

When to use:
    - When all errors should be treated equally.
    - When you want a simple, intuitive average error.
    - When your data may contain outliers.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Delivery time prediction (Zomato, Swiggy)
      → "On average, we are off by 6 minutes."

    - Forecasting demand in retail (Amazon, Flipkart)
      → Outliers (holiday spikes) shouldn't dominate the reported error.


2. Mean Squared Error (MSE)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MSE = (1/n) * Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²

Meaning:
    - Measures the average of squared differences between actual and predicted values.
    - Penalizes large errors heavily (because errors are squared).
    - Useful when large deviations are especially harmful.

Characteristics:
    - Units are squared → not directly interpretable.
    - More sensitive to outliers than MAE.
    - Differentiable everywhere, which is why it's the default training loss for OLS/gradient descent
      (MAE has a non-differentiable point at zero).

When to use:
    - When large mistakes must be penalized strongly.
    - As a training objective (differentiability matters more than interpretability during optimization).

Practical Scenarios:
    - Loan default prediction (banks)
      → Large prediction errors are extremely costly.

    - Insurance claim cost prediction
      → Underestimating big losses is dangerous.


3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    RMSE = √MSE

Meaning:
    - Square root of MSE → brings metric back to the original units.
    - Represents average magnitude of prediction error.
    - Sensitive to large errors, but easier to interpret than MSE.

When to use:
    - When you want interpretability like MAE, but still want to penalize large errors more.
    - Balanced choice for most regression tasks, especially for reporting to non-technical stakeholders.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Energy consumption forecasting (electricity usage)
      → RMSE tells "typical error" in actual units (kWh).

    - Weather forecasting (temperature, rainfall)
      → Large errors matter more; but units must remain interpretable.


4. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MAPE = (100/n) * Σ |(yᵢ − ŷᵢ) / yᵢ|

Meaning:
    - Expresses error as a percentage of the actual value — scale-independent.
    - Lets you compare error across datasets/targets with different units or magnitudes.
    - Undefined or exploding when yᵢ is at or near zero.
    - Asymmetric: penalizes overprediction more heavily than underprediction for the same absolute error
      (because the denominator shrinks as ŷᵢ grows relative to a fixed yᵢ).

When to use:
    - When stakeholders think in percentages rather than absolute units.
    - When comparing forecast quality across products/regions with very different scales.
    - Avoid when the target can be zero or near-zero.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Sales forecasting across products with vastly different price points
      → "We're off by 8% on average" is comparable across a ₹200 item and a ₹20,000 item.

    - SaaS revenue forecasting for executive dashboards
      → Percentage error is more intuitive than absolute currency error.


5. Root Mean Squared Log Error (RMSLE)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    RMSLE = √[ (1/n) * Σ (log(1 + yᵢ) − log(1 + ŷᵢ))² ]

Meaning:
    - RMSE computed on log-transformed values — compresses large-magnitude differences.
    - Penalizes underprediction more than overprediction (opposite skew from MAPE).
    - Well-suited to targets spanning several orders of magnitude.

When to use:
    - When the target has exponential-like growth or wide dynamic range (counts, prices, population).
    - When underestimating is worse than overestimating.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Demand/inventory count prediction (Kaggle-style forecasting competitions)
      → Running out of stock (underprediction) is worse than overstocking.

    - Website traffic or view-count prediction
      → Errors on a video with 10M views shouldn't dominate errors on one with 100 views.


6. Median Absolute Error
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    MedAE = median( |yᵢ − ŷᵢ| )

Meaning:
    - Robust central-tendency version of MAE — a few extreme outliers cannot shift it at all.
    - Unlike MAE, which still averages in outlier contributions, MedAE ignores them entirely.

When to use:
    - When outliers are known data-quality issues, not signal, and should have zero influence.
    - As a sanity-check metric alongside MAE/RMSE to see how much outliers are inflating the mean-based metrics.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Sensor data with occasional glitches/misreads
      → A handful of corrupted readings shouldn't move the reported error at all.

    - Property price prediction with rare ultra-luxury outliers
      → MedAE reflects "typical" pricing error unaffected by a few mansions.


7. Huber Loss
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    L_δ(e) = 0.5 * e²                     if |e| ≤ δ
            δ * (|e| − 0.5 * δ)           if |e| > δ
    where e = yᵢ − ŷᵢ

Meaning:
    - Hybrid of MSE and MAE: quadratic (like MSE) for small errors, linear (like MAE) for large errors.
    - Differentiable everywhere (unlike MAE), while still being robust to outliers (unlike MSE).
    - δ is a tunable threshold controlling where the loss switches behavior.

When to use:
    - As a training loss when you want outlier robustness but still need a gradient-friendly objective.
    - Classic answer to "how would you make regression robust to outliers without losing differentiability."

Practical Scenarios:
    - Robust regression training pipelines where the training data has heavy tails (e.g. fraud amounts,
      real-world sensor noise) but gradient-based optimization is still required.


8. R² Score (Coefficient of Determination)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    R² = 1 − (RSS / TSS)

Meaning:
    - Measures how much of the variance in y is explained by the model.
    - R² = 1 → perfect prediction
    - R² = 0 → model does no better than predicting the mean
    - R² < 0 → model is worse than mean prediction

When to use:
    - To understand how much variation in y is captured by the model.
    - For comparing models with the same dependent variable and the same dataset.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Academic/statistical modeling
      → Measure explanatory power of a regression (econometrics).

    - Business dashboards where interpretability matters
      → R² shows "model quality" at a glance.


9. Adjusted R²
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Formula:
    Adjusted R² = 1 − [(1 − R²) * (n − 1) / (n − p − 1)]

Where:
    n = number of data points
    p = number of predictors (features)

Meaning:
    - Adjusts R² for number of predictors.
    - Penalizes adding irrelevant features.
    - Only increases if the new feature genuinely improves model performance.

When to use:
    - Comparing models with different numbers of features.
    - Avoiding overfitting when adding more predictors.

Practical Scenarios:
    - Feature selection in ML pipelines
      → Helps detect if adding new features actually improves the model.

    - Marketing mix modeling (MMM)
      → Many candidate features (spend, impressions, seasonality); prefer simpler models unless new
        predictors add real value.


Key Relationships & Caveats
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    - RMSE ≥ MAE always, with equality only when every error has the same absolute magnitude.
      The gap between them grows as errors become less uniform (more dominated by a few large errors).

    - Minimizing MSE is equivalent to Maximum Likelihood Estimation under the assumption that residuals
      are Gaussian-distributed. Minimizing MAE is equivalent to MLE under a Laplacian (double-exponential)
      noise assumption. This is why MSE is the natural loss for OLS and MAE the natural loss for
      quantile/median regression.

    - R² is not well-defined for models without an intercept (some libraries compute it differently in
      that case), can be artificially inflated by a single dominant/high-variance feature, and can go
      negative on out-of-sample data if the model performs worse than predicting the mean.

    - Adjusted R² only corrects for the number of predictors — it does not detect multicollinearity,
      non-linearity, or omitted-variable bias, so a high Adjusted R² is not proof of a well-specified model.

    - When RMSE is low but R² is also low, the target variable itself has low variance (errors are small
      in absolute terms, but so is the spread of y, so the model isn't explaining much beyond the mean).


Interview Q&A
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Q: Why would you report RMSE instead of MSE to a business stakeholder, but still use MSE as the training loss?
A: RMSE is in the same units as the target, so "we're off by ₹500 on average" is directly interpretable,
   while MSE's squared units aren't. During optimization, though, differentiability and smooth gradients
   matter more than interpretability, and MSE's gradient is simpler and better-behaved than RMSE's
   (RMSE's gradient involves an extra division by the RMSE value itself). In practice you train on MSE
   and report RMSE.

Q: MAE is robust to outliers, so why isn't it the default training loss for linear regression?
A: MAE's gradient is constant in magnitude and undefined at zero error, which makes gradient-based
   optimization less stable near the minimum and prevents the closed-form OLS solution (which relies on
   MSE being smooth and quadratic). Huber loss is the usual middle ground: quadratic near zero for
   stable gradients, linear for large errors for outlier robustness.

Q: When would R² be misleading, and does Adjusted R² fix it?
A: R² is misleading when a model has no intercept, when one high-variance feature dominates the fit
   without real predictive value, or when comparing across different datasets/targets. Adjusted R² only
   fixes one specific failure mode — it discourages adding features that don't improve fit — but it says
   nothing about non-linearity, multicollinearity, or whether the model generalizes out-of-sample.

Q: What's the probabilistic interpretation of MAE vs. MSE?
A: Minimizing MSE is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation assuming residuals are i.i.d. Gaussian.
   Minimizing MAE is equivalent to MLE assuming residuals follow a Laplacian distribution, whose heavier
   tails make it naturally more tolerant of large errors. This is also why the OLS estimator (MSE-minimizing)
   is the mean-predicting solution, while the MAE-minimizing solution predicts the median.

Q: Why does MAPE fail on some datasets, and what would you use instead?
A: MAPE divides by the actual value, so it blows up or is undefined when yᵢ is zero or very small, and it's
   asymmetric — it penalizes overprediction more than underprediction for symmetric absolute errors. When
   the target can be near zero, use a scale-independent alternative like SMAPE (symmetric MAPE) or just
   report RMSE/MAE alongside a normalized version (e.g. RMSE / mean(y)).

Q: Given a business scenario, how do you decide which metric to optimize for?
A: Start from what an error actually costs the business. If errors are equally bad in either direction and
   outliers are noise, use MAE. If large errors are disproportionately costly (e.g. financial risk), use
   MSE/RMSE. If stakeholders think in relative terms across differently-scaled targets, use MAPE. If
   underprediction is worse than overprediction and the target spans orders of magnitude (e.g. inventory,
   traffic), use RMSLE. Use R²/Adjusted R² separately as an explanatory-power diagnostic, not as the
   optimization target itself.
