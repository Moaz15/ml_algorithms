REGRESSION EVALUATION METRICS
=============================

QUICK REFERENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Metric        Scale-dependent  Outlier sensitivity  Units / reading
------------  ---------------  -------------------  -------------------------
MAE           Yes              Low                  target units
MSE           Yes (squared)    High                 squared units (train loss)
RMSE          Yes              High                 target units
MAPE          No (%)           High near y=0        percentage
RMSLE         Reduced          Low (log-damped)     relative, log scale
Median AE     Yes              Very low (ignores)   target units
Huber         Yes              Tunable via δ        target units (train loss)
R²            No               High                 0 to 1 (can go negative)
Adjusted R²   No               High                 0 to 1, penalizes features

Rule of thumb: TRAIN on MSE or Huber (differentiable), REPORT RMSE or MAE
(interpretable), DIAGNOSE with R² and residual plots.


1. MAE — MEAN ABSOLUTE ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    MAE = (1/n) Σ |yᵢ - ŷᵢ|

Average absolute miss, in target units. All errors weighted equally, so it is
robust to outliers. Not differentiable at zero, which is why it is rarely the
training loss.

    Delivery time prediction: "on average we are off by 6 minutes."
    Retail demand: holiday spikes should not dominate the reported error.

Use when errors are equally bad in both directions and outliers are noise.


2. MSE — MEAN SQUARED ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    MSE = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ - ŷᵢ)²

Squaring punishes large errors disproportionately: being off by 10 is 100 times
worse than being off by 1, not 10 times. Units are squared, so it does not report
well — but it is smooth and convex, which is why it is the default training loss
and the basis of the closed form.

    Insurance claim cost: underestimating a huge loss is far worse than being
    slightly off on many small ones.

Use as the training objective; use RMSE to report it.


3. RMSE — ROOT MEAN SQUARED ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    RMSE = √MSE

MSE brought back to target units. Keeps the large-error penalty while staying
readable, which makes it the usual default for reporting.

    Energy forecasting: "typical error is 40 kWh."

RMSE ≥ MAE always, with equality only when every error has the same magnitude.
The GAP between them is informative: a large gap means a few big errors dominate,
so go look at them.


4. MAPE — MEAN ABSOLUTE PERCENTAGE ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    MAPE = (100/n) Σ |(yᵢ - ŷᵢ)/yᵢ|

Scale-free, so it compares across targets of wildly different magnitude.

    Sales forecasting: "off by 8%" is comparable across a ₹200 item and a
    ₹20,000 item, where an absolute error is not.

Two failure modes: it explodes or is undefined when yᵢ is at or near zero, and it
is asymmetric — over-prediction is penalized harder than under-prediction for the
same absolute error. When the target can be near zero, use SMAPE, or report
RMSE alongside RMSE/mean(y).


5. RMSLE — ROOT MEAN SQUARED LOG ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    RMSLE = √[ (1/n) Σ (log(1 + yᵢ) - log(1 + ŷᵢ))² ]

RMSE on log values, so it measures RELATIVE error and compresses large
magnitudes. Penalizes under-prediction more than over-prediction.

    Inventory demand: stocking out (under-predicting) costs more than
    overstocking.
    View-count prediction: errors on a 10M-view video should not swamp errors on
    a 100-view one.

Use when the target spans orders of magnitude, or when under-shooting is worse.


6. MEDIAN ABSOLUTE ERROR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    MedAE = median(|yᵢ - ŷᵢ|)

Outliers cannot move it at all — MAE still averages their contribution, MedAE
ignores them entirely.

    Property prices: MedAE is the typical pricing error, unaffected by a few
    mansions.

Best used ALONGSIDE MAE or RMSE: the size of the gap tells you how much the
mean-based metrics are being inflated by a handful of points.


7. HUBER LOSS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    L_δ(e) = ½e²                if |e| ≤ δ
             δ(|e| - ½δ)        if |e| > δ

Quadratic near zero (smooth gradients like MSE), linear in the tails (outlier
robustness like MAE), differentiable everywhere. δ sets the switch point.

The standard answer to "how do you make regression robust to outliers without
losing differentiability."


8. R² — COEFFICIENT OF DETERMINATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    R² = 1 - RSS/TSS

    RSS = Σ(yᵢ - ŷᵢ)²      what the model failed to explain
    TSS = Σ(yᵢ - ȳ)²       total variation to explain

    R² = 1    perfect fit
    R² = 0    no better than always predicting the mean ȳ
    R² < 0    WORSE than predicting the mean (possible on test data)

The baseline is the crucial part: R² measures you against "just predict the
average", not against zero. And it is a RELATIVE measure, so it depends on the
variance of y. Low RMSE with low R² simply means y barely varies — your errors
are small, but so is the thing you are explaining.

Why it needs an intercept. R² as "share of variance explained" rests on
TSS = ESS + RSS, which holds only because residuals are orthogonal to fitted
values — and that orthogonality comes from having an intercept column
(derivation Part 4). Without one the decomposition fails, R² can be negative or
meaningless, and libraries compute it inconsistently.

The trap that matters most: TRAINING R² NEVER DECREASES WHEN YOU ADD A FEATURE.
Adding a column can only enlarge the column space, so RSS can only fall — even
for a column of pure random noise. Training R² therefore always prefers the
bigger model and is useless for deciding whether a feature helped. Two
consequences: never select features on training R², and never quote R² without
saying whether it is train or test.


9. ADJUSTED R²
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Adjusted R² = 1 - [ (1 - R²)(n - 1) / (n - p - 1) ]      p = features

Same idea as R², but it charges you for each feature. It rises only if the new
feature earns more than its cost in degrees of freedom, and it can FALL when you
add a useless one — which is exactly what plain R² cannot do.

    Marketing mix modelling: dozens of candidate spend, impression and
    seasonality features. Adjusted R² is what tells you the 30th feature is not
    paying for itself.

Use it to compare models with different feature counts on the same data. But it
only corrects for COUNT — it says nothing about multicollinearity, non-linearity
or omitted-variable bias, so a high adjusted R² is not proof of a good model.


10. TRAIN/TEST SPLIT AND CROSS-VALIDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every metric above is meaningless on training data alone, for the reason in
section 8: a flexible enough model drives training error to zero. What matters is
error on data the model has not seen.

Holdout split. Fit on 70-80%, evaluate on the rest. Simple and fast, but the
score depends on which rows happened to land in the test set — noisy for small
datasets.

k-fold cross-validation. Split into k folds (5 or 10 typically), train on k-1 and
test on the held-out fold, rotate, and average the k scores. Every row is used
for both training and testing, so the estimate is far more stable, and the spread
across folds tells you how sensitive the model is to the sample. Cost is k fits.

    This is how you choose a polynomial degree (PLR/concept.txt section 8) or a
    ridge λ: sweep the hyperparameter, take the CV error at each value, pick the
    minimum.

Two rules that get violated constantly:

  - Fit preprocessing on the TRAINING fold only. Computing the mean and standard
    deviation for standardization on the full dataset leaks test information into
    training, and your reported score comes out optimistic. Use a pipeline.
  - Respect structure in the data. For time series, never shuffle — train on the
    past and test on the future (rolling/expanding window), or you are predicting
    Monday using Tuesday's data. For grouped data (multiple rows per customer),
    split by GROUP, or the same customer appears on both sides.

Report the metric with its context: "test RMSE 40 kWh, 5-fold CV, σ 3.2" says
something. "RMSE 40" does not.


CHOOSING A METRIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Start from what an error actually costs:

    Errors equally bad both ways, outliers are noise      -> MAE
    Large errors disproportionately costly               -> RMSE
    Outliers are noise and must have zero influence      -> Median AE
    Comparing across differently-scaled targets          -> MAPE (if y ≠ 0)
    Target spans orders of magnitude, under-shooting bad -> RMSLE
    Need a robust TRAINING loss                          -> Huber
    Explanatory power, feature-count comparison          -> R² / Adjusted R²

Two closing facts worth carrying:

  - Minimizing MSE is MLE under Gaussian errors; minimizing MAE is MLE under
    Laplace errors. That is why MSE gives the conditional MEAN and MAE the
    conditional MEDIAN (derivation Part 1).
  - Never optimize R². It is a diagnostic of explanatory power, not a loss. Pick
    the metric that matches the business cost, then report R² alongside it.
