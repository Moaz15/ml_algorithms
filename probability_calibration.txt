PROBABILITY CALIBRATION: MEASUREMENT, CAUSES, FIXES, AND INTERVIEW Q&A
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT CALIBRATION MEANS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A model is calibrated if its stated probabilities match observed frequencies:

    P(y = 1 | p_hat = q) = q      for all q in [0, 1]

Plain English: of all the cases where the model said 0.7, about 70% should
actually be positive. That is it.

Two independent properties of a classifier, and conflating them is the mistake
this whole note exists to prevent:

    DISCRIMINATION   can the model separate positives from negatives? A
                     property of the RANKING. Measured by AUC/ROC, PR-AUC,
                     Gini. Invariant to any monotone transformation of the
                     scores.
    CALIBRATION      are the numbers themselves believable as probabilities? A
                     property of the SCALE. Measured by reliability diagrams,
                     ECE, the Brier score's reliability term.

They are genuinely independent:
    - Take a perfectly calibrated model and square its outputs: AUC is
      unchanged (squaring is monotone), calibration is destroyed.
    - A model that outputs the base rate 0.05 for everyone is perfectly
      calibrated and completely useless: AUC 0.5.

So "my AUC is 0.85, so my probabilities are fine" is wrong, and it is a
frequent interview trap. AUC cannot see calibration by construction.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHY IT MATTERS (and when it does not)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Calibration matters whenever the NUMBER is consumed, not just the order:

    Expected-value decisions   expected loss = p * amount. A fraud system that
                               blocks when p*amount > cost needs p to mean
                               something. Doubling all probabilities changes
                               every decision while leaving AUC identical.
    Cost-sensitive thresholds  the optimal threshold under a cost matrix is
                               t* = C_fp / (C_fp + C_fn) -- a statement about
                               probabilities. Meaningless if p is not a
                               probability.
    Aggregation                "we expect 340 churners next month" is
                               sum(p_i). Miscalibration makes forecasts wrong
                               even when the ranking is perfect.
    Combining models           stacking, ensembling and any Bayesian
                               combination weight components by their stated
                               confidence.
    Downstream ML              a probability feeding another model or a
                               bandit/RL policy propagates its distortion.
    Human trust                telling a doctor "82% chance of malignancy" is a
                               commitment. Overconfident models erode trust
                               permanently once clinicians notice.

Calibration does NOT matter much when only the ranking is used and the threshold
is chosen empirically: top-k retrieval, a fixed review queue capacity, lead
prioritisation. There, spend your effort on discrimination.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. MEASURING IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 Reliability diagram (calibration curve) -- always look at this first
    Bin predictions (equal-width, or equal-count/quantile bins which are better
    behaved under imbalance). Plot mean predicted probability per bin on x
    against observed positive fraction on y. Perfect calibration is the
    diagonal.

    Reading the shapes:
        below the diagonal   over-confident (predicts higher than reality)
        above the diagonal   under-confident
        sigmoid shape        classic over-confidence at both extremes; typical
                             of boosted trees and SVMs pushed toward 0 and 1
        inverse-sigmoid      predictions bunched toward the middle; typical of
                             bagged models and heavily regularised models
    Always plot the histogram of predictions underneath. A bin containing 4
    points is noise, and equal-width bins under imbalance leave most bins nearly
    empty while 95% of the mass sits in the first bin.

3.2 Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
    ECE = sum_b (n_b / n) * | acc(b) - conf(b) |
    A single number, and a fragile one: it depends on the binning scheme and the
    number of bins, is biased downward with few bins, and can be gamed. Report
    it with the bin count and scheme, and never rely on it alone. MCE (the max
    over bins) is even noisier.

3.3 Brier score and its decomposition
    Brier = (1/n) * sum (p_i - y_i)^2 -- a strictly proper scoring rule
    (loss_functions.txt section 6), so it rewards honest probabilities.

    Murphy decomposition:
        Brier = reliability - resolution + uncertainty
            reliability   calibration error   (lower better)
            resolution    how much predictions vary across outcomes
                          (higher better -- this is discrimination)
            uncertainty   base-rate variance p*(1-p) -- irreducible
    This is the clean way to say that a proper score measures BOTH properties at
    once, and why a good Brier score cannot distinguish "well calibrated but
    uninformative" from "informative but miscalibrated" unless you decompose it.

3.4 Log loss
    Also strictly proper, and far more sensitive to confident mistakes than
    Brier because it is unbounded. Good for optimisation, harsh as a diagnostic:
    a single p = 0.001 on a positive can dominate the average.

3.5 Calibration slope and intercept
    Regress the true labels on the logits: fit logistic regression of y on
    logit(p_hat), giving y ~ sigmoid(a + b*logit(p_hat)).
        b = 1, a = 0   calibrated
        b < 1          over-confident (spread too wide) -- the usual case
        b > 1          under-confident
        a != 0         base-rate shift / systematic bias
    Two interpretable numbers, no binning required. Underused; the clinical
    prediction literature treats this as the standard report.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHO IS MISCALIBRATED, AND WHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Model                    Typical pattern       Mechanism
    ----------------------   -------------------   -------------------------
    Logistic regression      well calibrated       trained on a strictly proper
    (unregularised, correct                        loss whose minimiser IS the
     link, no resampling)                          probability; the fitted
                                                   score-equations force the
                                                   predicted positives to match
                                                   the observed count
    Logistic regression      shrunk toward base    the penalty biases weights
    (strong L1/L2)           rate (under-conf.)    toward zero
    Naive Bayes              extreme, near 0/1     conditional-independence
                                                   assumption double-counts
                                                   correlated evidence, so
                                                   log-odds pile up
    SVM                      sigmoid distortion    hinge loss is not a proper
                                                   scoring rule; the output is
                                                   a margin, not a probability
    Boosted trees            over-confident,       the objective keeps pushing
    (AdaBoost, GBM)          pushed to extremes    margins outward past the
                                                   point of correct classification
    Random forest            under-confident,      averaging many trees pulls
                             pulled to the middle  extremes toward the mean; a
                                                   vote fraction is not a
                                                   probability, though it is
                                                   often closer than most
    Deep networks            strongly over-conf.   capacity to drive training
                                                   NLL to zero; modern nets fit
                                                   labels perfectly and keep
                                                   sharpening confidence
    Any model + resampling   base-rate shifted     see section 6
    or class weights

Key asymmetry worth stating: logistic regression is calibrated because of the
loss it minimises, not because it is linear. Any model trained to convergence on
log loss without regularisation, resampling, or capacity to memorise has a
reason to be calibrated on its training distribution -- and each of those three
caveats is exactly what breaks it in practice.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE FIXES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All post-hoc methods learn a map g: score -> probability on data the model did
NOT train on. Fitting a calibrator on training predictions calibrates the
memorised scores and does nothing for new data -- that is leakage
(data_leakage.txt).

5.1 Platt scaling (sigmoid calibration)
    Fit a one-dimensional logistic regression on the scores:
        p = sigmoid(a * s + b)              two parameters, fitted by MLE
    - Parametric, so it works with a few hundred calibration points.
    - Strictly monotone, so the ranking and therefore AUC are exactly preserved.
    - Assumes the distortion has a sigmoid shape, which is a good match for
      SVMs and boosted trees and a bad match for arbitrary shapes.
    - Platt's original recipe also regularises the targets slightly (using
      (N+1)/(N+2) instead of 1) to avoid infinite parameters under separation.
    - Beta calibration is a three-parameter generalisation that fixes the
      sigmoid's inability to be identity-shaped near the boundaries.

5.2 Isotonic regression
    Fit the best non-decreasing step function mapping score to probability
    (pool-adjacent-violators algorithm).
    - Non-parametric: can correct any monotone distortion, including shapes a
      sigmoid cannot express.
    - Needs much more data -- roughly a thousand-plus calibration points, more
      under imbalance -- and overfits happily on less.
    - Piecewise constant, so it creates ties. That flattens the ranking within
      each step and CAN slightly reduce AUC, unlike Platt.
    - Cannot extrapolate: it clamps outside the observed score range, and it
      will happily output exactly 0 or 1, which is fatal under log loss. Clip.

5.3 Temperature scaling (the deep-learning default)
    Divide the logits by a single scalar T > 0 before the softmax:
        p = softmax(z / T)
    Fit T on a validation set by minimising NLL. T > 1 softens.
    - One parameter, so it barely overfits.
    - The softmax argmax is unchanged, so accuracy is EXACTLY preserved -- the
      property that made it the standard choice for neural networks.
    - It cannot fix class-specific or input-dependent miscalibration, since it
      applies one global scale. Vector/matrix scaling and Dirichlet calibration
      relax that at the cost of more parameters.

5.4 Choosing

    Situation                                  Method
    ----------------------------------------   --------------------------
    Small calibration set (< ~1000)            Platt / beta
    Large calibration set, unknown shape       Isotonic
    Neural network, multiclass                 Temperature scaling
    Must not change accuracy at all            Temperature scaling
    Must not change ranking at all             Platt (strictly monotone)
    Multiclass, non-neural                     one-vs-rest then renormalise,
                                               or Dirichlet calibration

5.5 Where the calibration data comes from
    - Dedicated hold-out: simple, but the calibrator is fitted on less data and
      the model on less than the full set.
    - Cross-validated calibration (what sklearn's CalibratedClassifierCV does
      with cv=k): out-of-fold predictions calibrate, and the result is an
      ensemble of k calibrated models. Uses all the data, costs k fits.
    - Out-of-fold predictions you already have from cross_validation.txt are
      exactly the right input, and free if you kept them.

5.6 A note on fixing it at the source
    Post-hoc calibration treats a symptom. Sometimes the cause is fixable:
    reduce regularisation, stop resampling and use a threshold instead, add
    label smoothing or weight decay for a network, use log loss rather than
    exponential loss for boosting, or reconsider naive Bayes' independence
    assumption. Post-hoc is usually cheaper and more reliable, but it is worth
    knowing which knob created the problem.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. IMBALANCE, RESAMPLING, AND PRIOR CORRECTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

If you train on a resampled set, the model learns the RESAMPLED base rate, so
every probability is systematically inflated. Undersample negatives 100:1 and a
true 1% risk comes out near 50%. Ranking is unaffected; the scale is wrong.

Correct analytically, given the training prior pi_train and the true prior
pi_true (equivalently, adjust the intercept of a logistic model):

    odds_corrected = odds_model * (pi_true / (1 - pi_true)) * ((1 - pi_train) / pi_train)

    p_corrected = odds_corrected / (1 + odds_corrected)

Class weights cause the same shift for the same reason. The cleanest practical
policy: do not resample or reweight at all, train on the natural distribution
with a proper loss, and move the decision THRESHOLD to reflect costs. You keep
calibration for free and you can change the operating point without retraining.
See handling_imbalanced_data.txt.

Also, under heavy imbalance the reliability diagram needs quantile bins and a
log-scaled x axis, otherwise everything interesting is compressed into the
leftmost bin.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. CALIBRATION IN PRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Calibration is a property of a model AND a distribution, so it decays under
drift even when discrimination holds up. A model whose AUC is stable but whose
predicted positive count has drifted away from the observed count is telling you
the base rate moved (prior shift), which is the cheapest kind of drift to fix:
recalibrate, do not retrain.

Monitor, on a rolling window:
    mean predicted probability versus observed positive rate
    calibration slope and intercept
    ECE with a fixed binning scheme so the numbers are comparable over time
    the prediction histogram

Watch out for a feedback trap: if the model's decisions change who gets labelled
(blocked transactions never produce a chargeback, rejected loans never default),
the observed rate is censored and naive recalibration bakes in the policy.
See ML_Lifecycle/theory/12_monitoring_and_drift.txt.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. THE THIRTY-SECOND ANSWER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A model is calibrated when things it calls 70% likely happen about 70% of the
time. It is a property of the scale of the scores, entirely separate from
discrimination, which is a property of their ranking -- so AUC is blind to
calibration by construction, since any monotone transformation leaves it
unchanged. It matters whenever the number is consumed rather than just ordered:
expected-value decisions, cost-based thresholds, forecast totals, stacking. You
diagnose it with a reliability diagram plus the calibration slope, and summarise
with a proper score like Brier, whose reliability term isolates the calibration
part. Logistic regression is calibrated because log loss is a strictly proper
scoring rule; SVMs are not, because hinge loss is not proper; boosted trees and
deep networks are over-confident; bagged models are under-confident; and any
resampling or class weighting shifts the base rate. The fixes are post-hoc maps
fitted on held-out data: Platt scaling for small samples and exact rank
preservation, isotonic for large samples and arbitrary monotone distortions,
temperature scaling for neural networks because it preserves accuracy exactly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. INTERVIEW Q&A
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: My model has 0.92 AUC. Are the probabilities trustworthy?
A: AUC says nothing about it. AUC depends only on the ordering of the scores, so
it is invariant to any monotone transformation -- I can square every probability
or pass them through any increasing function and AUC will not move, while
calibration is destroyed. To answer the question I would plot a reliability
diagram with quantile bins, fit the calibration slope and intercept by
regressing the labels on the logits, and check whether the sum of predicted
probabilities matches the observed positive count. A high AUC with a calibration
slope of 0.6 is a common and perfectly consistent result.

Q: Why is logistic regression usually calibrated while an SVM is not?
A: Logistic regression is fitted by maximising the Bernoulli likelihood, and log
loss is a strictly proper scoring rule, so its population minimiser is the true
conditional probability -- the model is being asked for a probability and
optimally answers with one. In fact the score equations force the sum of
predicted probabilities to equal the observed count of positives, so it is
calibrated in-sample by construction. An SVM minimises hinge loss, which is not
proper and is exactly zero for any point beyond the margin, so the output is a
distance from a boundary with no probabilistic meaning; turning it into a
probability requires a separate calibration step, which is what Platt scaling
was invented for.
Follow-up: what breaks logistic regression's calibration? Strong regularisation,
resampling or class weights, and a misspecified link or missing interactions.

Q: Platt scaling or isotonic regression?
A: Data size and the shape of the distortion. Platt fits two parameters, so it
works on a few hundred calibration points and is strictly monotone, meaning the
ranking and AUC are exactly preserved -- but it can only express a sigmoid, so
it will not fix an odd-shaped curve. Isotonic is non-parametric and fits any
monotone distortion, but it needs on the order of a thousand or more points,
overfits below that, and produces a step function that creates ties and can
therefore nudge AUC down slightly. It also clamps at the ends and can emit exact
zeros and ones, which is fatal under log loss, so I clip. For neural networks I
would use temperature scaling instead, since one parameter suffices and accuracy
is provably untouched.

Q: Where do you get the data to fit the calibrator?
A: Never from the training predictions -- the model has memorised those to some
degree, so the calibrator would learn to correct a distortion that does not
exist on fresh data, and the result is worse than doing nothing. Either hold out
a dedicated calibration split, or use cross-validated calibration where
out-of-fold predictions fit the calibrator, which uses all the data at the cost
of k fits. If I already ran cross-validation, the stored out-of-fold predictions
are exactly the right input and cost nothing extra.

Q: You undersampled negatives 50:1 to train. Your model says 0.6 for a case you
believe is roughly 2% risk. Explain and fix.
A: The model learned the resampled base rate, not the real one, so every
probability is inflated while the ranking is untouched. The fix is a prior
correction on the odds: multiply the model's odds by the true prior odds and
divide by the training prior odds, then convert back to a probability -- for a
logistic model this is just an intercept shift. Better still, I would question
the undersampling: training on the natural distribution with log loss and then
moving the decision threshold gets the same operating flexibility while keeping
calibration for free, and it lets me change the threshold without retraining.

Q: Random forests and gradient boosting are both tree ensembles. Why do they
miscalibrate in opposite directions?
A: Because of how the ensemble is formed. A random forest averages many
independently grown trees; averaging pulls values toward the middle, so the
extremes get compressed and the model is under-confident -- a vote fraction of
0.9 typically corresponds to a higher true rate than 0.9. Gradient boosting
fits stages sequentially to reduce a margin-based loss, which keeps pushing
scores away from the boundary even after the points are correctly classified, so
predictions pile up near 0 and 1 and the model is over-confident. That opposite
distortion is visible as an inverse-sigmoid reliability curve for the forest and
a sigmoid one for the boosted model.

Q: Does calibration improve accuracy?
A: Not by itself, and this is worth being precise about. Platt scaling and
temperature scaling are monotone, so the ranking is unchanged and accuracy at a
FIXED probability threshold like 0.5 can change only because the numbers moved
across that threshold -- which is a real and often large improvement in
practice, but it comes from relocating the operating point, not from better
discrimination. If instead you had already tuned the threshold optimally on the
raw scores, calibration cannot improve accuracy at all. What it improves is
every decision that depends on the value of p: expected-cost decisions, volume
forecasts, and downstream models.

Q: Your model's AUC is flat over three months but the predicted positive rate
has drifted from 3% to 7% while the observed rate stayed at 3%. Diagnosis?
A: Stable discrimination with broken calibration -- the ranking still works, so
the features have not lost their relationship to the target, but the scale has
shifted. The usual cause is covariate shift moving the feature distribution
without changing the conditional relationship, or an upstream pipeline change
altering a feature's units or null rate. Since the observed rate is unchanged,
this is not a genuine base-rate move. I would recalibrate on recent labelled
data rather than retrain, verify feature distributions against the training
window to find the upstream change, and check that label collection has not
become censored by the model's own decisions.
