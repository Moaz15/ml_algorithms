5. REGULARIZATION AND PRUNING

5.1 Why a fully grown tree memorizes noise
------------------------------------------

What "fully grown" means

    No constraints at all. The tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure - one class, often a
    single row. Training accuracy is 100 percent by construction, which is exactly why it tells you
    nothing.

WHY THAT IS A PROBLEM

    Noise splits too    :  a tree cannot tell signal from noise. It only sees "this split lowers
                           impurity", and a coincidence in the training set lowers impurity just as
                           well as a real pattern does.

    Leaves get tiny     :  near the bottom a leaf may hold two or three rows. Its prediction is an
                           estimate from three samples, reported with total confidence.

    The boundary frays  :  every noise split adds another rectangle, so a few clean regions become a
                           jagged patchwork traced around individual training points.

    Variance explodes   :  those splits are pinned to particular rows, so resampling the data moves
                           them. See 2.6.

WATCHING IT HAPPEN

     max_depth    leaves   train acc   test acc
             2         4       0.785      0.751
             3         8       0.809      0.762
             5        25       0.855      0.800   <- best on unseen data
             8        71       0.934      0.776
            12       124       0.989      0.747
          none       140       1.000      0.749

    Train accuracy climbs all the way to 1.000 and never once warns you. Test accuracy peaks at
    depth 5 and then falls back to where depth 2 was - the last 115 leaves were bought with noise.

> Real-world: this is why "my model gets 100 percent on training data" is a red flag rather than a
  result. For a tree it is not even an achievement - an unconstrained tree gets 100 percent on any
  dataset where no two identical rows carry different labels.

> Likely follow-up: "Can a fully grown tree ever be the right answer?"
  Inside an ensemble, yes. Random Forest deliberately grows deep unpruned trees, because bagging is
  there to kill the variance and you want each tree's bias low. On its own, almost never.


5.2 Pre-pruning knobs: stopping the tree while it grows
-------------------------------------------------------

    Pre-pruning means refusing to split a node during training. The tree never gets large in the
    first place, so it is the cheap option.

THE KNOBS

    max_depth              :  hard cap on levels from root to deepest leaf. The bluntest and most
                              intuitive knob, and it caps memory and inference cost directly.

    min_samples_split      :  a node needs at least this many rows before it is even considered for
                              splitting. Self-adjusting: plenty of rows near the root so splitting
                              continues, few near the bottom so it stops.

    min_samples_leaf       :  every child must keep at least this many rows or the split is
                              rejected. This is the one that kills single-outlier leaves and smooths
                              regression output.

    max_features           :  how many features are sampled and scanned at each node. Cuts training
                              time directly - the scan is linear in features, see 4.6 - and stops
                              one dominant feature driving every split.

    min_impurity_decrease  :  a split must buy at least this much impurity drop or it is refused.

WHICH DIRECTION TIGHTENS EACH ONE

    knob                    to regularize   what it mainly buys
    max_depth               decrease        speed, memory, a readable tree
    min_samples_split       increase        no rules built on a handful of rows
    min_samples_leaf        increase        smoother output, no single-outlier leaves
    max_features            decrease        speed, and feature diversity
    min_impurity_decrease   increase        only splits that pay for themselves

WHICH ONE TO REACH FOR

    Noisy data, outliers  :  min_samples_leaf, set to something like 10-50. An outlier then cannot
                             claim a leaf of its own.

    Choppy regression     :  min_samples_leaf again. A leaf predicts the mean, so more rows per leaf
                             means a smoother surface.

    Wide data, slow fits  :  max_features. The per-node scan is linear in features, so this is the
                             knob that actually buys time.

    Needs to be readable  :  max_depth at 3 to 5. Anything deeper stops being a thing a human reads.

> Real-world: min_samples_leaf is the knob worth reaching for first on real data. max_depth is the
  one everybody tunes, but it constrains the whole tree uniformly, while min_samples_leaf lets the
  tree stay deep where there is data to support it and stop early where there is not.

> Likely follow-up: "max_depth and min_samples_leaf both limit the tree. Why have both?"
  They constrain different things. max_depth is global and blunt - it stops every branch at the same
  level whether or not that branch had the data to keep going. min_samples_leaf is local and
  adaptive, so a dense region of feature space can grow deep while a sparse one stops early. On
  unbalanced data that difference is large.


5.3 Post-pruning: cost-complexity pruning and ccp_alpha
-------------------------------------------------------

The idea

    Grow the tree too big on purpose, then go back over it and ask of each branch: was that worth
    keeping?


EVERY BRANCH HAS A PRICE

    Collapsing a branch back into a single leaf does two things at once:

        training error goes UP      - you threw away some detail
        leaf count goes DOWN        - the model got simpler

    So each branch has a price - what that simplification cost you:

        price  =  extra error from collapsing it  /  number of leaves you save

    A cheap branch loses you almost no accuracy and saves several leaves, so cut it. An expensive
    one costs a lot of accuracy to save one leaf, so keep it.

    Example    :  two branches of the same tree

                  branch A  -  4 leaves, 10 training mistakes.  As one leaf: 22 mistakes.
                  branch B  -  3 leaves, 18 training mistakes.  As one leaf: 20 mistakes.

                  A:  extra error 12,  leaves saved 3   ->  price = 4.0
                  B:  extra error  2,  leaves saved 2   ->  price = 1.0

                  Branch B is the cheaper cut, so branch B goes first.


THE ALGORITHM

    1.  Price every branch.
    2.  Cut the cheapest one.
    3.  Repeat until only the root is left.

    That leaves you a list of trees, each smaller than the one before:

        T0 (full)  ->  T1  ->  T2  ->  ...  ->  Tk (root only)

    ccp_alpha is just where you stop on that list: cut every branch priced below alpha. So alpha =
    0 keeps the full tree, and a large alpha cuts all the way down to a stump.

    Note that you never search over tree shapes. The candidates are this single list, so tuning is
    picking one point on it - which is why post-pruning has one knob and pre-pruning has four.


WHY THE BOOKS WRITE IT AS  R_alpha(T) = R(T) + alpha * |T|

    The same rule from the other side. Charge rent of alpha for every leaf the tree uses, then keep
    whichever tree minimises error plus rent. R(T) is the training error, |T| is the leaf count.
    A branch survives only if it earns more than alpha per leaf it occupies.


THE SAME DATASET, PRUNED

     ccp_alpha   leaves   train acc   cv acc   test acc
       0.00000      140       1.000    0.739      0.749
       0.00091      136       0.998    0.739      0.751
       0.00169       87       0.963    0.758      0.751
       0.00185       65       0.942    0.755      0.749
       0.00472       14       0.850    0.778      0.784   <- best by CV
       0.12459        1       0.505    0.635      0.504

    140 leaves down to 14 - ten times smaller - and test accuracy goes UP, from 0.749 to 0.784.
    Push alpha too far and the tree collapses to a stump that predicts one class for everything.


IN SCIKIT-LEARN

    path   = tree.cost_complexity_pruning_path(train_x, train_y)
    alphas = path.ccp_alphas          # the branch prices, ascending
    # fit one tree per alpha, score each by cross-validation, keep the best


> Real-world: when someone asks for "the single best decision tree", this is the method that
  answers them. You get a smaller tree than pre-pruning usually lands on, better held-out accuracy,
  and one hyperparameter to defend instead of four.

> Likely follow-up: "Can pruning ever improve training accuracy?"
  Never. Every cut increases training error by construction - that is the price you are paying for
  a simpler tree. In the table above training accuracy falls from 1.000 to 0.850 while test
  accuracy rises. That is the trade working exactly as intended.


5.4 Pre-pruning vs post-pruning, and the horizon effect
-------------------------------------------------------

    Pre-pruning   :  decides whether to split before seeing what would come below. Cheap, because
                     the tree is never grown large.

    Post-pruning  :  grows everything first, then decides with the whole subtree in view. More
                     expensive, but far better informed.

THE HORIZON EFFECT

    A split that looks worthless on its own can be the one that makes the next two splits possible.
    Pre-pruning stops there and never finds out. Post-pruning has already grown past it and can see
    whether what followed was worth keeping.

    This is the XOR problem from 2.4 wearing a different hat, and min_impurity_decrease is precisely
    the knob that triggers it: set it above zero and any interaction whose first split looks flat
    becomes unreachable.

SIDE BY SIDE

                    pre-pruning               post-pruning
    decision made   during growth             after the tree is fully grown
    cost            cheap - never grows big   grow everything, then cut
    knobs to tune   4-5, and they interact    one (alpha), on a 1-D path
    horizon effect  vulnerable                immune

> Real-world: in practice most teams use both. Loose pre-pruning keeps training tractable on large
  data - a max_depth of 20 or so, which is nowhere near binding - and then ccp_alpha does the real
  work. Pre-pruning as a budget, post-pruning as the decision.

> Likely follow-up: "If post-pruning is better informed, why does anyone pre-prune?"
  Cost, mostly. Growing a full tree on millions of rows and then throwing most of it away is
  wasteful, and on very wide data it may not fit in memory at all. Pre-pruning also gives you direct
  control over inference latency, which post-pruning only gives you indirectly.


5.5 Tuning the knobs with cross-validation
------------------------------------------

    The rule       :  none of these can be chosen on training data. A tree with no constraints
                      always wins there - that is the definition of overfitting, not a tie-breaker.

    What to score  :  the cross-validated score on held-out folds. The table in 5.3 shows why:
                      training accuracy ranks the 140-leaf tree first and the correct answer last.

A WORKABLE ORDER

    1.  Plot train and validation score against max_depth. This alone tells you roughly
        where overfitting starts and gives you a baseline to beat.
    2.  Add min_samples_leaf. On noisy data this is the most improvement per unit of effort.
    3.  Only then run a grid. max_depth x min_samples_leaf x min_samples_split is usually
        enough - and it has to be a grid, because the knobs interact.
    4.  Or skip the grid entirely and tune ccp_alpha, which is a single 1-D search.

TWO THINGS THAT CATCH PEOPLE

    One-standard-error rule  :  among the settings within one standard error of the best CV score,
                                take the simplest tree. You give up almost no accuracy and gain
                                interpretability and stability.

    Leakage                  :  anything fitted on the data - encoders, imputers, and especially the
                                target encoding from 4.4 - belongs inside the CV folds, not before
                                them. Fit it outside and your CV score is optimistic and your chosen
                                alpha is wrong.

> Real-world: a candidate who says "I tuned max_depth until training accuracy stopped improving" has
  said the quiet part out loud. Training accuracy never stops improving. The whole job of this
  section is knowing which number you are allowed to look at.

> Likely follow-up: "How many folds, and does it matter here?"
  Five is the usual default and it is fine. It matters more than usual for trees because they are
  high-variance - a single validation split can rank two settings wrongly just from which rows
  landed where. If the CV scores across folds are widely spread, that spread is itself telling you
  the tree is under-regularized.
