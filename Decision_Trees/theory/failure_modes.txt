6. FAILURE MODES

6.1 Instability: a small data change produces a completely different tree
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

What happens

    Refit the tree on a slightly different sample of the same data and you get a different tree.
    Different root, different rules, a different story about what matters.

WHY: THE ROOT SPLIT IS OFTEN A NEAR-TIE

    The scan keeps one winner and throws the rest away. That is fine when one feature is clearly
    best. It is fragile when two are close.

    Best root split available on each feature, on the full training set:

        twin_a          0.343
        twin_b          0.368     <- wins, by 0.025
        distractor      0.015

    twin_a and twin_b carry nearly the same information, so they score nearly the same - a gap of
    0.025, next to a distractor sitting 25 times further back. Resample the rows and a gap that
    small moves around easily. Across 40 resamples the closest it ever got was 0.003, and 6 times
    twin_a won instead.

    Then remember the root is permanent (see 2.3). Flip that one near-coin-toss and every split
    beneath it is chosen on a different subset of rows, so the entire tree is rewritten.


MEASURING IT

    40 bootstrap refits of the same dataset:

    what changes a lot
        root feature chosen      twin_b 34/40,  twin_a 6/40
        tree depth               5 to 6
        leaf count               10 to 21

    what barely changes
        test accuracy            0.919 +/- 0.017
        pairwise disagreement    6.1% of test rows

    So the explanation is unstable while the predictions are not. The tree stays about as right as
    it was - it just keeps giving a different reason for being right.


> Real-world: you present the tree in a model review, someone asks why twin_b is at the top, and
  next month's refit puts twin_a there instead. Nothing was wrong either time - the two features
  carry the same information and the tree had to pick one. But the story you told is now
  unsupportable. A runnable version of this is Q11 in code/practice.ipynb.

> Likely follow-up: "How do you get a stable story out of a tree, then?"
  Not from one tree's structure. Refit on bootstrap samples and report how often each feature shows
  up near the top - which is essentially what a Random Forest's importance is doing - or use
  permutation importance or SHAP, which describe the predictions rather than the shape of the tree.


6.2 Axis-alignment: diagonal boundaries need a staircase of splits
------------------------------------------------------------------

    The geometry is in 2.5: every split is "feature_j <= t", a cut perpendicular to one axis, so the
    boundary can only ever be a staircase. What matters here is what that costs you.

MEASURING IT

    the true boundary is exactly diagonal: label = 1 when x2 > x1

        model                        test acc   leaves
        logistic regression             0.998        -
        tree, max_depth 2               0.890        4
        tree, max_depth 4               0.963       15
        tree, max_depth 6               0.982       33
        tree, unlimited depth           0.980       37
        tree, depth 2, + (x2 - x1)      1.000        2

    37 leaves to approximate one straight line, and it still loses to a logistic regression with two
    coefficients. Then hand the tree the difference x2 - x1 as a column and depth 2 gets it exactly,
    with two leaves.

WHAT TO DO

    Detect it  :  fit a linear model on the same features. If it beats a well-tuned tree, your
                  boundary is probably close to linear and the tree is spending its depth budget on
                  stairs.

    Fix it     :  engineer the combination the boundary actually uses - a difference, a ratio, a
                  rate. One good column beats thirty splits.

    Or rotate  :  PCA aligns the data with its own axes, which helps the tree, but you lose the
                  interpretability that made you pick a tree.

> Real-world: a fraud rule like "flag it if the amount is more than twice this customer's usual
  amount" is a diagonal in (amount, usual_amount) space. Teams routinely throw depth at it when a
  single ratio column would have ended the problem.

> Likely follow-up: "Doesn't a deeper tree eventually fix this?"
  No, and the table shows it - unlimited depth scores no better than depth 6, it just adds leaves.
  The staircase only converges to the diagonal in the limit of infinite data; with a finite sample
  the extra steps are fitting noise, so you pay variance for nothing.


6.3 Regression trees cannot extrapolate beyond the training range
-----------------------------------------------------------------

The problem, in one line

    A regression tree can never output a number outside the range of the targets it was trained
    on. Not rarely - never.


WHY

    A tree's prediction is just the constant stored in whichever leaf you land in, and every leaf
    value is an average of training targets. So the biggest number the tree can ever say is the
    biggest leaf average - and no input, however extreme, can push it past that.

    The leaf at the top end has no upper boundary. It covers everything from its threshold upwards,
    so x = 12, x = 15 and x = 20 all land in the same leaf and get the same answer. The bottom end
    is flat for the same reason.

    This is structural, not a setting you got wrong. More depth, more training data inside the
    range, a different criterion - none of them move the flat tail.


MEASURING IT

    trained on x in [0, 10] where the true relationship is y = 2x

             x    true y   tree says
           8.0      16.0       15.46
          10.0      20.0       20.49   <- edge of the training range
          12.0      24.0       20.49
          15.0      30.0       20.49
          20.0      40.0       20.49

    Inside the range the tree tracks y = 2x well. Past the edge it stops dead: at x = 20 the truth
    is 40 and the tree still says 20.49 - which is simply the largest leaf average it has, and the
    largest training target was 20.98.


> Real-world: any trending series - revenue, user counts, prices. A tree trained through last
  quarter will predict last quarter's ceiling forever. The usual fix is to model the trend
  separately, fit the tree on the detrended residuals, then add the trend back. Q12 in
  code/practice.ipynb runs this.

> Likely follow-up: "Does Random Forest or XGBoost fix it?"
  No. Every tree in the ensemble has the same flat tail, and averaging flat tails gives you a flat
  tail - boosting adds flat tails together and gets one too. This is the one tree failure mode that
  ensembling cannot touch, which is why linear models still win on extrapolative forecasting.


6.4 Missing values: surrogate splits in CART vs learned default directions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The problem, in one line

    A split asks "is x <= t?". If x is missing, that question has no answer - the row cannot go
    left and it cannot go right. Something has to decide for it.


THREE WAYS TO DECIDE

    Fill it in first   :  impute the missing value - mean, median, whatever - so the question has an
                          answer again. This is the old default, and it is the worst of the three.

    Surrogate splits   :  CART's answer, and R's rpart still does it. Alongside the real split,
                          store backup splits on other features that partition the rows almost the
                          same way; if the real feature is missing, ask the best available backup
                          instead. Accurate, but you compute and store extra splits at every node.

    Learned direction  :  what XGBoost, LightGBM and scikit-learn 1.3+ do. During the scan, try
                          sending every missing row left, then try sending them all right, and keep
                          whichever scores better. The tree simply learns which way they go, and it
                          costs almost nothing because the scan was running anyway.


MEASURING IT

    The same feature, the same 60 rows made missing. Only the labels on those rows differ.

        missing rows are labelled 1   ->   tree predicts 1 for a missing input
        missing rows are labelled 0   ->   tree predicts 0 for a missing input

    Nothing was filled in, and the answer flipped with the data. The tree learned which way to send
    the missing rows, which is exactly what it means to treat missingness as signal rather than as a
    gap to be patched.


THE TRAP

    Imputing with the mean invents a value that was never observed, piles a spike of rows onto it,
    and then lets the tree split on that spike. Worse, it erases the fact that the value was missing
    at all - and missingness is often the most predictive thing in the row. If you must impute, add
    a was_missing indicator column alongside it.


> Real-world: a loan application where income is blank. That blank is not random noise - it
  correlates with the outcome, often strongly. Mean-imputing it hands the model a plausible-looking
  income and throws away the signal that the applicant did not give one.

> Likely follow-up: "Is mean imputation ever fine for a tree?"
  When values really are missing completely at random and the rate is low, it is harmless. The
  moment missingness correlates with the target - which is most real data - you have deleted a
  useful feature and added a fake spike for the tree to split on. The indicator column costs one
  column and protects you either way.


6.5 Class imbalance: class_weight and threshold tuning
------------------------------------------------------

The problem, in one line

    When 98% of your rows are one class, predicting that class for everything is already 98%
    accurate. The tree discovers this and stops there.


WHY

    A split only gets chosen if it lowers impurity. When a node is already 98 percent one class its
    impurity is barely above zero, so there is almost nothing left to remove. A split that correctly
    isolates a small pocket of positives moves that number by a rounding error, and loses the scan
    to a split that carves up the majority instead.

    So the minority never gets a leaf of its own. Every leaf votes majority, and the tree predicts
    the majority everywhere - while reporting excellent accuracy, because on this data that IS
    excellent accuracy.

    min_samples_leaf makes it worse. At 2 percent positives, a 50-row leaf can only vote positive if
    the pocket is unusually dense.


MEASURING IT

    2% of rows are positive, and the signal is weak

    setting                     accuracy   recall  precision  flagged
    default                        0.980    0.000      0.000        0
    class_weight='balanced'        0.773    0.722      0.061      425
    default, threshold 0.05        0.874    0.556      0.087      230

    The default tree flags 0 rows. Not a few - none. Recall is exactly zero while accuracy reads
    0.980, which is the whole trap in one line.


THE TWO FIXES, AND WHAT EACH ACTUALLY CHANGES

    class_weight      :  weights the rows inside the impurity calculation, so one minority row
                         counts for many. That changes which splits win the scan, so you get a
                         different tree.

    threshold tuning  :  leaves the tree exactly as it is and stops using 0.5 as the cut on
                         predict_proba. Same tree, different decision.

    Both find real positives and both cost precision. That trade is the actual decision, and it
    belongs to whoever owns the cost of a false alarm downstream.


> Real-world: fraud, churn, defect detection, rare disease - the positive class is the whole point
  and it is always the small one. Reporting accuracy on any of these is how a model that has never
  flagged a single case gets signed off.

> Likely follow-up: "class_weight or threshold tuning - which one?"
  Start with the threshold. It is reversible, it costs one line, and you can pick the operating
  point after seeing the precision-recall curve rather than guessing beforehand. Reach for
  class_weight when the tree is not even finding minority-relevant splits to score - at that point
  no threshold can help you, because the information is not in the tree.


6.6 Bias toward features with many distinct values
--------------------------------------------------

The problem, in one line

    A feature with more distinct values gets more chances to look good, so the tree prefers it -
    even when the feature means nothing at all.


WHY

    Every distinct value is one more threshold the scan can try. A binary column offers exactly one
    place to cut. A column of 2000 unique numbers offers 1999.

    So the high-cardinality column enters the scan with 1999 tickets and the binary column with one.
    Even when its numbers are pure noise, one of those 1999 cuts will happen to separate the rows in
    front of it better than the single honest cut does - by luck alone. It wins, and it keeps
    winning, all the way down the tree.


TWO WAYS IT HURTS YOU

    It wins the root       :  an ID, a raw timestamp or a hash left in the table gets chosen first,
                              splits the data into near-singletons, and hands you perfect training
                              accuracy that transfers to nothing.

    It poisons importance  :  feature_importances_ is computed on training data and sums impurity
                              drops, so it rewards whatever gets used most often and deepest - which
                              is exactly what a high-cardinality column does.


MEASURING IT

    Three binary features actually drive the label. random_noise drives nothing.

    feature           distinct values      MDI   permutation
    random_noise                 2000    0.386         0.011   <- pure noise
    binary_b                        2    0.244         0.160
    binary_a                        2    0.216         0.144
    binary_c                        2    0.154         0.175

    random_noise carries no information whatsoever, and MDI still ranks it first with more credit
    than any real feature. Permutation importance, measured on held-out data, correctly puts it at
    roughly zero.


WHAT TO DO

    Detect  :  compare MDI against permutation importance, and check the cardinality of whatever
               sits at the top of your importance list.

    Fix     :  drop identifiers before training, report importance with permutation or SHAP rather
               than MDI, and target-encode genuine high-cardinality categoricals out-of-fold.


> Real-world: this is the single most common way a tree model looks brilliant in a notebook and dies
  in production. Someone leaves customer_id or created_at in the frame, the tree memorises it, and
  cross-validation does not catch it because the same IDs appear in every fold. Q9 in
  code/practice.ipynb reproduces it.

> Likely follow-up: "Why does MDI get it so wrong?"
  Two things compounding. It is measured on training data, where the noise column genuinely does
  reduce impurity - that is what overfitting means. And it accumulates over every use of the
  feature, so a column with that many thresholds keeps winning deep in the tree where nodes are
  small and almost any cut looks like progress.
