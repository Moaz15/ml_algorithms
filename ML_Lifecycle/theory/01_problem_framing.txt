STAGE 1: BUSINESS PROBLEM FRAMING, SUCCESS METRICS, FEASIBILITY, ETHICS
================================================================================

The single most common failure in applied ML is not a bad model. It is a
correctly trained model for a wrongly framed problem. This stage costs days and
saves quarters.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. FROM BUSINESS PROBLEM TO ML PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The translation has four slots. If you cannot fill all four, you are not ready
to touch data.

    Decision      What action changes because of the prediction?
    Prediction    What quantity, for which entity, at what time?
    Label         How is the ground truth observed, and after how long?
    Constraint    Latency, cost, coverage, interpretability, regulation.

Example: "reduce customer churn"
    Decision    : who gets a retention offer this week (budget: 5k offers)
    Prediction  : P(user cancels within next 30 days), per active user, scored
                  every Monday
    Label       : cancellation event observed in the 30 days AFTER the score
                  date -> the label matures 30 days late
    Constraint  : batch scoring is fine (weekly), must explain to the CRM team
                  why a user was picked

Notice what the framing bought you:
- It is a ranking problem under a budget of 5000, not a classification problem.
  So the metric is precision@5000 / lift@k, not accuracy.
- The label matures in 30 days, so the freshest 30 days of data are unusable for
  training and your feedback loop is at best monthly.
- Offers change behaviour, so the moment you deploy, your training data is
  contaminated by your own intervention (see section 6).

Common framings and their natural targets:
    "Who will do X"          -> binary classification, ranked by score
    "How much / how many"    -> regression, or quantile regression if the cost
                                of over- and under-prediction differs
    "Which one of these"     -> ranking / learning-to-rank, not classification
    "Is this weird"          -> anomaly detection (labels usually absent)
    "What is the effect of"  -> causal inference / uplift, NOT prediction.
                                A churn model tells you who will leave. An uplift
                                model tells you who will stay BECAUSE of the
                                offer. These have different, sometimes disjoint,
                                top-k sets. Confusing them is a classic and very
                                expensive mistake.

Do you even need ML? Prefer a rule if:
- the mapping is a known deterministic policy (tax rules, thresholds in law),
- there are fewer than a few hundred labelled examples,
- errors are unacceptable rather than merely costly,
- a simple heuristic already captures most of the value (see stage 5 baselines).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. SUCCESS METRICS: THREE LAYERS, NEVER ONE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Layer 1  Business KPI      revenue, retention rate, cost per resolved ticket
    Layer 2  Product/proxy     offer acceptance rate, click-through, escalations
    Layer 3  ML metric         AUC, PR-AUC, RMSE, NDCG@10, calibration error

Rules:
- Optimise layer 3, validate layer 2, get paid on layer 1.
- Write down, before modelling, the assumed chain: "+0.02 PR-AUC -> +1.5pp
  precision@5k -> ~180 extra saves/month -> ~X of retained revenue". If you
  cannot write the chain, the ML metric is unanchored.
- Every metric needs a guardrail metric that must NOT degrade (latency, fairness
  gap, false-positive rate on a protected slice, support ticket volume).
- Prefer a single decision metric plus guardrails over a weighted blend of five
  metrics that nobody can reason about.

Choosing the ML metric is a cost question, not a taste question. Write the cost
matrix. If a false negative costs 20x a false positive, accuracy is meaningless
and you should be tuning a threshold on expected cost. See 08_evaluation_metrics.

Baselines to state alongside any target number:
    - current system / human performance
    - the trivial predictor (majority class, last value, global mean)
    - the theoretical ceiling (label noise, irreducible error)
"90% accuracy" is a fact about the base rate until you give these three.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. FEASIBILITY AND ROI
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Feasibility checklist (any "no" is a redesign, not a risk to accept):
    Signal      Is there plausible predictive signal, i.e. do features causally
                or reliably co-vary with the target? Sanity-check with a quick
                correlation / small-sample model before committing a quarter.
    Volume      Enough positives, not just enough rows. 10M rows with 40
                positives is a 40-example problem.
    Timeliness  Are features available AT PREDICTION TIME with acceptable
                latency? A feature computed by a nightly job cannot serve a
                real-time API without a feature store.
    Label       Can you observe ground truth at all, and how late?
    Ceiling     Even a perfect model - how much value? Sometimes the answer is
                "the process downstream can only act on 200 cases/week", which
                caps the value regardless of model quality.

ROI sketch:
    value  = (volume of decisions) x (improvement in decision quality)
             x (value per decision) x (adoption rate)
    cost   = build + data + serving infra + on-call + retraining + risk

Adoption rate is the term engineers always forget and it is often 0.3. A model
nobody trusts or that lands in a workflow nobody uses returns zero.

Risk register worth keeping short and explicit:
    - the label is a proxy for what you actually care about (proxy risk)
    - training data reflects a past policy that will change (drift by design)
    - the model creates a feedback loop that invalidates its own training data
    - a rare-but-catastrophic error mode (safety, legal, reputational)
    - single-source data dependency that can silently break upstream

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. DATA AVAILABILITY, ETHICS AND PRIVACY AUDIT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before the first join, answer:
    - Do we have the legal basis to use this data for THIS purpose? Consent
      collected for billing does not automatically cover model training.
    - Is there PII? Can it be dropped, hashed, tokenised, or aggregated? Default
      to not ingesting what you do not need.
    - Retention: how long may we keep it, and does the model itself count as a
      derived artefact that must be deleted on a deletion request?
    - Are protected attributes present? You often need them to MEASURE fairness
      even when you must not USE them as features. Store them in an evaluation-
      only table with restricted access. See 10_error_analysis_and_fairness.
    - Proxy discrimination: postcode, device type, and name-derived features can
      encode protected attributes. Removing the attribute does not remove the
      bias.
    - Is the decision high-stakes for an individual (credit, hiring, medical,
      moderation)? Then interpretability and appeal paths are requirements, not
      nice-to-haves, and may be legally mandated.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WRITE THE ONE-PAGER BEFORE THE NOTEBOOK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A design doc that fits on one page and contains:
    problem, decision it feeds, entity + prediction time, label definition and
    maturation lag, metric + guardrails + target, baseline, data sources,
    serving mode and latency budget, main risks, what "kill this project" looks
    like.

The last item matters. Pre-committing to a stop condition is what separates an
experiment from a sunk cost.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. FEEDBACK LOOPS: THE FRAMING TRAP
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Once deployed, the model influences the data it will next be trained on.
    - Fraud model blocks transactions -> you never observe whether they were
      fraudulent -> the next model is trained on the survivors only.
      (selection bias / "reject inference")
    - Recommender shows what it predicts you like -> clicks confirm it -> the
      model becomes more confident about a narrowing slice (rich-get-richer).
    - Churn model targets likely-churners with offers -> they stay -> the model
      looks WRONG on exactly the users it got right.

Mitigations, decided at framing time, not after:
    - hold out a small random control that bypasses the model, permanently
    - log the score, the decision, and the policy version with every event, so
      you can reweight (inverse propensity) later
    - use exploration (epsilon-random, Thompson sampling) where the cost allows
    - evaluate against the control group, not against historical data

If an interviewer asks "how would you know your model is working in production",
the expected answer is "a holdout / A-B test against the control", not "monitor
AUC".
