ML LIFECYCLE: INTERVIEW Q&A (MODEL-AGNOSTIC)
================================================================================

This is the ML-system-design and ML-judgement half of a Senior MLE loop. The
algorithm-specific Q&A lives in each topic folder. Underlying detail for
everything here is in the numbered files in this directory.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 1: PROBLEM FRAMING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: A product manager says "build a model to reduce churn." What do you ask
   before writing any code?
A: Four things. What decision the prediction feeds (who gets a retention offer,
   and how many offers can we afford?), the entity and prediction time (which
   users, scored when?), how the label is defined and when it matures (cancelled
   within 30 days -> labels are 30 days late), and the constraints (latency,
   interpretability, budget). The answers usually reframe the problem: a fixed
   offer budget turns it from classification into ranking under a budget, so the
   metric becomes precision@k or lift@k, not accuracy. I would also flag early
   that if we want to know who to TARGET rather than who will leave, this is an
   uplift problem, not a prediction problem.
   Follow-up an interviewer may ask: why is the top-k of a churn model different
   from the top-k of an uplift model?

Q: How do you connect an ML metric to a business KPI?
A: Three layers: the business KPI (retained revenue), a product proxy (offer
   acceptance rate), and the ML metric (PR-AUC). Before modelling I write the
   assumed chain, e.g. +0.02 PR-AUC -> +1.5pp precision@5k -> ~180 extra saves
   per month -> X revenue. If I cannot write that chain, the ML metric is
   unanchored and optimising it is faith-based. I also define guardrail metrics
   that must not degrade, and I expect the offline/online gap to be information
   about the framing rather than noise.

Q: When should you NOT use machine learning?
A: When the mapping is a known deterministic policy, when you have a few hundred
   examples, when errors are unacceptable rather than merely costly, when a
   simple heuristic already captures most of the value, or when nothing downstream
   can act on the prediction. Also when you cannot observe ground truth at all -
   without labels or a proxy you cannot improve or even measure the system.

Q: What is a feedback loop and why does it matter at framing time?
A: The deployed model changes the data it will be trained on next. A fraud model
   blocks transactions so you never learn whether they were fraudulent; a churn
   model saves the users it flagged so it looks wrong exactly where it was right.
   Mitigations have to be designed in up front: a permanent random holdout that
   bypasses the model, logging score + decision + policy version so you can
   reweight later, and some exploration. If you ask "how would you know the model
   works in production", the answer is the holdout or the A/B test, not the AUC.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 2-3: DATA, EDA, QUALITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: What is point-in-time correctness and why is it hard?
A: Every feature for a row scored at time t must be computed only from data
   available strictly before t, and the label only from after t. It is hard
   because production databases are mutable - if user.plan is updated in place,
   the historical value is gone, and joining today's dimension table onto
   historical events silently injects the future. The fixes are CDC or snapshot
   tables, event-time windowed aggregations, as-of joins, and keeping both
   event_time and ingest_time so you can reconstruct what you knew at t.

Q: What is training/serving skew and how do you eliminate it?
A: Features computed one way for training (batch SQL) and another way for
   serving (streaming Java) diverge, so the model sees different inputs in
   production than it was trained on. Three structural fixes: one implementation
   with two runners, a feature store materialising one definition to both offline
   and online stores, or log-and-train - log the exact feature vector used at
   serving time and train on those logs, which makes skew impossible by
   construction.

Q: Missing values - how do you decide what to do?
A: First classify the mechanism. MCAR: dropping is safe but wasteful. MAR
   (depends on observed features): conditional/model-based imputation is
   principled. MNAR (depends on the unobserved value itself, e.g. high earners
   hiding income): no imputation recovers it, so keep the missingness as a
   signal. In practice I almost always add an is_missing indicator, compare the
   target rate for missing versus present (a big gap hints at MNAR or leakage),
   and fit imputers inside the CV fold on training data only.

Q: What is multicollinearity, how do you detect it, and does it matter?
A: One feature is nearly a linear combination of others, so X^T X is near
   singular. Detect with a correlation matrix (pairwise only), VIF = 1/(1-R_j^2)
   with >5 suspicious and >10 severe (catches multi-way), or the condition
   number. It inflates coefficient variance, so signs flip between refits and
   coefficients are uninterpretable - but PREDICTION is usually fine as long as
   the collinearity structure holds at serving time. So it matters for inference
   and stability, not accuracy. Ridge is the canonical fix because it shrinks
   correlated coefficients together.

Q: You get 0.99 AUC on your first model. What do you do?
A: Assume leakage until proven otherwise. Check for a single feature with
   near-perfect AUC on its own; look for names like final_, total_, status,
   reason, refund; check whether the feature's null rate differs by class; verify
   the split is not random over time-ordered or grouped data; check for duplicate
   entities across train and test; and confirm no preprocessing statistic was
   fitted before the split. The test for each suspect feature is: would this
   value have existed, with this value, one second before prediction time?

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 4: SPLITTING AND VALIDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: How do you choose a splitting strategy?
A: Ask what the model must generalise ACROSS at serving time and split along
   exactly that axis. New rows from the same population -> random. Future periods
   -> time-based. New users/patients/devices -> group split. Rare classes ->
   stratified. Most real systems need grouped plus temporal. A random split on
   temporal or grouped data is the classic disaster because the model memorises
   entity quirks or peeks at the future.

Q: Name the ways preprocessing leaks.
A: Scaler statistics, imputation values, target encoding, feature selection,
   and resampling/SMOTE all applied before the split or outside the fold.
   Target encoding is the worst because it injects the label directly. Feature
   selection is the sneakiest because it never touches a label at fit time but
   the selection itself used the labels of the whole dataset. The structural fix
   is to put every transform in a Pipeline and fit the Pipeline inside the CV
   loop, so leakage is impossible rather than merely forbidden.

Q: Why is random search usually better than grid search?
A: Hyperparameter importance is very unequal - typically two or three parameters
   dominate. With n trials, grid search over k parameters tries only n^(1/k)
   distinct values per parameter, while random search tries n distinct values of
   every parameter. So for the same budget random search explores the important
   axes far more densely. Use log-uniform sampling for scale parameters, and move
   to Bayesian optimisation or Hyperband when each training run is expensive.

Q: What is nested cross-validation and when do you need it?
A: An inner loop tunes hyperparameters and an outer loop estimates
   generalisation, with tuning repeated independently inside each outer fold. You
   need it when the reported performance estimate must be unbiased for a tuned
   model - a single CV loop that both selects and reports is optimistically
   biased because selection fits the validation folds. In industry a clean,
   touched-once held-out test set usually plays the role of the outer loop; nested
   CV matters most on small data and in regulated or published work.

Q: Your validation score improved by 0.3 points. Ship it?
A: Not on that alone. I would bootstrap the test set for a confidence interval,
   check whether 0.3 is inside the noise band, use a paired test (McNemar for two
   classifiers on the same test set), check per-slice results, count how many
   experiments have already been run against this validation set (selection
   optimism grows with the number of comparisons), and confirm the change is
   worth its operational cost. Then a shadow or canary deploy, and an A/B test to
   see whether the business metric actually moves.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGES 5-6: MODELLING AND TUNING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Why start with a baseline?
A: It defines what "better" means and it validates the plumbing end to end. Three
   tiers: the trivial predictor (majority class / mean / last value), the existing
   business heuristic, and a simple regularised model. An improvement stated
   without these is meaningless - 90% accuracy is just a restatement of the base
   rate. And an implausibly good baseline immediately exposes a broken split or a
   broken metric, for free.

Q: Tabular problem, one million rows. Which model and why?
A: Gradient boosted trees, almost certainly - they handle mixed types, missing
   values, monotone constraints, and interactions with little preprocessing, and
   they remain the state of the art on tabular data. I would still fit a
   regularised logistic/linear model first to see whether the problem is basically
   linear and to get a naturally calibrated reference, and I would only consider a
   deep model for multi-modal inputs, very large data, or a shared-representation
   requirement - not for a few points of AUC.

Q: Your model underfits. Your model overfits. What do you do in each case?
A: Underfitting (train error already high): more capacity, better features, less
   regularisation, longer training - and first check that the model can overfit
   100 rows to near-zero loss, because if it cannot, this is a bug, not a
   modelling problem. Overfitting (train much better than validation): more data,
   more regularisation, simpler model, early stopping, augmentation. The learning
   curve decides between "get more data" and "get a better model": if validation
   is still improving with size, collect data; if the curves have converged at a
   poor level, more data will not help.

Q: What is the one-standard-error rule and why use it?
A: After cross-validation, instead of taking the configuration with the best mean
   score, take the simplest configuration whose score is within one standard error
   of the best. With many trials and a noisy validation metric, the winner is
   partly the luckiest configuration; the rule buys robustness and usually a
   simpler model at negligible cost.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 7: EVALUATION, INTERPRETABILITY, FAIRNESS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC?
A: ROC-AUC is the probability a random positive is scored above a random
   negative - pure ranking quality, invariant to class balance. That invariance is
   its weakness under heavy imbalance: FPR has an enormous true-negative
   denominator, so thousands of false positives barely move the curve and a 0.95
   ROC-AUC model can be operationally useless. PR-AUC focuses on the positive
   class and reflects precision at realistic operating points, but its baseline is
   the prevalence, so it is not comparable across datasets with different base
   rates. Rare positives and you care about the flagged set -> PR-AUC.

Q: How do you choose the classification threshold?
A: From the cost matrix, not from 0.5. For calibrated probabilities the
   cost-minimising threshold is t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN), so if a miss costs 10x
   a false alarm, t* is about 0.09. Alternatives are a capacity constraint (k
   alerts per day) or a target precision. The threshold is a deployment parameter
   - set it on validation, verify on test, store it as config so it can change
   without retraining. Note the prerequisite: the cost formula only works if the
   probabilities are calibrated.

Q: What is calibration, who needs it, and how do you fix it?
A: A model is calibrated if among predictions of 0.7, about 70% are positive.
   Calibration and ranking are independent - recalibrating never changes AUC. You
   need it whenever the probability itself feeds a decision: expected value,
   pricing, cost-derived thresholds, or a number shown to a human. Measure with a
   reliability diagram, ECE, and the Brier score (a proper scoring rule). Fix with
   Platt scaling (parametric, small data), isotonic regression (flexible, needs a
   few thousand points), or temperature scaling for neural nets - always fitted on
   a held-out calibration set. Boosted trees and deep nets are overconfident;
   Naive Bayes is extremely so; anything trained with resampling or class weights
   is miscalibrated by construction.

Q: SHAP vs LIME.
A: Both are local, post-hoc, model-agnostic. LIME fits a sparse linear surrogate
   on perturbations weighted by proximity: cheap and intuitive, but unstable
   across runs, sensitive to the kernel width, and with no fidelity guarantee.
   SHAP computes Shapley values - the average marginal contribution over all
   feature orderings - and is the unique attribution satisfying local accuracy,
   missingness and consistency; TreeSHAP makes it exact and fast for tree
   ensembles. I default to SHAP for tabular work. Both share the fundamental
   caveat: they explain the MODEL, not the world. If the model learned a spurious
   correlation, SHAP faithfully explains the spurious correlation. Correlated
   features also split credit, so low attribution does not mean unimportant.

Q: What is wrong with a partial dependence plot?
A: Two things. It assumes the plotted feature is independent of the others, so it
   averages the model over combinations that cannot occur (2m tall and 40kg). And
   it is an average, so opposite effects in two subgroups cancel into a flat line
   for a highly influential feature. Fix the second by plotting ICE curves
   alongside; fix the first by using ALE, which uses local differences within
   small intervals and is unbiased under correlation.

Q: What is slice analysis and why does the aggregate metric hide problems?
A: The aggregate is dominated by the majority slice, so a model can improve
   overall and get worse for every high-value segment - Simpson's paradox is
   routine when slice sizes differ. I enumerate slices from domain knowledge,
   compute the metric per slice with size and a confidence interval, rank by
   size times gap, and put per-slice acceptance gates in CI so a release that
   breaks a critical slice fails. And I control for multiple comparisons: search
   500 slices and some fail by chance, so I require a failure to reproduce.

Q: Can a model be fair by every definition at once?
A: No, and this is arithmetic, not engineering. When base rates genuinely differ
   between groups and the classifier is imperfect, calibration within groups,
   equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates cannot all hold -
   the Kleinberg et al. / Chouldechova impossibility result. That is exactly the
   COMPAS dispute: the tool was calibrated and its error rates differed by race,
   both true simultaneously. So fairness is a choice about which error to
   distribute, made with domain experts, legal and affected stakeholders - not a
   library setting. In practice I pick a definition tied to the harm (equal
   opportunity when the positive outcome is a benefit, equalised odds when false
   positives also harm), measure per group including intersections, and prefer
   fixing the label-generation process over post-hoc threshold surgery.

Q: You cannot use a protected attribute as a feature. Are you done?
A: No. Proxies - postcode, device, name-derived features, browsing history -
   encode it anyway, so removing the attribute removes your ability to MEASURE
   the disparity while leaving the disparity intact. I keep the attribute in an
   evaluation-only, access-controlled table so I can audit, and I look upstream:
   historical bias in the labels, representation bias, and measurement bias
   (where the proxy label means different things across groups) are the usual
   real sources.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGES 8-9: DEPLOYMENT AND MLOPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Batch or real-time serving - how do you decide?
A: By how fast the input changes and how fast the decision must be made. If the
   prediction depends only on state that changes slowly, precompute in batch and
   serve from a table: cheapest, simplest, no latency risk, easy to backfill and
   audit. Go real-time when the features depend on the request itself (this
   transaction, this query) or when staleness of minutes is unacceptable.
   Real-time buys freshness and costs you an SLO, a feature store, and an on-call
   rotation.

Q: Difference between shadow deployment, canary and A/B test?
A: Shadow sends real traffic to the new model and logs predictions without acting
   on them - it validates infrastructure, latency and prediction distribution at
   zero user risk, but it cannot measure business impact because no decision
   changed. Canary routes a small fraction of real decisions to the new model with
   automatic rollback, limiting blast radius. An A/B test randomises users between
   models and measures the business KPI with a proper statistical test - the only
   way to establish impact. The usual order is shadow, canary, A/B.

Q: Your offline metric improved but the A/B test shows no lift. What happened?
A: Candidate explanations, roughly in order: the offline metric is not the
   decision metric (better ranking on a metric nobody acts on); training/serving
   skew, so the production model does not see what you evaluated; the improvement
   is inside the noise band and the test is underpowered; the gain lives in a
   slice with no business value; a feedback loop means the old model already
   captured the easy wins; latency regression cancelled the quality gain; or the
   offline evaluation leaked. I would check skew and metric definition first
   because they are the most common and the easiest to verify from logs.

Q: Data drift vs concept drift.
A: Data (covariate) drift is P(X) changing with P(y|X) fixed - a new user
   segment; the learned relationship still holds and degradation is often modest.
   Concept drift is P(y|X) changing - the same inputs now imply a different
   outcome, e.g. fraudsters adapting; the model is now wrong, not merely
   extrapolating. Label/prior drift is P(y) moving, which breaks calibration and
   any cost-derived threshold. And in practice the most frequent cause of a drift
   alert is none of these: it is an upstream schema or logging change, which you
   should rule out first because the fix is upstream, not a retrain.

Q: How do you detect drift without labels?
A: Per-feature distribution tests against a reference window - PSI (>0.25 is the
   usual "significant" convention), KS for continuous, chi-square for
   categorical, Wasserstein or JS as effect sizes. But the strongest single tool
   is a domain classifier: train a model to distinguish reference rows from
   current rows; AUC near 0.5 means indistinguishable, and if it is high, its own
   feature importances name exactly what drifted. That also catches multivariate
   drift that per-feature tests miss. Complement with prediction-distribution
   drift and confidence collapse as early proxies for concept drift, and buy a
   small daily random sample of human labels so you have an unbiased quality
   signal before natural labels mature.

Q: With ten million rows, every KS test is significant. What do you do?
A: Stop testing p-values and monitor effect sizes - PSI, Wasserstein, JS - with
   thresholds set empirically by backtesting historical windows you know were
   fine. Also control multiple comparisons across hundreds of features by
   weighting drift by feature importance and alerting only on features the model
   actually relies on, and attach an estimated performance impact wherever
   possible.

Q: How often should you retrain?
A: Measure, do not guess. Backtest with a rolling origin: replay the last year,
   retrain at various cadences and window policies, and plot the realised metric.
   Pick the cadence where the decay curve starts to bend. Fraud and ads often
   need daily; churn weekly to monthly; a slow-moving risk model quarterly. Then
   layer event triggers (product launch, new market) on top of the schedule, and
   treat drift alerts as an invitation to check impact rather than an automatic
   retrain - otherwise you spend your year chasing seasonality.

Q: What must be true before an automated pipeline promotes a model to
   production?
A: Quality gates on a FROZEN evaluation set, not one regenerated by the same
   pipeline; per-slice gates, not just the aggregate; a sane prediction
   distribution versus the incumbent; data validation passed on the training
   snapshot; and a shadow or canary period with automatic rollback. Plus the
   basics: the model version records its code commit, data snapshot and config,
   and rollback is a pointer change that has actually been tested.

Q: What are the three things you must version, and why is a model version a
   triple?
A: Code, data, and the model artefact plus its metadata. A model version is the
   triple (code commit, data snapshot id, config), because reproducing a result
   requires all three - "SELECT * FROM users" is not a data version. If any one
   is unversioned the model cannot be debugged, compared, or defended in an
   audit.

Q: Name some ML-specific technical debt.
A: CACE - changing anything changes everything, because models entangle their
   inputs. Correction cascades, where model B patches model A's output and A can
   no longer change. Undeclared consumers reading your prediction table. Untracked
   data dependencies, where an unused feature still costs a live pipeline.
   Feedback loops, direct and hidden. Configuration debt - thresholds and windows
   scattered and untested. Pipeline jungles and glue code, where the model is 5%
   of the system.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYNTHESIS QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q: Design an end-to-end system for detecting fraudulent transactions.
A: Frame it: score every transaction in real time, decide block / step-up
   authentication / allow, so this is ranking into three actions with a very
   asymmetric cost matrix and a hard latency budget (tens of milliseconds).
   Labels come from chargebacks and are 30-90 days late, plus fast analyst
   labels on a reviewed sample. Data: transaction stream plus point-in-time
   account and device aggregates from a feature store, with the same definitions
   online and offline. Split by time, with purging around the label window, and
   by entity so one card is not in both sides. Baselines: the existing rule
   engine, then a GBM. Metric: recall at a fixed false-positive budget plus
   precision@k for the analyst queue, calibrated probabilities so the cost
   threshold is meaningful, evaluated per slice (card type, geography, merchant
   category, new versus established accounts). Serve behind a low-latency API
   with the rule engine as fallback, log every score and feature vector, shadow
   then canary then a controlled ramp. Monitor feature freshness and nulls
   first, then prediction and feature drift, then chargeback-based metrics as
   they mature. Retrain weekly on a weighted window because adversaries adapt,
   and keep a random unblocked holdout so I can still observe the outcomes of
   transactions the model would have blocked - otherwise the next model trains
   only on the survivors.
   Follow-up to expect: how do you handle the fact that blocking prevents you
   from observing the label? (reject inference, propensity weighting, and the
   holdout.)

Q: A model has been in production for six months and stakeholders say it "feels
   worse". Nobody has an alert. How do you investigate?
A: Layer by layer from the outside in. Infrastructure: latency, error rates,
   timeouts - a p99 regression or partial outage looks like quality loss. Data
   quality: feature freshness, null rates, cardinality and schema versus
   training; a silently failing upstream job is the most likely cause. Then
   distribution: input drift with a domain classifier, and prediction
   distribution against the historical one. Then quality: recompute the metric on
   whatever labels have matured, sliced - the degradation is usually concentrated
   in a segment (a new market, a new device) rather than spread evenly. In
   parallel I check whether the decision policy or the threshold changed, and
   whether something downstream changed how the prediction is used. The
   deliverable is not only the fix but the monitor that should have caught it,
   plus the measured detection gap.
