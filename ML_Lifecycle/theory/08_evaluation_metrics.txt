STAGE 7a: EVALUATION METRICS AND VALIDATION
================================================================================

Regression metric derivations (R^2, adjusted R^2, RMSE vs MAE) are covered in
../../Linear_Regression/theory/evaluation_metrics.txt. Imbalanced-data metric
choice is in ../../handling_imbalanced_data.txt. This file is the model-agnostic
overview: what each metric measures, when it lies, and how to turn scores into
decisions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE CONFUSION MATRIX AND ITS FAMILY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

                      predicted +      predicted -
    actual +             TP               FN        (type II error, a miss)
    actual -             FP               TN        (type I error, false alarm)

    Accuracy    = (TP+TN)/all       useless under imbalance: 99% accuracy on a
                                    1%-positive problem = predicting "no".
    Precision   = TP/(TP+FP)        of those we flagged, how many were right.
                                    "Do not waste the action."
    Recall/TPR  = TP/(TP+FN)        of the real positives, how many we caught.
                                    "Do not miss the case."
    Specificity = TN/(TN+FP)        true negative rate.
    FPR         = FP/(FP+TN)        = 1 - specificity.
    F1          = harmonic mean of precision and recall. The harmonic mean
                  punishes imbalance between the two - 1.0 and 0.1 gives 0.18,
                  not 0.55.
    F-beta      = (1+b^2) PR / (b^2 P + R). b > 1 weights recall (b=2 for
                  medical screening), b < 1 weights precision (b=0.5 when an
                  action is expensive).
    MCC         = correlation between predicted and actual labels, uses all four
                  cells, robust under imbalance. Underused and a good answer to
                  "what would you use instead of F1".
    Balanced acc= mean of recall and specificity; Cohen's kappa corrects
                  accuracy for chance agreement.

Precision-recall trade-off: both are functions of the threshold. Lower the
threshold -> recall up, precision usually down. Any claim about precision or
recall without stating the threshold is incomplete.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THRESHOLD-FREE METRICS: ROC-AUC VS PR-AUC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ROC curve: TPR vs FPR across all thresholds. AUC = P(score of a random positive
> score of a random negative), i.e. a pure RANKING quality measure. 0.5 is
random, 1.0 is perfect.

Properties:
    + invariant to the class balance and to any monotonic transform of scores
    + summarises all thresholds at once, good for comparing model candidates
    - that same balance-invariance is the problem under heavy imbalance: FPR has
      a huge denominator (TN), so thousands of false positives barely move the
      curve. A model with 0.95 ROC-AUC can still be operationally useless at 1%
      positives.
    - it says nothing about calibration or about performance at the one
      threshold you will actually deploy.

PR curve: precision vs recall. Its baseline is the positive base rate (not 0.5),
so PR-AUC / average precision is NOT comparable across datasets with different
prevalence. Use PR-AUC when positives are rare and you care about the flagged
set: fraud, search relevance, rare-disease screening.

Rule of thumb: care about both classes and roughly balanced -> ROC-AUC. Care
about the positive class under imbalance -> PR-AUC (average precision).

Operating-point metrics matter more than either in production:
    precision@k, recall@k, lift@k    when you have a fixed action budget
    recall at fixed precision        "how many frauds do we catch while keeping
                                     the false-alarm rate under 2%"
    partial AUC                      restricted to the FPR range you can afford

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THRESHOLD SELECTION IS A BUSINESS DECISION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Never keep 0.5 by default. Choose it by one of:

    Expected cost minimisation - the principled answer. With costs C_FP and C_FN,
    the optimal threshold for a CALIBRATED probability is

        t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)

    (act when p * C_FN > (1-p) * C_FP). If a miss costs 10x a false alarm,
    t* = 1/11 = 0.09, not 0.5. This formula, and the fact that it requires
    calibrated probabilities, is a standard senior-level question.

    Capacity constraint    - pick the threshold that yields k alerts per day.
    Metric target          - the point maximising F1/F-beta on validation, or
                             the highest recall subject to precision >= p_min.
    Youden's J             - argmax(TPR - FPR); assumes symmetric costs, so it
                             is rarely the right business answer.

Tune the threshold on validation, verify on test, and store it as configuration
so it can be changed without retraining. Under class rebalancing or negative
downsampling the threshold also has to be re-derived on the natural base rate.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. CALIBRATION: THE MOST OVERLOOKED PART OF EVALUATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A model is calibrated if, among predictions of 0.7, about 70% are positive.
Ranking quality (AUC) and calibration are independent: a perfectly ranked model
can be badly calibrated, and recalibrating never changes AUC.

You need calibration whenever the probability itself feeds a decision:
expected-value calculations, pricing, risk scores shown to humans, thresholds
derived from costs, or combining model outputs with other estimates.

Measuring it:
    - reliability diagram: bin predictions, plot mean predicted vs observed
      frequency; the diagonal is perfect. Look at the shape, not just the score.
    - Expected Calibration Error (ECE): weighted mean gap across bins. Sensitive
      to binning choices; report the bin count.
    - Brier score = mean squared error of probabilities. A PROPER scoring rule,
      minimised only by the true probabilities, and it decomposes into
      reliability, resolution and uncertainty. Log loss is the other standard
      proper score, and it punishes confident mistakes much harder (an infinite
      penalty for a confident wrong prediction at probability 0 or 1) - which is
      why log loss is the better training loss and Brier the friendlier report.

Who is miscalibrated and why:
    Logistic regression      naturally well calibrated (it optimises log loss)
    SVM                      outputs distances, not probabilities
    Naive Bayes              extreme, over-confident probabilities because of
                             the independence assumption
    Random forest            pulled toward the middle by averaging
    Boosted trees            over-confident at the extremes
    Deep nets                systematically over-confident, worse with more
                             capacity
    Any model after resampling or class weighting - the base rate is shifted

Fixes, fitted on a held-out calibration set (never on the training data):
    Platt scaling        fit a logistic regression on the model's scores.
                         Parametric, works with little data, assumes a sigmoid
                         shape of the distortion.
    Isotonic regression  fit a non-decreasing step function. More flexible, can
                         fix any monotone distortion, needs more data (>= a few
                         thousand) or it overfits.
    Temperature scaling  single-parameter softmax scaling for neural nets; keeps
                         the argmax unchanged, so accuracy is untouched.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. REGRESSION, RANKING, AND OTHER TASK TYPES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Regression (details in the Linear_Regression note):
    MAE      robust, in target units, optimised by the conditional MEDIAN
    RMSE     penalises large errors, optimised by the conditional MEAN, in units
    MSE      same ordering as RMSE, not in units
    MAPE     scale-free but explodes near zero and is asymmetric (it punishes
             over-prediction more); sMAPE and MASE are the usual repairs
    R^2      fraction of variance explained relative to the mean predictor; can
             be negative; adjusted R^2 penalises extra features
    Quantile / pinball loss   the right metric when over- and under-prediction
             have different costs, and the way to produce prediction intervals

Ranking / recommendation:
    Precision@k, Recall@k, MAP, MRR, NDCG@k (position-discounted, handles graded
    relevance), coverage and diversity as guardrails.

Clustering (no labels): silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz;
with labels: ARI, NMI. See ../../Clustering/.

Probabilistic forecasting: CRPS, pinball loss, interval coverage.

Generative / LLM systems: task-specific automatic metrics are weak; use
human/LLM-as-judge with a rubric, pairwise preference, and a fixed golden set.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. REPORTING WITHOUT MISLEADING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    - always alongside the baseline (Tier 0/1/2 from 06_...) and the ceiling
    - always with an uncertainty estimate (bootstrap CI on the test set)
    - always with slice results, not only the aggregate (10_error_analysis)
    - state the threshold, the base rate, and the test-set size
    - offline metric vs online metric: state the expected chain from 01_..., and
      treat a discrepancy as information about the framing, not as noise
    - beware Simpson's paradox: a model can be better overall and worse on every
      segment (or the reverse) when segment sizes differ between the compared
      populations
    - beware Goodhart's law: once a metric becomes the target, it stops being a
      good measure. Guardrails exist for this reason.
