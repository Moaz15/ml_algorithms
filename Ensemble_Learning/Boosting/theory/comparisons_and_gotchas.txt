BOOSTING - 1.8 COMPARISONS AND INTERVIEW GOTCHAS
================================================================================
The last file in the boosting track. Nothing new is introduced here - this is
where the earlier files get turned into answers you can say out loud in under
two minutes.

HOW TO USE THIS FILE
--------------------
Each section is a question a panel actually asks, followed by the short answer,
then the reasoning, then the follow-up they will ask next. If you can only
revise one file before an interview, revise this one and foundations.txt.


================================================================================
1) RANDOM FOREST VS GRADIENT BOOSTED TREES
================================================================================

1.1 The short answer
--------------------
"Both are tree ensembles, but they attack opposite problems. Random Forest
averages independent deep trees to kill VARIANCE. Boosting sums dependent
shallow trees to kill BIAS. That single difference explains every other
difference between them."

1.2 The table
-------------
                           RANDOM FOREST            GBDT
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------
    Trees are              independent, parallel    sequential, dependent
    Attacks                variance                 bias
    Base tree              deep, unpruned           shallow (depth 3-8)
    Accuracy (tuned)       good                     usually BETTER on tabular
    Accuracy (untuned)     good                     can be much worse
    Tuning sensitivity     forgiving (n_estimators  sensitive (nu, M, depth,
                           and mtry, roughly)       subsample, penalties)
    Risk of overfitting    low; more trees is safe  real; more trees can hurt
    Training               embarrassingly parallel  sequential rounds
    Training cost          many deep trees          many cheap shallow trees,
                                                    often FASTER in wall-clock
                                                    with hist methods
    Inference cost         deep trees, slower       shallow trees, faster
    Noisy labels           robust                   fragile (chases them)
    Free validation        OOB score, no split      none; needs a real split
    Probabilities          decent, often under-     good with log loss, but
                           confident at the extremes see failure_modes.txt 5
    Number of knobs        few                      many

1.3 What to ship
----------------
    SHIP GBDT when: accuracy matters, labels are reasonably clean, and you have
    the time and the validation discipline to tune and monitor it. This is most
    tabular production systems.

    SHIP RANDOM FOREST when: you need a strong result with almost no tuning,
    the labels are noisy, you want OOB estimates without a holdout, or the
    model will be maintained by people who will not retune it. It is also the
    better BASELINE - if your boosted model cannot beat a default RF, something
    is wrong with your setup, not with boosting.

1.4 The follow-up
-----------------
"If GBDT is usually more accurate, why does anyone still use Random Forest?"
Because the gap after tuning is often small, RF has far fewer ways to go
wrong, and a model that nobody can break is worth more than two points of AUC
that depend on a validation split somebody will eventually misuse.


================================================================================
2) WHY BOOSTING WANTS SHALLOW TREES AND RANDOM FOREST WANTS DEEP ONES
================================================================================

2.1 The short answer
--------------------
"Each ensemble should supply what its combination rule cannot fix. Averaging
removes variance but not bias, so RF needs trees with LOW BIAS - grow them deep.
Summing sequentially removes bias but has no mechanism to remove variance, so
boosting needs trees with LOW VARIANCE - keep them shallow."

2.2 The reasoning, both directions
----------------------------------
    RANDOM FOREST
      Combination rule: average B independent trees.
      What averaging fixes:        variance (foundations.txt section 1)
      What averaging cannot fix:   bias - E[average] = E[one tree]
      Therefore:                   the base tree must have low bias already
      Low bias means:              DEEP, unpruned, fit the training data hard
      The resulting variance is fine, because averaging is about to remove it.

    BOOSTING
      Combination rule: add trees sequentially, each fitted to the remainder.
      What the sequence fixes:     bias - that is what each round reduces
      What the sequence cannot fix: variance - trees are SUMMED, not averaged,
                                   so a noisy tree's mistake stays in the model
      Therefore:                   the base tree must have low variance already
      Low variance means:          SHALLOW, high-bias, deliberately inadequate
      The resulting bias is fine, because the next 500 rounds will remove it.

2.3 The second reason, specific to boosting
-------------------------------------------
A deep tree would fit the current pseudo-residuals almost exactly in round 1,
leaving nothing for round 2 (foundations.txt section 4.1). Boosting requires
each step to be insufficient. There is no analogous requirement in bagging -
each tree is meant to be as good as possible.

2.4 Why this question is asked so often
---------------------------------------
Because it is impossible to answer correctly by memorisation. Either you
understand that averaging and summing fix different things, or you do not.


================================================================================
3) ADABOOST VS GRADIENT BOOSTING: IS ONE A SPECIAL CASE OF THE OTHER?
================================================================================

3.1 The short answer
--------------------
"Essentially yes. Both are forward stagewise additive models. AdaBoost is the
instance you get with exponential loss, and gradient boosting generalises the
scheme to any differentiable loss by fitting each tree to the negative gradient.
They are not line-by-line identical implementations, but AdaBoost is correctly
described as the exponential-loss case."

3.2 The side-by-side
--------------------
                        ADABOOST                 GRADIENT BOOSTING
    -----------------------------------------------------------------------
    Framework           forward stagewise        forward stagewise
                        additive modelling       additive modelling
    Loss                exponential, fixed       ANY differentiable loss
                        exp(-y*F)
    How the next
    learner is steered  REWEIGHT the rows        fit to the NEGATIVE GRADIENT
                        (w = exp(-y*F))          (pseudo-residuals)
    Learner weight      alpha from a closed      shrinkage nu, plus per-leaf
                        form, exact line search  values solved against the loss
    Labels              {-1, +1}                 anything (real, counts,
                                                 probabilities, quantiles)
    Robustness          poor (exponential loss)  depends on the chosen loss
    Typical base        decision stumps          depth 3-8 trees

3.3 The nuance, if they push
----------------------------
The reweighting and the gradient view coincide under exponential loss: the
weight exp(-y*F_(m-1)) IS the exponential loss evaluated at the current model,
and the pseudo-residual of exponential loss is y*exp(-y*F), which points in the
same direction. The differences that remain are mechanical - AdaBoost solves
for alpha exactly, while gradient boosting takes a fixed shrinkage step and
re-solves leaf values; and AdaBoost's base learner is fitted to weighted
CLASSIFICATION, while gradient boosting always fits a REGRESSION tree to
real-valued targets.

3.4 The follow-up
-----------------
"So why did gradient boosting replace it?" Because fixing the loss to
exponential fixes three things at once: you cannot do regression, you cannot
get calibrated probabilities, and you inherit exponential loss's extreme
fragility to mislabeled rows (adaboost.txt section 4). Freeing the loss removed
all three limits in one move.


================================================================================
4) GRADIENT BOOSTING VS DEEP LEARNING ON TABULAR DATA
================================================================================

4.1 The short answer
--------------------
"On small-to-medium heterogeneous tabular data, GBDT still usually wins, and
with far less tuning. Deep learning takes over when the data is very large,
when categoricals are high-cardinality enough to want learned embeddings, when
the tabular data has to be fused with text or images, or when you need
pretraining or an end-to-end differentiable pipeline."

4.2 Why trees win on tabular
----------------------------
  - TABULAR TARGETS ARE OFTEN NON-SMOOTH. Real functions have cliffs - a credit
    policy changes at a threshold, a shipping cost jumps at a weight boundary.
    Trees are built from steps and model that for free. MLPs are biased toward
    smooth functions and have to spend capacity approximating a jump.
  - IRRELEVANT FEATURES ARE FREE. A tree simply never splits on a useless
    column. A dense layer multiplies every input by a weight and must learn to
    zero it out, which costs data.
  - NO PREPROCESSING. Mixed scales, skewed distributions, monotone transforms:
    all irrelevant to a tree. Neural nets need normalisation to train at all.
  - ROTATION INVARIANCE IS THE WRONG PRIOR. An MLP is roughly invariant to
    rotations of the feature space, but in tabular data the individual COLUMNS
    are meaningful - "age" is a real thing and "0.3*age + 0.7*income" is not.
    Trees are axis-aligned, which matches the data. (Grinsztajn et al., 2022
    make this the central argument.)
  - MISSING VALUES AND CATEGORIES are handled natively (xgboost.txt section 6,
    lightgbm_catboost.txt section 5).

4.3 When deep learning wins
---------------------------
  - Very large datasets (tens of millions of rows and up) where the extra
    capacity can be fed.
  - High-cardinality categoricals where learned EMBEDDINGS capture similarity
    between levels - trees treat levels as unordered symbols with no notion of
    "these two products are alike".
  - MULTIMODAL inputs: tabular + free text + images in one model. Trees cannot
    do this; you would need a separate encoder anyway, at which point an
    end-to-end net is simpler.
  - TRANSFER LEARNING / PRETRAINING across related tables or tasks.
  - When the model must be differentiable end to end - as part of a larger
    network, or for gradient-based downstream optimisation.
  - Sequence structure over time per entity, where an RNN/transformer beats
    hand-rolled lag features.

4.4 The honest framing
----------------------
Specialised tabular architectures (TabNet, FT-Transformer, SAINT, NODE) have
been published for years, each claiming to beat GBDT. Independent benchmarks
(Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2022; Grinsztajn et al. 2022) repeatedly find that a tuned
GBDT matches or beats them on most datasets, and costs much less to tune.

The professional answer: "Start with GBDT. It is the baseline, and on tabular
data the baseline usually wins. Reach for deep learning when you have a
specific reason - scale, embeddings, multimodality or transfer - not because it
is newer."

5) WHAT IF THE BASE LEARNER IS LINEAR INSTEAD OF A TREE?
================================================================================

5.1 The short answer
--------------------
"The ensemble collapses. A sum of linear models is itself a linear model, so
boosting linear learners gives you no extra expressiveness - what you actually
get is a regularized linear model fitted by stagewise coordinate descent."

5.2 The algebra
---------------
    F(x) = (a_1 + b_1 x) + (a_2 + b_2 x) + ... + (a_M + b_M x)
         = (SUM a_m) + (SUM b_m) x
         = A + B x

A hundred boosting rounds produce exactly one intercept and one slope. Compare
with trees, where summing step functions genuinely creates a new, far richer
function class.

5.3 So what IS it good for
--------------------------
The thing you get is not useless - it is a DIFFERENT ALGORITHM than you thought:

  - If each round fits the single best feature to the residual and adds a small
    multiple of it, you are doing stagewise coordinate descent, whose solution
    path closely approximates the LASSO path (the point Hastie et al. make,
    also mentioned in foundations.txt section 3). You get L1-like feature
    selection for free.
  - XGBoost exposes this as booster='gblinear'. It is occasionally useful on
    very high-dimensional sparse linear problems (text-like data) where trees
    struggle.
  - It EXTRAPOLATES, which trees cannot (failure_modes.txt section 3). That is
    sometimes exactly why you would want a linear component - typically as the
    trend part of a hybrid model with trees on the residuals.

5.4 What you lose
-----------------
Interactions (a linear model has none unless you build them by hand),
automatic nonlinearity, native missing-value handling, and invariance to
monotone feature transforms. Which is to say: everything that made boosted
TREES the default.

5.5 The general lesson
----------------------
Boosting is a META-ALGORITHM, and its value depends entirely on whether summing
the base learner's outputs produces a richer function class than the base
learner itself. Step functions: yes, enormously. Linear functions: no, not at
all. That is the real reason trees and boosting are paired everywhere.

6) EXPLAINING XGBOOST'S EDGE OVER PLAIN GRADIENT BOOSTING IN TWO MINUTES
================================================================================

6.1 The two-minute version
--------------------------
"Same algorithm, better objective and much better engineering. Five things:

 1. A REGULARIZED OBJECTIVE. Classic GBM controls complexity from outside with
    max_depth and a learning rate. XGBoost puts gamma-per-leaf and an L2 penalty
    on leaf weights INSIDE the objective, so complexity is priced into every
    decision the tree makes.

 2. SECOND-ORDER OPTIMISATION. It Taylor-expands the loss to second order, so
    each round uses the gradient AND the hessian. That makes the leaf value a
    Newton step, w* = -G/(H+lambda), and makes any twice-differentiable loss
    pluggable - the tree code only ever sees g and h.

 3. A SPLIT CRITERION DERIVED FROM THAT OBJECTIVE. Instead of Gini or variance
    reduction, the gain is the improvement in the regularized objective itself,
    minus gamma. Pruning and stopping fall out of the same formula.

 4. SPARSITY-AWARE SPLITTING. Missing values are not imputed; every split
    learns a default direction by trying both sides. It preserves the signal in
    missingness and skips the zeros, which is a large speedup on sparse data.

 5. SYSTEMS WORK. Pre-sorted column blocks computed once and reused by every
    node of every tree, parallel split finding across columns, cache-aware
    gradient prefetching, and out-of-core blocks. That is where the 10x came
    from."

6.2 The honest closing line
---------------------------
"The accuracy gain over a well-tuned classic GBM is real but modest. The speed
gain was enormous, and that is what mattered in practice - being 10x faster
means you try 10x more ideas, and the ideas are usually worth more than the
algorithm."

6.3 The follow-up
-----------------
"And what did LightGBM change after that?" Histogram binning, leaf-wise growth,
GOSS and EFB - all attacks on the cost of finding a split
(lightgbm_catboost.txt). "And CatBoost?" Ordered target statistics and ordered
boosting - fixes for two leakage/bias problems - plus oblivious trees for fast
inference.


================================================================================
7) WHAT CAN STILL BE PARALLELISED WHEN THE TREES ARE SEQUENTIAL
================================================================================

7.1 The short answer
--------------------
"The ROUNDS cannot be parallelised, because tree m's targets are defined by
trees 1..m-1. Everything inside a round can be, and in practice that is enough
to saturate a machine."

7.2 What is strictly sequential
-------------------------------
Exactly one thing: the sequence of rounds. Tree 300's pseudo-residuals do not
exist until tree 299 is finished. That is a genuine data dependency, so the
wall-clock floor is (number of rounds) x (time for one tree), and no amount of
hardware removes it.

7.3 What is parallel
--------------------
    WITHIN ONE TREE
      - split finding across FEATURES (XGBoost's column blocks exist for this)
      - split finding across candidate thresholds / histogram bins
      - histogram construction per feature, then a reduce
      - sibling nodes at the same depth are independent of each other

    ACROSS ROWS
      - computing g_i and h_i - one independent calculation per row, so it
        parallelises across cores and across machines
      - distributed training: each worker builds partial histograms on its
        shard, then an allreduce combines them. Only the histograms cross the
        network, not the data.

    ACROSS TREES, WHERE THEY ARE NOT DEPENDENT
      - MULTICLASS: the K trees of a single round are independent of each other
        (gradient_boosting.txt section 7.3)
      - INFERENCE: F(x) = SUM_m h_m(x), so at prediction time all M trees are
        independent and can be evaluated in any order

    AROUND THE FIT
      - cross-validation folds
      - hyperparameter search trials (usually the best use of extra machines)

7.4 The contrast to state
-------------------------
"Random Forest parallelises across trees and scales nearly linearly with cores.
Boosting parallelises within trees instead. The practical consequence is not
that boosting is slow - a histogram-based GBDT trains a shallow tree very
quickly - it is that you cannot buy your way past the serial chain by adding
machines, so the learning rate and round count are compute decisions, not just
accuracy decisions."

8) RAPID-FIRE GOTCHAS
================================================================================

Short traps that come up as follow-ups. One line each, with where to read more.

  - "Boosting cannot overfit." FALSE. Training loss falls forever; validation
    traces a U. (failure_modes.txt section 1)

  - Early stopping on the test set. That is leakage - the test set has now
    chosen a hyperparameter. Use train / validation / test.
    (regularization_and_tuning.txt section 2.4)

  - Lowering the learning rate without raising n_estimators. That is
    underfitting, not regularization. They trade off directly.
    (regularization_and_tuning.txt section 1.3)

  - Reporting "feature importance" without saying WHICH TYPE. weight, cover and
    gain give different rankings, and weight is biased toward high-cardinality
    features. (failure_modes.txt section 6)

  - Standardising or scaling features for a GBDT. Unnecessary. Trees are
    invariant to any monotone transform of a feature, so log(x) and x produce
    identical splits.

  - One-hot encoding a 2,000-level categorical. Memory explodes and every dummy
    is a near-useless split. (failure_modes.txt section 7)

  - Target encoding computed before the train/test split. Leakage, and your
    validation score is contaminated too.
    (lightgbm_catboost.txt section 5)

  - Expecting a GBDT to forecast above its training range. It physically
    cannot - the output is a sum of leaf constants.
    (failure_modes.txt section 3)

  - Using accuracy on a 0.5% positive rate. Predicting "no" everywhere scores
    99.5%. Use PR-AUC or expected cost. (failure_modes.txt section 4)

  - Leaving the decision threshold at 0.5 on imbalanced data. Tune it on
    validation; it often matters more than the model.
    (failure_modes.txt section 4.3)

  - Reading boosted scores as probabilities after setting scale_pos_weight.
    Weighting shifts the base rate and breaks calibration.
    (failure_modes.txt section 5)

  - Comparing a tuned GBDT to an untuned Random Forest and concluding boosting
    is better. Tune both or neither.

  - Setting num_leaves = 1024 with max_depth = 7 in LightGBM. Impossible; the
    depth cap silently wins. (lightgbm_catboost.txt section 2.4)

  - Deep trees in a boosted model. Depth is interaction order, and depth 12 in
    a GBDT usually means a different parameter is the real problem.
    (foundations.txt section 4.4)

  - Assuming the base learner must be a tree. It must only be something whose
    SUM is richer than itself - which rules out linear learners (section 5).


================================================================================
THE ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
================================================================================
Random Forest and GBDT differ in one thing from which everything else follows:
averaging independent deep trees removes variance, summing dependent shallow
trees removes bias - which is also why RF wants deep trees and boosting wants
shallow ones, and why more trees is always safe in one and eventually harmful in
the other. AdaBoost is the exponential-loss instance of the same stagewise
framework gradient boosting generalises, and freeing the loss is what bought
regression, calibrated probabilities and noise tolerance. On tabular data GBDT
still beats deep learning on most small-to-medium problems because trees handle
non-smooth targets, irrelevant columns and unscaled mixed features natively,
while neural nets win on scale, embeddings, multimodality and transfer. Boosting
a LINEAR learner collapses to a single linear model, which is the sharpest way
to see that boosting only pays off when summing the base learner creates a
richer function class. XGBoost's edge over classic GBM is a regularized
objective, second-order Newton steps, a split criterion derived from that
objective, sparsity-aware default directions, and a great deal of systems
engineering - with the speed mattering more in practice than the accuracy. And
while the rounds themselves are strictly sequential, split finding, histogram
building, gradient computation, the K trees of a multiclass round, inference,
and the whole tuning search all parallelise.
