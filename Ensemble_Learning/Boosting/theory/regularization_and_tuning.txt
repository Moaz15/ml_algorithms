BOOSTING - 1.4 REGULARIZATION AND TUNING
================================================================================
Boosting will fit your training set perfectly if you let it. This file is about
the knobs that stop it, and the order to turn them in.
Read foundations.txt and gradient_boosting.txt first.

THE WHOLE IDEA IN ONE STORY
---------------------------
Think of boosting as walking toward a target in the dark.

  - SHRINKAGE (nu) is how big each step is. Small steps are slower but you are
    much less likely to walk straight past the target.
  - NUMBER OF ROUNDS (M) is how many steps you take. Too few and you never
    arrive; too many and you walk past it.
  - SUBSAMPLING is closing one eye each step so you do not over-trust any single
    look at the data.
  - TREE SIZE LIMITS cap how far one single step is allowed to carry you.
  - L1/L2 PENALTIES pull every step slightly back toward zero.
  - CONSTRAINTS are a rule like "never walk downhill", enforced regardless of
    what the data seems to say.

Every regularizer in boosting is one of those six things. There is no seventh
idea hiding anywhere.


================================================================================
1) SHRINKAGE: THE LEARNING RATE
================================================================================

1.1 What it is
--------------
    F_m(x)  =  F_(m-1)(x)  +  nu * h_m(x)        with  0 < nu <= 1

nu (learning_rate, eta) says what fraction of each new tree you actually keep.
At nu = 0.1 you throw away 90% of every tree's advice.

1.2 Why throwing away 90% of the work helps
-------------------------------------------
Because each tree is fitted to a NOISY target. The pseudo-residuals contain both
real signal and the random quirks of your particular training sample. A tree
added at full strength commits to both. A tree added at 10% commits mostly to
the part that the next 50 trees will agree with - the random parts do not
reinforce each other, the real signal does.

It is the same reason you average 100 trees in a random forest rather than
trusting one, except here the averaging happens over SEQUENTIAL steps.

1.3 The trade against the number of trees
-----------------------------------------
nu and M are not independent knobs. They multiply.

    RULE OF THUMB: halve nu, and you need roughly twice as many trees.

    nu = 0.30   ->  ~  200 trees         fast, noticeably worse generalisation
    nu = 0.10   ->  ~  600 trees         the standard starting point
    nu = 0.05   ->  ~1,200 trees         typical for a final model
    nu = 0.01   ->  ~6,000 trees         when accuracy matters more than time

Lower nu is almost always better for ACCURACY and always worse for TRAINING
TIME. So the real decision is "how much compute am I willing to spend", and the
answer is usually 0.05 for a shipped model, 0.1 while iterating.

1.4 The trap
------------
Lowering nu without raising M makes the model WORSE, not better. You have
shortened the steps without taking more of them, so you stop short of the
target - that is plain underfitting. Never tune nu without early stopping
active (section 2).


================================================================================
2) EARLY STOPPING: THE HONEST WAY TO PICK n_estimators
================================================================================

2.1 Why M cannot be guessed
---------------------------
From foundations.txt section 5: training loss falls EVERY round by
construction. It carries zero information about when to stop. The right M
depends on nu, tree depth, dataset size, and noise level - all at once. Any
fixed number you hard-code is wrong for the next dataset.

2.2 The procedure
-----------------
    1. Split off a validation set the model never trains on.
    2. Set M very high (say 10,000) - you are not going to reach it.
    3. Evaluate the validation metric every round.
    4. If it has not improved for `early_stopping_rounds` consecutive rounds
       (patience, typically 50), stop.
    5. KEEP THE BEST ITERATION, not the last one.

        xgboost  : early_stopping_rounds=50, then model.best_iteration
        lightgbm : callbacks=[lgb.early_stopping(50)], model.best_iteration_
        sklearn  : n_iter_no_change=50, validation_fraction=0.1

2.3 Why patience exists
-----------------------
The validation curve is noisy. It goes up for 8 rounds and then down for 30.
Stopping at the first uptick would cut the model off far too early. Patience of
50 says "I need 50 consecutive rounds of no improvement before I believe it".

2.4 The traps
-------------
  - EARLY STOPPING ON THE TEST SET IS LEAKAGE. You have used the test set to
    choose a hyperparameter, so it is no longer an unbiased estimate. You need
    three splits: train / validation (for stopping) / test (touched once).
  - Early stopping on a RANK-ONLY metric like AUC optimises ranking, not
    calibration. If you need probabilities, stop on log loss. See
    failure_modes.txt section 5.
  - After tuning everything, it is common to retrain on train+validation using
    the M that early stopping found (sometimes scaled up slightly for the extra
    data). Doing this gives up your stopping signal, so only do it last.
  - With cross-validation, use xgb.cv / lgb.cv which gives you the best round
    averaged over folds, rather than early-stopping each fold separately.


================================================================================
3) ROW AND COLUMN SUBSAMPLING
================================================================================

3.1 What they are
-----------------
    subsample / bagging_fraction  = fraction of ROWS used to grow each tree
                                    (typical 0.5 - 0.8)
    colsample_bytree / feature_fraction  = fraction of COLUMNS offered per tree
    colsample_bylevel                    = per depth level
    colsample_bynode                     = per split

Friedman called row subsampling STOCHASTIC GRADIENT BOOSTING (1999). He found
it improved accuracy, not just speed - the randomness is a genuine regularizer.

3.2 How this differs from bagging's bootstrap
---------------------------------------------
Both sample rows. They are not the same thing.

                          BAGGING / RF              BOOSTING
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------
    Sampling              WITH replacement          WITHOUT replacement
    Sample size           100% of N (with dupes)    50 - 80% of N
    Fresh sample          per tree, independent     per ROUND, on top of a
                                                    sequence that already
                                                    depends on all prior rounds
    Purpose               make trees independent    perturb the gradient step
                          so averaging works        so the model cannot
                                                    over-commit
    Trees afterwards      still independent         still fully dependent
    Free validation       yes - OOB rows            no, not reliably

The key conceptual difference: in bagging, resampling is the MECHANISM - without
it the trees would be identical and averaging would do nothing. In boosting,
resampling is purely a REGULARIZER bolted onto a mechanism that already works.
Boosting with subsample=1.0 is a perfectly sensible algorithm; bagging with
"bootstrap off and 100% of rows" is just one tree, 500 times.

3.3 Column subsampling
----------------------
Hiding features forces the model to use alternatives instead of leaning on the
same dominant feature in every tree. It directly decorrelates the trees - the
same reason Random Forest does it, used here for the same reason. It also speeds
up split finding proportionally.

Practical: colsample_bytree 0.6-0.9 is a near-free win when you have many
features; 1.0 is fine when you have fewer than ~20.


================================================================================
4) TREE-SIZE LIMITS
================================================================================

These cap the complexity of each individual step.

4.1 max_depth
-------------
Maximum levels. Typical 3-8 for boosting. Remember from foundations.txt section
4: DEPTH IS INTERACTION ORDER. depth 3 means "at most 3 features may conspire".
A depth of 10+ in a boosted model usually means you are trying to fix an
underfitting problem with the wrong knob.

4.2 num_leaves (LightGBM)
-------------------------
LightGBM grows leaf-wise, so depth is not the natural limit - leaf count is.
The constraint to respect:

    num_leaves  <  2^max_depth

If you set num_leaves = 1024 with max_depth = 7 (max 128 leaves), the depth cap
silently wins and your num_leaves setting is a lie. Default 31 is a sane start;
this is THE main overfitting knob in LightGBM.

4.3 min_child_weight (XGBoost)
------------------------------
Despite the name, it is not a row count. It is the MINIMUM SUM OF HESSIANS in a
leaf. Why that matters:

    squared loss  ->  h_i = 1 for every row, so the sum IS the row count.
                      min_child_weight = 20 means "at least 20 rows".

    log loss      ->  h_i = p(1-p), which is 0.25 at p=0.5 and ~0.01 at p=0.99.
                      So 20 confidently-predicted rows contribute only ~0.2,
                      while 20 uncertain rows contribute 5.0.

So under log loss this parameter means "a leaf must contain enough UNRESOLVED
signal to be worth creating", which is a smarter criterion than a raw count.
Raising it is one of the most effective anti-overfitting moves in XGBoost.

4.4 min_samples_leaf / min_data_in_leaf / min_child_samples
-----------------------------------------------------------
The plain row count version. Typical 20-100; raise it hard on noisy data. On a
1M-row dataset, leaves of 20 rows are memorising.

4.5 How these interact
----------------------
They are three ways to say the same thing (make the trees simpler), so do not
crank all three. Pick depth/num_leaves as the primary capacity dial, and one
minimum-size parameter as the safety net.


================================================================================
5) L1 AND L2 PENALTIES ON THE LEAF WEIGHTS
================================================================================

5.1 Where they act
------------------
Not on feature coefficients - boosted trees have none. They act on the LEAF
OUTPUT VALUES. The optimal leaf value in XGBoost (derived in xgboost.txt) is:

    w*  =  -G / (H + lambda)

where G and H are the sums of gradients and hessians in that leaf, and lambda
is reg_lambda (L2).

5.2 What lambda actually does, numerically
------------------------------------------
Take a leaf with G = -20, H = 10:

    lambda =  0   ->  w* = 20/10   = 2.00      (unpenalised)
    lambda =  5   ->  w* = 20/15   = 1.33
    lambda = 10   ->  w* = 20/20   = 1.00
    lambda = 40   ->  w* = 20/50   = 0.40

It is a soft brake on every leaf output, and it bites HARDEST on leaves with
small H - that is, leaves built from few or already-confident rows. Exactly the
leaves most likely to be fitting noise. That is the elegant part: one parameter,
and it automatically penalises the untrustworthy leaves more.

5.3 The three penalties in XGBoost
----------------------------------
    reg_lambda (L2)   default 1. Shrinks leaf values smoothly. Safe to raise
                      (try 1, 5, 20, 100). The main one.
    reg_alpha  (L1)   default 0. Soft-thresholds leaf values, can set them to
                      exactly 0. Useful with very many features / sparse data.
    gamma             default 0. Minimum GAIN a split must earn to be kept -
                      see xgboost.txt section 4. A pruning knob, not a weight
                      penalty. Try 0, 0.1, 1, 5.

LightGBM calls them lambda_l2, lambda_l1, min_gain_to_split. CatBoost exposes
l2_leaf_reg.

5.4 Honest expectation
----------------------
These usually move your metric by a little. Learning rate, tree size and feature
quality move it by a lot. Tune them last, and do not be surprised when the
default lambda=1 wins.


================================================================================
6) MONOTONIC AND INTERACTION CONSTRAINTS
================================================================================

These are different from everything above: they are PRIORS YOU FORCE ON THE
MODEL, not penalties. You are not nudging the model, you are declaring certain
models illegal.

6.1 Monotonic constraints
-------------------------
The example that makes it obvious: you are pricing houses, and your model has
learned - from 8 noisy sales - that houses between 2,400 and 2,600 sqft are
cheaper than houses at 2,300 sqft. That is noise, and no customer will accept
"your house is worth less because it is bigger".

    monotone_constraints = [1, 0, -1, 0, ...]      one entry per feature
         1  = prediction must be NON-DECREASING in this feature
        -1  = prediction must be NON-INCREASING
         0  = unconstrained

Real cases:
    price      must not DECREASE with square footage        ->  +1
    default risk must not DECREASE with debt-to-income      ->  +1
    insurance premium must not INCREASE with safety rating  ->  -1

How it is enforced: when considering a split on a constrained feature, the
library rejects any split whose child values would violate the direction, and it
clamps descendant leaf values so later splits cannot sneak around it.

Why you would want it:
  - REGULATORY / EXPLAINABILITY. Credit and insurance models often must be
    monotone in key variables to be approved.
  - It is free regularization. A true domain fact injected as a hard constraint
    removes a whole family of noise-fitting models, so variance drops.
  - Trust. One nonsensical reversal in a demo destroys confidence in the model.

Cost: a small drop in training fit, and it is WRONG if the true relationship is
genuinely non-monotone (age vs risk is often U-shaped - do not constrain that).

6.2 Interaction constraints
---------------------------
Declare which features are allowed to appear together in the same tree branch.

    interaction_constraints = [[0, 1, 2], [3, 4]]

means features 0,1,2 may interact with each other, 3 and 4 may interact with
each other, and nothing crosses between the groups.

Uses:
  - Force an ADDITIVE model: put every feature in its own group, and the
    ensemble becomes f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + ... which you can plot feature by
    feature. Same effect as depth 1, but you keep depth for within-group splits.
  - Block nonsense interactions you know are spurious (e.g. do not let
    customer_id interact with anything).
  - Enforce known structure, like "time features interact with each other, not
    with product features".

Both of these cost you accuracy on the training set and often BUY accuracy on
the test set, because a correct prior is worth more than the data it replaces.


================================================================================
7) A TUNING ORDER THAT WORKS
================================================================================

7.1 The order
-------------
    STEP 0  Set up early stopping with a real validation split. Without this
            nothing below is measurable. Fix nu = 0.1 for the whole search.

    STEP 1  CAPACITY. Tune max_depth (3,4,5,6,8) or num_leaves (15,31,63,127).
            This is the single biggest lever. Then set a minimum leaf size
            (min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf) to stop tiny leaves.

    STEP 2  RANDOMNESS. Tune subsample (0.6,0.8,1.0) and colsample_bytree
            (0.6,0.8,1.0). Cheap, usually a small but reliable gain.

    STEP 3  PENALTIES. Tune reg_lambda (1,5,20,100) and gamma (0,0.1,1,5).
            Expect small effects. Skip if time is short.

    STEP 4  FINAL MODEL. Drop nu to 0.03-0.05, keep everything else, re-run with
            early stopping. Accept the longer training time once, at the end.

7.2 Why this order
------------------
Each step is roughly independent of the ones below it but NOT of the ones above
it. Capacity decides how much the randomness and penalties have to rein in, so
tuning penalties before capacity means redoing them. And nu is held fixed during
the search because changing it changes the optimal M for every single
configuration, making the comparisons meaningless.

7.3 Which knobs actually move the number
----------------------------------------
    BIG EFFECT
      - feature engineering (not a hyperparameter, and still the biggest lever)
      - learning rate x number of trees, with honest early stopping
      - tree capacity: max_depth / num_leaves
      - the LOSS FUNCTION, if the default is wrong for your target
        (see gradient_boosting.txt section 6)

    MODERATE
      - min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf, especially on noisy data
      - subsample and colsample
      - scale_pos_weight on imbalanced data (failure_modes.txt section 4)

    SMALL - usually not worth a grid search
      - reg_alpha, reg_lambda, gamma
      - max_bin, tree_method
      - everything else

7.4 Practical notes
-------------------
  - Use random search or Bayesian optimisation (Optuna), not an exhaustive
    grid - the response surface is flat in most directions, so a grid wastes
    almost all its evaluations.
  - Tune on CV folds, not a single split, unless the dataset is large.
  - Stop when the improvements are smaller than the fold-to-fold noise. A 0.001
    AUC gain on one split is not a gain.
  - A reasonable default config beats a badly tuned one: nu=0.05, depth 6,
    subsample 0.8, colsample 0.8, early stopping on. Start there.


================================================================================
THE ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
================================================================================
Boosting is regularized in six ways, and they are all "take smaller or less
confident steps": shrinkage keeps a fraction nu of each tree so the noise in any
one tree never fully lands, the number of rounds is chosen by early stopping on
a validation set because training loss falls forever and tells you nothing, row
and column subsampling perturb each step (unlike bagging's bootstrap, this is a
regularizer rather than the mechanism, and it is sampling without replacement),
tree-size limits cap what one step can express - with depth as interaction order
and min_child_weight as a sum of hessians rather than a row count - and L1/L2
penalties shrink the leaf outputs themselves, biting hardest on the leaves built
from the least evidence. Monotonic and interaction constraints are a different
species: hard priors that declare whole families of model illegal, which costs
training fit and often buys test accuracy and regulatory approval. Tune in the
order capacity, then randomness, then penalties, with nu fixed at 0.1 and early
stopping always on, and lower nu only for the final model - and accept that tree
depth, the learning-rate/rounds pair and the choice of loss will move your
metric far more than any penalty term.
