BOOSTING - 1.1 FOUNDATIONS
================================================================================
THE WHOLE IDEA IN ONE STORY
---------------------------
You are preparing for an exam.

  Bagging:  take 100 practice tests at once and average the scores. A more
            stable estimate of how you will do - but you never look at what you
            got wrong, so you do not actually get better.
  Boosting: take one test, mark the questions you got wrong, study ONLY those,
            take another test, mark the new mistakes. Repeat 500 times.

Bagging makes your performance less noisy; boosting makes you better. Boosting
is a long chain of small models, each trained on what the chain so far is still
getting wrong - why that is necessary (1), why the small models are allowed to
be bad (2), how the chain is built (3), what they should be (4), what it does to
overfitting (5), what it costs in training time (6).

Vocabulary used throughout:
  base learner / weak learner  - the small model added at each step (a tree)
  round / stage / iteration    - one step of the chain (one new tree)
  ensemble                     - the running sum of all trees so far
  residual / error             - how far the ensemble still is from the truth
  M (or n_estimators)          - total number of rounds
  nu (or learning_rate)        - how much of each new tree we actually keep

================================================================================
1) THE VARIANCE FLOOR: WHAT AVERAGING CANNOT FIX
================================================================================

1.1 The instability, and why averaging helps
--------------------------------------------
Predicting house prices, true price 500k. Train ONE decision tree on 2,000 past
sales, then retrain it on different random samples of those sales:

    sample 1 -> 512k    sample 2 -> 478k    sample 3 -> 531k    sample 4 -> 465k

Tens of thousands of swing purely from which rows were drawn. That is VARIANCE:
the tree being unstable, not the market. Average the four and you get 496.5k,
much closer to 500k than most of the individuals. That is bagging, and it
genuinely works.

1.2 Why it stops working
------------------------
All four trees read bootstrap samples of the SAME 2,000 sales, so any two share
about 63% of their rows. They are not four independent opinions - they are four
slightly different readings of the same book. If those 2,000 sales happen to
contain almost no houses near the new metro line, then EVERY tree under-prices
houses near the metro line, and averaging 500 trees that all make the same
mistake just gives you that mistake.

    Averaging only cancels the errors the models DISAGREE on.

1.3 Splitting a tree's miss into two parts
------------------------------------------
Every miss (true price 500k) is two different things added together:

    THE SHARED PART  - the mistake EVERY tree makes, because it comes from the
                       training data they all read: the metro-line problem from
                       1.2. Nobody disagrees about it. Say it is -10 for all.
    THE PRIVATE PART - the mistake THIS tree makes on its own, from which rows
                       its bootstrap sample grabbed and which splits it picked.

    tree 1:  +12  =  (-10 shared)  +  (+22 private)
    tree 2:  -22  =  (-10 shared)  +  (-12 private)
    tree 3:  +31  =  (-10 shared)  +  (+41 private)
    tree 4:  -35  =  (-10 shared)  +  (-25 private)

Average the four and watch the two parts separately:

    PRIVATE:  +22, -12, +41, -25. Mixed signs, so they partly cancel: their
              average is +6.5, much smaller than any one of them, and they
              cancel further as more trees are added.
    SHARED:   -10, -10, -10, -10. Averaging four copies of -10 gives you -10.
              It does not move AT ALL.

That is the entire idea. AVERAGING IS A MACHINE THAT CANCELS DISAGREEMENT, and
the shared part is exactly the part the trees do not disagree about. No number
of trees can cancel it.

1.4 Now the formula writes itself
---------------------------------
Give the two parts names and the standard formula falls out with no algebra.

    sigma^2 = the total wobble of ONE tree (the spread of 512/478/531/465)
    rho     = the FRACTION of that wobble which is the shared part, in [0,1]

By the definition of "fraction", one tree's wobble splits as

        shared  piece  =       rho   * sigma^2
        private piece  =   (1 - rho) * sigma^2
                           ---------------------
        total          =             sigma^2

Now average B trees and apply 1.3 to each piece: the shared piece is identical
in every tree, so averaging leaves it exactly as it was, while the private piece
is B independent wobbles, and averaging B independent things divides variance
by B.

    Var( average of B trees )  =  rho * sigma^2  +  (1 - rho) * sigma^2 / B
                                  \___________/    \____________________/
                                   the VARIANCE      the only thing more
                                   FLOOR, untouched  trees can buy you

(One standard fact was used: averaging B INDEPENDENT things divides variance by
B - the usual "an average of many measurements is more stable than one". It
applies only to the independent part, which is the whole point of this section.)

    A note on rho: textbooks define rho as the CORRELATION between any two
    trees' predictions, not as "the fraction that is shared". Both give the same
    number - the only reason two trees move together is the part of the error
    they share. In bagging rho is high, since any two trees share about 63% of
    their rows; Random Forest's feature subsampling exists to push rho down.

1.5 Putting numbers in it (sigma^2 = 1)
---------------------------------------
    rho = 0.0, B = 100       ->  0.010      (the dream: independent trees)
    rho = 0.6, B = 10        ->  0.600 + 0.400/10     = 0.640
    rho = 0.6, B = 100       ->  0.600 + 0.400/100    = 0.604
    rho = 0.6, B = 10,000    ->  0.600 + 0.400/10,000 = 0.60004
    rho = 0.6, B = infinity  ->  0.600                <- the floor

Six tenths of each tree's wobble is the shared mistake, so 0.600 of the variance
is untouchable from the start and all the trees in the world only ever chip away
at the remaining 0.400. Going from 100 trees to 10,000 - a hundred times the
compute - improves the variance by 0.004. By 100 trees you are already sitting
on the floor.

That is why Random Forest subsamples FEATURES. It is not trying to add more
trees to the second term; it is trying to make rho smaller, which lowers the
floor itself. Drop rho from 0.6 to 0.3 and the floor halves - worth far more
than any number of extra trees.

1.6 The part the formula does not show, and the real reason boosting exists
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
    E[ average of B trees ]  =  E[ one tree ]

Averaging does not change the EXPECTED prediction at all: whatever the base
model systematically gets wrong, the ensemble systematically gets wrong. Make it
concrete. The truth is curved - price rises steeply up to 2,000 sqft and then
flattens - but your base model is a straight-line fit. Bag 1,000 of them on
1,000 bootstrap samples. Each is a straight line, and the average of 1,000
straight lines is a straight line. It still cannot bend. You have removed the
wobble and kept the error.

That gap between what your model can express and the truth is BIAS, and:

    bagging   ->  pushes the variance term down, leaves bias exactly where it is
    boosting  ->  pushes the bias down, and pays for it with variance

Two different diseases, two different medicines.

2) WEAK LEARNERS: BEATING RANDOM GUESSING IS ENOUGH
================================================================================

2.1 The example
---------------
Spam classification, with the dumbest possible model - one yes/no question:

    "Does the email contain the word FREE?"  -> if yes, spam; if no, not spam

Run it on your inbox: right 58% of the time, against coin-flipping's 50%. Almost
worthless - an edge of 8 percentage points over guessing. That one-question
model is a DECISION STUMP, and it is a textbook WEAK LEARNER.

2.2 The definition
------------------
A weak learner only has to satisfy:

    error  <=  0.5 - gamma       for some gamma > 0   (binary, balanced classes)

gamma is called the EDGE: how far below coin-flipping the model manages to get.
The FREE rule is wrong 42% of the time, so its edge is gamma = 0.08. That is the
entire bar. A STRONG learner, by contrast, is one you can push to arbitrarily
small error.

The founding result of the field (Schapire, 1990): WEAK AND STRONG LEARNABILITY
ARE EQUIVALENT. Anything that reliably beats a coin flip can be combined into a
model with arbitrarily small training error. Boosting is the recipe that does
it.

2.3 Why such a bad model is enough
----------------------------------
Because the next weak learner is not asked the same question on the same data.
It is asked to fix what is left over.

    Round 1: "contains FREE?"          -> 58% accurate.
             Now look only at the emails it got wrong. Many of those are real
             spam that never says FREE.
    Round 2: trained with those rows weighted up, the best question becomes
             "is the sender not in my contacts?"  -> catches a different slice.
    Round 3: among what is STILL wrong -> "more than 5 links?"
    Round 4: -> "subject in ALL CAPS?"

Each rule is individually terrible. Each one covers a slice the others missed.
The ensemble asks all of them and takes a weighted vote.

2.4 The wrong mental model, and the right one
---------------------------------------------
THE WRONG ONE (adding). "Each rule is 8 points better than a coin flip, so 200
rules are 200 x 8 = 1,600 points better." Obviously nonsense - accuracy stops at
100%. Edges do not add up.

THE RIGHT ONE (multiplying). A round does not remove a fixed AMOUNT of error, it
removes a fixed FRACTION OF WHATEVER ERROR IS LEFT - like a water filter taking
out 2% of the REMAINING dust on each pass:

    start          100.0% of the dust
    after 1 pass    98.0%          (2% of 100)
    after 2 passes  96.0%          (2% of 98, not another 2 of the original)
    after 10        81.7%
    after 50        36.4%
    after 200        1.8%

You do not compute "200 passes x 2% = 400% removed", you compute 0.98^200 - and
a number slightly below 1 multiplied by itself a few hundred times collapses.

WHY BOOSTING BEHAVES LIKE THE FILTER, AND NOT LIKE ADDITION
Because of the reweighting in 2.3: after round 1 the rows it got right are
discounted and the rows it got wrong carry the weight. (In AdaBoost this is
exact - the misclassified rows end up holding precisely half the total weight,
see adaboost.txt section 1.3.) So each round works on a smaller problem than the
one before it:

    round 1 attacks  100% of the difficulty, removes a slice
    round 2 attacks  WHAT REMAINS,           removes a slice of that
    round 3 attacks  WHAT REMAINS OF THAT,   ...

Fractions of fractions of fractions. That is a product, and a product of numbers
below 1 decays exponentially. THIS is why 8-point edges are enough: they do not
need to add up to 50, they need to multiply down to nothing.

2.5 The formula, and what it buys
---------------------------------
The precise version of the filter picture - one round multiplies the remaining
training error by a computable factor, and the shape of the table is the whole
story:

    factor for one round  =  2 * sqrt( err * (1 - err) )

    err = 0.50  ->  factor = 1.000     a coin flip removes NOTHING
    err = 0.45  ->  factor = 0.995
    err = 0.40  ->  factor = 0.980     our 60%-accurate rule
    err = 0.35  ->  factor = 0.954
    err = 0.30  ->  factor = 0.917
    err = 0.20  ->  factor = 0.800
    err = 0.10  ->  factor = 0.600
    err = 0.01  ->  factor = 0.199     a near-perfect rule ends it almost alone

At err = 0.5 the factor is exactly 1.0, so a useless learner leaves the error
untouched - and the algorithm agrees, giving that learner a vote of zero
(adaboost.txt section 2.1). "Beat a coin flip" is just "make the factor smaller
than 1". Multiply the factors across all rounds and you get AdaBoost's training
error bound:

    training error  <=  PROD_m  2 * sqrt( err_m * (1 - err_m) )

    60%-accurate rules (err = 0.40, factor 0.9798):
         50 rounds  =  0.9798^50   =  0.36      -> training error below 36%
        200 rounds  =  0.9798^200  =  0.017     -> training error below 1.7%
    65%-accurate rules (err = 0.35, factor 0.9539):
        200 rounds  =  0.9539^200  =  0.00008   -> training error below 0.01%

Two hundred rules, each barely better than guessing, classify the training set
essentially perfectly. And notice how much that tiny per-rule improvement gained
- which the tidier form of the bound explains:

    with err = 0.5 - gamma, the factor is exactly  sqrt( 1 - 4*gamma^2 )

    so       training error  <=  exp( -2 * SUM_m gamma_m^2 )

THE EDGE ENTERS SQUARED. Doubling the edge quadruples the exponent: gamma = 0.1
decays as exp(-0.02*M), gamma = 0.2 as exp(-0.08*M) - four times faster.
Slightly better weak learners are worth much more than slightly more of them.

2.6 The catch (say this in an interview)
----------------------------------------
That bound is about TRAINING error. It says nothing about test error - perfectly
fitting the training set is also what overfitting looks like. Why boosted models
often still generalise well is a separate argument (margins), in adaboost.txt.

One more practical reason weak is good: a weak learner is cheap, so you can
afford 1,000 rounds, and it cannot swallow all the signal in one step - which is
what keeps rounds 2..1000 worth running.


================================================================================
3) ADDITIVE STAGEWISE FITTING: ONE TERM AT A TIME, NEVER REVISITED
================================================================================

3.1 The shape of the model
--------------------------
A boosted model is a SUM of small models:

    F_M(x)  =  F_0(x)  +  nu*h_1(x)  +  nu*h_2(x)  +  ...  +  nu*h_M(x)

F_0 is a constant starting guess. Each h_m is one small tree. nu is the
LEARNING RATE (shrinkage), typically 0.01 to 0.1 - we deliberately keep only a
fraction of each tree's advice.

3.2 Worked example: three houses
--------------------------------
Three houses, true prices 300k / 400k / 500k. Use nu = 0.1. Start with the mean.

  Round 0:  F_0 = 400 for everyone (the best single number).
            errors (residuals):   A: -100     B: 0     C: +100

  Round 1:  Fit a small tree to those residuals. Say it predicts -100 / 0 / +100
            (it sees the pattern perfectly).
            We do NOT add all of it. We add nu * tree = -10 / 0 / +10.
            F_1 =  390 / 400 / 410
            new residuals:        A: -90      B: 0     C: +90

  Round 2:  Fit a tree to the NEW residuals -> -90 / 0 / +90.
            Add nu * tree = -9 / 0 / +9.
            F_2 =  381 / 400 / 419
            new residuals:        A: -81      B: 0     C: +81

Each round closes 10% of the remaining gap. Tracking house A:

    round      0      1      2      5     10     22     50
    F(A)   400.0  390.0  381.0  359.0  334.9  309.8  300.5
    gap    100.0   90.0   81.0   59.0   34.9    9.8    0.5

It takes about 22 rounds to close 90% of the gap, and 50 to get essentially
there. That slowness is not a bug. It IS the regularization - see section 5.

3.3 The two words that matter
-----------------------------
The general recipe (FORWARD STAGEWISE ADDITIVE MODELLING):

    F_0(x)   = the constant that minimises the loss over all rows
    for m = 1..M:
        (beta_m, h_m) = argmin over (beta, h) of
                            SUM_i  L( y_i ,  F_(m-1)(x_i) + beta * h(x_i) )
        F_m(x)        = F_(m-1)(x) + nu * beta_m * h_m(x)

GREEDY - at each round you solve one small easy problem: fit ONE tree against
the current errors. You never try to choose all 500 trees jointly, which would
be computationally hopeless.

STAGEWISE - once a tree is in, it is FROZEN. In the example above, tree 1
contributed -10 to house A. Round 40 cannot go back and say "tree 1, you were
too aggressive, shrink yourself". The only thing later trees can do is add a
correction on top of it.

Two alternatives exist, and naming them is useful:
  - Fully corrective boosting / backfitting: re-optimise ALL existing trees'
    weights every round. Fits the training set faster, overfits faster, costs
    much more.
  - Stepwise regression: re-estimates every coefficient when a new variable
    enters. Same idea, and NOT what boosting does.

Freezing is not a compromise to apologise for - it is a regularizer. Small
steps that are never undone trace a slow, cautious path through model space.
(Hastie et al. show this path is very close to the lasso / L1 solution path.)


================================================================================
4) WHY THE BASE LEARNER IS A SHALLOW TREE
================================================================================

Boosting wants a base learner that is high-bias, low-variance and cheap. A tree
of depth 1-6 is all three. Each reason has a concrete failure behind it.

4.1 High bias is the POINT, not a compromise
--------------------------------------------
Suppose you use a fully grown tree instead - deep enough to give nearly every
training row its own leaf. Round 1:

    residuals going in:   -100, 0, +100, -45, +62, ...
    the deep tree fits:   -100, 0, +100, -45, +62, ...   (exactly)
    residuals coming out:    0, 0,    0,   0,   0, ...

There is nothing left for round 2. You have not built an ensemble of 500 trees,
you have built one gigantic overfitted tree with 499 useless trees attached.
Boosting only works if each step is DELIBERATELY INADEQUATE and leaves work for
the next one.

4.2 Low variance matters because nothing averages the noise away
----------------------------------------------------------------
In bagging the trees are AVERAGED, so a wild tree gets diluted by 499 others. In
boosting the trees are SUMMED. A tree that chased a noisy outlier contributes
that mistake to the final prediction forever. There is no cleanup step.

4.3 Cheap matters because you need hundreds of them
---------------------------------------------------
M is 500 - 5,000. Half a second per tree is 40 minutes of training. Cost per
tree multiplies straight through.

4.4 Depth = how many features are allowed to interact (the overlooked one)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the most useful way to think about max_depth.

    A STUMP (depth 1) can only say:
        "4+ bedrooms          ->  +50k"

    It can NEVER say:
        "4+ bedrooms AND good school district  ->  +120k"

    because that needs two questions stacked - depth 2.

So:
    depth 1  ->  no interactions at all. The ensemble becomes
                 f(x) = f_1(bedrooms) + f_2(size) + f_3(district) + ...
                 a GENERALISED ADDITIVE MODEL - you can literally plot the
                 learned effect of each feature on its own.
    depth 2  ->  pairwise interactions
    depth d  ->  up to d-way interactions

Typical settings: max_depth 3-6 for XGBoost-style GBDT (or num_leaves around 31
for LightGBM's leaf-wise growth). Pick depth 1 when you want an interpretable
additive model; go deeper only when you believe real multi-way interactions
exist.

4.5 Why trees and not something else
------------------------------------
No feature scaling needed, mixed numeric/categorical features are fine,
invariant to monotone transforms of a feature (log(size) splits the same way as
size), and missing values can be handled inside the split rule (xgboost.txt).


================================================================================
5) BOOSTING ATTACKS BIAS, BAGGING ATTACKS VARIANCE
================================================================================

5.1 Side by side
----------------
                       BAGGING / RANDOM FOREST      BOOSTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
How trees are built    independently, in parallel,  sequentially, each on the
                       on bootstrap samples         previous ensemble's errors
Attacks                variance                     bias
Base learner wanted    low bias, high variance      high bias, low variance
                       -> DEEP trees                -> SHALLOW trees
Combined by            averaging / voting           weighted sum
More trees             safe, curve just flattens    eventually OVERFITS
Tuning                 forgiving                    sensitive (nu, M, depth)
Noisy labels           robust (noise averages out)  fragile (noise gets chased)
Parallel training      yes, trivially               no (section 6)

5.2 The consequence that gets asked about: where overfitting is controlled
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
In bagging, overfitting is handled by the averaging itself. B is not really a
tuning parameter - set it as high as your compute allows:

    Random Forest, house price, validation RMSE (thousands):
        50 trees  -> 44.0
       200 trees  -> 42.0
     1,000 trees  -> 41.3
     5,000 trees  -> 41.2        flat. More trees never made it worse.

In boosting, M IS a regularization parameter. Training loss goes down EVERY
round by construction, so training loss tells you nothing at all. Validation
error traces a U:

    GBDT, same data, RMSE (thousands):
        rounds    train     validation
            50       62         66
           200       41         48
           600       28         41
         1,200       19         39     <- best, stop here
         2,000       12         40
         5,000        4         46     <- clearly overfitting now

Training RMSE 4 and validation RMSE 46 is the model memorising the training
rows. If you had only watched the training curve you would have concluded the
model was getting better the whole time.

5.3 What this means in practice
-------------------------------
  - nu and M trade off directly. Halving the learning rate roughly doubles the
    number of rounds you need. Small nu + large M + early stopping is the
    standard safe recipe.
  - The only honest way to choose M is EARLY STOPPING on a validation set:
    keep training while validation improves, stop after it has failed to
    improve for N rounds, keep the best iteration. Never hard-code M.
  - The flat bottom of that U can be very long with a small nu, which is where
    the myth "boosting does not overfit" comes from. It is a myth. See
    failure_modes.txt.


================================================================================
6) SEQUENTIAL DEPENDENCE: WHY YOU CANNOT PARALLELISE ACROSS TREES
================================================================================

6.1 The reason
--------------
Go back to the practice-test story. You cannot study the mistakes from test 2
before you have sat test 2. And you cannot sit test 2 meaningfully before
studying the mistakes from test 1.

Same thing formally - tree m is fitted to targets DEFINED BY trees 1..m-1:

    AdaBoost          -> the row weights depend on what F_(m-1) got wrong
    Gradient boosting -> the targets are -dL/dF evaluated at F_(m-1)

Tree 300's training targets do not exist until tree 299 is finished. This is a
genuine data dependency, not an implementation detail, so no amount of hardware
removes it.

Bagging has the opposite structure: all B bootstrap samples exist up front, so
B trees can be trained on B machines at once.

    Random Forest, 1,000 trees at 0.4s each, 8 cores:
        1,000 * 0.4 / 8   ~=  50s          (scales with cores)

    GBDT, 1,000 rounds at 0.4s each, 8 cores:
        the 1,000 rounds stay strictly one after another.
        What the 8 cores do is make each ROUND faster - say 0.4s -> 0.08s:
        1,000 * 0.08      ~=  80s          (and that floor cannot be removed)

6.2 What CAN still be parallelised (standard follow-up question)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Plenty, and this is why modern libraries are fast despite being sequential:

  - Split finding WITHIN one tree: all features can be scanned at once, and all
    candidate split points within a feature. This is where XGBoost's column
    blocks and LightGBM's histograms get their speed.
  - Gradient and hessian computation: one independent number per row, so it
    parallelises across rows and across machines.
  - Histogram building per feature, then a reduce across workers.
  - Sibling nodes at the same depth of one tree are independent of each other.
  - PREDICTION: F(x) = SUM_m h_m(x). At inference the trees are independent and
    can be summed in any order, on any number of cores.
  - Everything around the fit: CV folds, hyperparameter search, and in
    multiclass the K trees of a single round (one per class).

So "boosting is sequential" is true at the level of ROUNDS only. In practice a
modern GBDT library will happily saturate your machine; what the serial chain
imposes is a wall-clock floor that extra cores cannot push through.


================================================================================
THE ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
================================================================================
Averaging many similar models drives variance down to a floor of rho*sigma^2 and
leaves bias exactly where it was - 1,000 straight lines still cannot fit a
curve. Boosting goes after the bias instead: fit a deliberately weak, cheap
model (a shallow tree), add a small fraction of it to the running sum, freeze
it, then fit the next one to whatever the sum is still getting wrong. Hundreds
of tiny corrections compose into a low-bias model - and because the errors being
corrected are a product, not a sum, learners barely better than coin flips
compound into near-perfect training fit. The bill comes in three parts: the
rounds are strictly sequential, noise gets chased instead of averaged away, and
the number of rounds becomes a regularization parameter you must early-stop on.
